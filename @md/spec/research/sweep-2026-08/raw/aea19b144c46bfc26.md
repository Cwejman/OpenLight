## 1. Zanzibar (USENIX ATC '19) — the paper's own words on transitive cost

Ruoming Pang et al., *Zanzibar: Google's Consistent, Global Authorization System*, USENIX ATC '19, Renton WA, July 2019 — [usenix.org/conference/atc19/presentation/pang](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc19/presentation/pang), PDF at [atc19-pang.pdf](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/atc19-pang.pdf), abstract at [research.google](https://research.google/pubs/zanzibar-googles-consistent-global-authorization-system/). Grade: **paper-only for the system, shipped-and-adopted inside Google**.

The transitive-cost problem is stated by the authors, not by commentators. §1: *"Evaluating whether a user belongs to a group can entail following a long chain of nested group memberships."* §3.2.4: *"Recursive pointer chasing during check evaluation has difficulty maintaining low latency with groups that are deeply nested or have a large number of child groups."* That sentence is the load-bearing one — depth **and** breadth both break the walk.

Leopard exists precisely for this. It denormalises the transitive closure into two tuple types: `GROUP2GROUP(s)→e` (ancestor → any direct-or-indirect subgroup) and `MEMBER2GROUP(s)→e` (user → direct parent group). *"Index tuples are stored as ordered lists of integers in a structure such as a skip list, thus allowing for efficient union and intersections among sets"*; intersection costs *"O(min(|A|,|B|)) skip-list seeks"* (§3.2.4). The price of denormalisation is write amplification: *"a single Zanzibar tuple addition or deletion may yield potentially tens of thousands of discrete Leopard tuple events."* Leopard is applied only to *"selected namespaces"* — Google did not materialise everything.

Numbers (§4, §4.2, §4.4): >2 trillion relation tuples; >10M client queries/sec; Dec 2018 7-day sample — Check peaks ~4.2M QPS, Read 8.2M, Expand 760K, Write 25K; >1,500 namespaces from hundreds of applications. Check-Safe latency *"at the 50th, 95th, 99th, and 99.9th percentiles it peaks at roughly 3, 11, 20, and 93 msec"* — note the 30× spread between median and 99.9th, which is the tail the deep walks live in. Leopard servers respond *"in fewer than 150 μsec at the median, or under 1 msec at the 99th percentile."* Hot spots (§3.2.5): *"We found the handling of hot spots to be the most critical frontier in our pursuit of low latency and high availability."* **The paper states no explicit Check depth limit** — the clone ecosystem invented those.

## 3. Depth limits are real, arbitrary, and being abandoned

- **SpiceDB**: default max depth **50 hops**, `--dispatch-max-depth`. Error: *"the check request has exceeded the allowable maximum depth of 50: this usually indicates a recursive or too deep data dependency."* Critically: *"SpiceDB does not have a dedicated cycle detector"* — cycles are detected by hitting the depth wall — [authzed.com/docs/spicedb/modeling/recursion-and-max-depth](https://authzed.com/docs/spicedb/modeling/recursion-and-max-depth).
- **OpenFGA**: `OPENFGA_RESOLVE_NODE_LIMIT` default **25**, plus a breadth limit; ListObjects/ListUsers capped at **1,000 results** by default — [openfga.dev running-in-production](https://openfga.dev/docs/best-practices/running-in-production).
- The depth limit is now being **removed as a bad proxy**. "OpenFGA's Move to Weighted Graph Resolution", Jul 21 2026: *"A shallow recursive graph can be more resource-intensive than a deep linear one, but the old algorithm used a fixed depth limit of 25 as its only complexity guard."* Replaced by *"datastore throttling, graph flattening, and context cancellation"* — [openfga.dev/blog/weighted-graph-upcoming-changes](https://openfga.dev/blog/weighted-graph-upcoming-changes). Depth ≠ cost; **fan-out is the real cost driver**. Name the folklore: "keep your hierarchy under N levels" is widely repeated and is the wrong metric.

**Hardest numbers available** — AuthZed "Query Planner Early Benchmarks", May 20 2026 ([link](https://authzed.com/blog/query-planner-early-benchmarks)). *DeepArrow* = a 30-level recursive folder chain: 772 µs → 70 µs in-memory (11×), 715 KB → 120 KB; against Postgres 19.55 ms → 10.41 ms. *WideArrow* (doc shared with 100 groups, 1,000 users): 43 KB → 4.2 KB. Vendor-authored, but a synthetic depth-30 chain costing ~20 ms per check on Postgres is the concrete price of transitivity. Corroborating diagnosis, Feb 2 2026: customers model with *"recursion, very deep nesting, and with relationships that connect thousands and thousands of objects"*; arrow operations over thousands of groups mean *"we would have done a lot of work for nothing"* when the subject is in none.

## 4. Materialise vs walk — the industry has converged on "walk fails at list-time"

AuthZed "Why Large Organizations Need Materialize", Jun 25 2026 ([link](https://authzed.com/blog/why-large-organizations-need-materialize)): *"a single relationship write can therefore affect permissions across large portions of the graph, particularly when nested groups are involved."* Named use cases: search pre/post-filtering, authorization-aware pagination, **RAG vector-DB filtering**. This is Leopard reinvented commercially, with the same write-amplification tradeoff. Grade: **shipped, vendor-marketing framing**.

## 5. Inheritance models and the AI oversharing problem — best-evidenced section

- **Google Drive** now *forbids* narrowing below inheritance: *"Access you apply to a folder is inherited by all files within it"* and *"You can no longer give someone less access to an individual file if they have higher access to its parent folder"* — [support.google.com/drive/answer/2494886](https://support.google.com/drive/answer/2494886). Google chose simplicity over expressiveness.
- **Notion**: subpages inherit parent permissions; *"Notion respects the broadest level of access given to a user"* — [notion.com/help/sharing-and-permissions](https://www.notion.com/help/sharing-and-permissions). Union semantics means adding one broad grant silently widens a whole subtree.
- **Microsoft, on the record.** *Restricted SharePoint Search*: *"It's a short-term solution that gives your organization's administrators time to review and audit site and file permissions. It's not intended or scalable for long-term use"*, capped at **100 sites**, and *"isn't a security boundary and doesn't change any permissions."* Retiring — new enablement blocked from **July 31 2026** (ms.date 2026-07-06, [link](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/sharepoint/restricted-sharepoint-search)). Its worked example is explicit: a budgeting site where *"the site owner hasn't set up proper permissions"*, so *"when Alex asks Copilot for some budgeting information, Copilot gets information from the budgeting site."* That is Microsoft documenting the oversharing failure mode in its own docs.
- Successor **Restricted Content Discovery** (ms.date 2026-07-27, [link](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/sharepoint/restricted-content-discovery)): *"doesn't change existing permissions"*, Copilot-licence gated, and for sites >500,000 items *"an update to Restricted Content Discovery could take more than a week to fully process."* Plus a whole "Remediate oversharing" pillar in the Copilot deployment blueprint (ms.date 2026-05-06). Grade: **shipped-and-adopted vendor admission** — unusually strong evidence because it is the vendor conceding the problem, not selling against it.

The structural point: latent transitive permissions were harmless while discovery was manual. An LLM retrieval layer performs the closure exhaustively, so **the AI didn't create the leak — it enumerated it**.

## 6. RAG/ACL filtering

Post-filtering wrecks recall by construction: filtering after top-k *"can return very few or even zero results, even though there may be relevant records in the dataset"*; pre-filtering *"disrupts how ANN engines work (ANN requires the full index)"* — Briggs, Pinecone, 2023-06-30 ([link](https://www.pinecone.io/learn/vector-search-filtering/)). The academic answer is ACORN (Patel, Kraft, Guestrin, Zaharia, arXiv 2403.04871, 2024-03-07): predicate-agnostic HNSW traversal, *"2–1,000x higher throughput at a fixed recall."* **Paper-only → partially shipped** (ideas adopted in vector DBs). Combined with §5: a permission filter is a high-cardinality, per-user, churning predicate — the worst case for filtered ANN — which is why vendors precompute permission sets instead.
