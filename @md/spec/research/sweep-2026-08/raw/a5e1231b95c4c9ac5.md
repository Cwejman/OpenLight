## 1. MINIMALISM — what was actually measured

**The primary experiments are real, small, and reported in full.** Carroll, Smith-Kerker, Ford & Mazur-Rimetz, "The Minimal Manual," *Human-Computer Interaction* 3(2):123–153, 1987 (PDF: https://davis68.github.io/2016-12-12-ttt-uiuc/files/papers/carroll-minimal-manual-1987.pdf). **Experiment 1: N=19** (10 minimal manual, 9 self-study), temp-agency office workers on an IBM Displaywriter. Results verbatim: "the MM subjects required 40% less learning time than the SS subjects, t(17) = 3.06, p < .01"; "the MM subjects accomplished 2.7 times as many performance subtasks, t(16) = 3.63, p < .01"; MM learners "more than twice as efficient, t(16) = 2.90, p < .01." **Experiment 2: N=32**, 2×2 between-subjects, 8 per cell: MM learners completed "58% more subtasks, F(1,28) = 5.31, p < .05," achieved "93% more per unit of time, F(1,28) = 6.13, p < .05," spent "29% less" time reading, and made "20% fewer errors, F(1,28) = 3.11, **n.s.**" Note that subjects spent "145 min — almost half their time — recovering from errors." Training wheels: Carroll & Carrithers, "Blocking Learner Error States in a Training-Wheels System," *Human Factors* 26(4):377–389, 1984 (https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/001872088402600402) — two experiments, abstract claims only "improved learning efficiency," no numbers in the abstract. **Grade: paper-only, but genuinely experimental, randomized, and with real effect sizes — unusually good for 1987.**

**It replicated, and there is a meta-analysis — but the meta-analysis was never peer-reviewed.** Ginns, Hollender & Reimann, "Meta-Analysis of the Minimalist Training Model," ERIC ED491708 (https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED491708.pdf), **AERA annual meeting, San Francisco, 6 April 2006, filed as "Online Submission"** — i.e. a self-deposited conference paper, not a journal article (https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED491708). **13 effects, total N=288**, from 5 journal articles, 3 conference papers, 1 tech report, 1 PhD thesis. Weighted mean **d = 1.12 (95% CI 0.83–1.41)**, homogeneous (Q=14.56, df=12, p=.266). Sub-analyses: "support error recognition and recovery" **d = 0.59** (3 studies, n=118); "slash the verbiage" **d = 0.89** (3 studies, n=120). The authors' own inference is the interesting one: because 0.89 approaches 1.12, "the reduction of redundancy might be the key driver of learning gains" — i.e. most of minimalism's measured benefit may be plain cognitive-load redundancy reduction, not the task-orientation theory. Caveats they state: many studies had "less than 10 per group"; training-wheels studies were **excluded** because they don't make the minimalist-vs-system-centred comparison; Carroll and van der Meij themselves helped select the included studies (acknowledged in the paper — a conflict worth naming). Effects are heavily right-skewed by two outliers (Gong & Elkerton 1990, d=2.34; Lohmann 1994, d=2.26). **No funnel plot, no publication-bias test.** Lazonder & van der Meij, "The Minimal Manual: Is Less Really More?," *IJMMS* 39:729–752, 1993 is the direct replication (d=1.01).

**The methodological critique is about guided exploration, not brevity.** Williams & Farkas, "Minimalism Reconsidered: Should We Design Documentation for Exploratory Learning?", *SIGCHI Bulletin*, April 1992 (https://faculty.washington.edu/farkas/dfpubs/Williams-Farkas-MinimalismReconsidered.pdf): of minimalism's four tenets, three "are largely non-controversial"; guided exploration "is the most questionable precept," inherited from Bruner-style discovery learning, and "the stated minimalist goal of enabling the learner to accomplish real work while learning a program is often thwarted by the act of compelling that learner to induce, through trial and error, the correct procedures." Carroll & van der Meij replied in "Ten Misconceptions about Minimalism," *IEEE Trans. Prof. Comm.* 39(2):72, June 1996 (https://ris.utwente.nl/ws/files/249663536/Caroll1996ten.pdf) — #1 on their list is "Minimalism means brevity," and they write: "Wantonly slashing text and leaving other design characteristics unchanged will not lead to a minimalist design… if it really did produce better instruction, it would be the mother of all panaceas." **This is the single most-repeated misreading of the research.**

**Current status in the 2020s: dormant in the literature.** Virtaluoto, Suojanen & Isohella, "Minimalism Heuristics Revisited," *Technical Communication* 68(1), 2021 (open AAM: https://osuva.uwasa.fi/bitstreams/e96bd3f9-c878-49ae-9cc0-e5774f840bdf/download) searched **six journals** — *IEEE TPC*, *Information Design Journal*, *JBTC*, *JTWC*, *Technical Communication*, *TCQ* — for 2014–2019 on "minimalism," "minimalist documentation," "minimal manual," and "came out empty-handed. Articles dealing with minimalism in these journals dated back to the 1990s–early 2000s." Stated obstacles: no textbook, no disseminated best practices, "very little information available on the cost benefits," and van der Meij's (2017) finding that **minimalist manual production takes 30% longer** than conventional. They also note the failure mode: "it is all too tempting to focus only on the often-cited principle of 'slashing the verbiage.'" **Grade: shipped-niche — alive in ISO 82079-1 (2019) and CIDM training, absent from research.**

## 2. DOCUMENTATION QUALITY MEASUREMENT

**The best empirical work is descriptive taxonomy, not effectiveness measurement.** Uddin & Robillard, "How API Documentation Fails," *IEEE Software* 32(4):68–75, 2015, DOI 10.1109/MS.2014.80 (PDF: https://www.cs.mcgill.ca/~martin/papers/ieeesw2015.pdf): **two surveys, 323 IBM professionals total.** Exploratory survey: 69/697 responded (9.9%), yielding 179 examples across 131 documentation units → 10 problem types (6 content: incompleteness, ambiguity, unexplained examples, obsoleteness, inconsistency, incorrectness; 4 presentation: bloat, fragmentation, excessive structural information, tangled information). Validation survey: **254/1,064 (23.8%)**, Canada + GB. Six were rated "Blocker" at least once. Ambiguity: 26.5% frequency ratio, top priority 51.9% of the time. Content beat presentation on every measure. Notably: "no statistically significant evidence that novices experienced documentation problems differently." Robillard, "What Makes APIs Hard to Learn?", *IEEE Software* 26(6):27–34, Nov/Dec 2009 (https://www.cs.mcgill.ca/~martin/papers/software2009a.pdf): **83 Microsoft developers, 8% response rate**, 80 usable, 74 named an obstacle; **50 of 74 cited resources/documentation** — the top obstacle category, above API structure. Aghajani et al., "Software Documentation Issues Unveiled," ICSE 2019, DOI 10.1109/ICSE.2019.00122: **878 mined artifacts** from mailing lists, Stack Overflow, issues, PRs → taxonomy; its methodological virtue is avoiding survey self-report. Aghajani et al., "Software Documentation: The Practitioners' Perspective," ICSE 2020, DOI 10.1145/3377811.3380405: **two surveys, 146 practitioners.** All four: **paper-only, descriptive, no outcome measure.**

**There is no validated instrument, and essentially no controlled experiment.** The closest thing to a standard is Treude, Middleton & Atapattu, "Beyond Accuracy: Assessing Software Documentation Quality," ESEC/FSE 2020 **Visions and Reflections track** (https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.10744, v1 21 Jul 2020): a 10-dimension framework of structure/content/style, demonstrated by recruiting technical editors — the abstract does not state N, and it is explicitly a vision paper. **Grade: paper-only, never validated at scale.** My arXiv sweeps for controlled experiments linking documentation quality to developer task outcomes (cs.SE, "controlled experiment" + documentation) returned crypto-API code-example studies and ADR-template comparisons (arXiv:2604.27333, 30 Apr 2026) — **nothing measuring docs quality → developer success.** That absence is itself the finding: the field measures what's wrong with docs, never what good docs buy you.

**DORA is the only large-N link to outcomes, and it does not hold up.** 2021 report (https://dora.dev/research/2021/dora-report/2021-dora-accelerate-state-of-devops-report.pdf, Sept 2021), N=1,200, seven Likert perception items (https://dora.dev/research/2021/questions/): "teams with higher quality documentation are **2.4 times more likely** to see better SDO performance," plus 3.8x security, 2.4x reliability, 3.5x SRE, 2.5x cloud — **no CIs, no p-values, no effect sizes published.** Method: cross-sectional, **PLS-SEM, which is correlation-based**, on a **snowball-plus-purchased-panel** sample. Then it reverses: **2022 report, "Surprises" (p.57): "Documentation practices negatively impacted software delivery performance. This is at odds with previous reports."** **2023 report (N≈3,000): software delivery performance = "No effect," footnoted "We continue to be surprised to find no effect of quality documentation on software delivery performance. This is the second year we see this behavior."** The 2023 amplifier table (p.28) — CI 2.4x, CD 2.7x, **trunk-based development 12.8x** — is a ratio of two unreported coefficients with no interval. 2024: "A 25% increase in AI adoption is associated with a 7.5% increase in documentation quality," with the methodology chapter (p.110) conceding "we didn't do longitudinal studies or a proper experiment… Correlation does not imply causation." **DORA 2025** (N=4,867 + 78 interviews, fielded 13 Jun–21 Jul 2025, https://dora.dev/research/2025/dora-report/) **drops "documentation quality" as a named capability**, replacing it with "healthy data ecosystems" and "AI-accessible internal data" — and **Figures 35–37 publish no numbers, only ordinal bands.** Endnote 20 retreats explicitly: "Last year, we spoke in terms of 'effects'. This year… we will speak in terms of comparisons… we don't want to give false assurances that we understand the underlying causal structure." **Any circulating "Nx" figure for 2025 internal-data quality is FOLKLORE.** The widely-cited 2022 percentage-lift table (CD 63%→656%, TBD 36%→1525%) appears **only** on dora.dev/capabilities/documentation-quality/ and a Google Cloud blog post dated 16 June 2023 — **not in the 2022 PDF: secondary-source-only.** Independent critique: Sallin et al., "Measuring Software Delivery Performance Using the Four Key Metrics of DevOps," XP 2021, LNBIP 419, DOI 10.1007/978-3-030-78098-2_7 — of 93 papers citing Forsgren et al., "none of them contained more information about the FKM than already presented by Forsgren et al." No peer-reviewed replication of the documentation construct exists.

**One solid, traceable statistic:** GitHub Open Source Survey 2017 (https://opensourcesurvey.org/2017/) — **93% of respondents observed incomplete or outdated documentation as a problem; 60% of contributors rarely or never contribute to docs.** N=5,500 **randomly sampled** from traffic to licensed repos across 3,800+ repositories (plus ~500 non-random off-platform). This is a genuine random sample, unusual in this literature — but it measures perceived prevalence, not cost.

## 3. DOCS FOR LLMs/AGENTS (2025–2026)

**The causal evidence splits cleanly, and it cuts against the prevailing practice.** Gloaguen, Mündler, Müller, Raychev & Vechev (ETH Zürich/LogicStar), "Evaluating AGENTS.md: Are Repository-Level Context Files Helpful for Coding Agents?", arXiv:2602.11988 (12 Feb 2026, rev. 23 Jun 2026): across SWE-bench tasks and fresh issues from repos with developer-committed context files, context files "do not generally improve task success rates, while increasing inference cost by over 20% on average." Agents *followed* the instructions; repository overviews — the most-recommended content — simply didn't help. **Paper-only, but the strongest experimental result in the space, and it contradicts vendor guidance.** Weaker corroboration: Khatri, arXiv:2607.27250 (28 Jul 2026), 17 tasks/288 runs, bounds any effect at ≤10–15pp — underpowered, so "no difference" here is not "no effect."

**Machine-facing descriptions are where the measurable gains are.** Hasan et al. (Queen's/Huawei), "MCP Tool Descriptions Are Smelly!", arXiv:2602.14878 (16 Feb 2026): 856 tools/103 servers, 97.1% carry ≥1 smell, 56% don't state purpose; augmenting descriptions gives **+5.85pp median task success and +15.12% partial goal completion — at +67.46% execution steps, with regressions in 16.67% of cases.** Wang et al., arXiv:2602.18914 (21 Feb 2026): 10,831 servers, then a mutation-based controlled experiment — standard-compliant descriptions reach **72% selection probability vs a 20% baseline**. The "**260% more often**" figure circulating (e.g. https://glama.ai/blog/2026-04-03-tool-description-quality-score-tdqs, 3 Apr 2026) is that 20%→72% restated as relative lift; real number, misleading framing, and it measures *tool selection among competitors*, not task success. **Near-FOLKLORE framing of a real result.**

**Conventions:** llms.txt is **shipped-niche and its SEO claims are dead** — SE Ranking, 300k domains, 7 Nov 2025 (https://seranking.com/blog/llms-txt/): 10.13% adoption, **no correlation with AI citation; dropping the variable *improved* their model.** Google's Gary Illyes (July 2025) says no support and no plans; John Mueller compared it to the keywords meta tag (https://www.searchenginejournal.com/google-says-llms-txt-comparable-to-keywords-meta-tag/544804/). **No LLM provider has confirmed consuming it.** AGENTS.md: **shipped-and-widely-adopted** ("over 60k open-source projects," now under the Agentic AI Foundation) **but with effectiveness disproven** by 2602.11988 — the widest adoption and the clearest null result in the same object. Agent Skills/SKILL.md: shipped Dec 2025 as an open spec, adopted by Codex CLI, Cursor, Copilot. Vendor traffic data is abundant and one-sided: Mintlify, 29 Jul 2026 (https://www.mintlify.com/blog/state-of-docs-traffic) reports agents at **66% of docs traffic** (213M agent requests vs 105M human page loads), up from 15.2% in January — Cloudflare user-agent classification, and they concede Codex sends no identifiable UA, so it's a floor. Their claim that serving llms.txt "reduced error rate by almost 90%" across 20 sites has **undisclosed methodology and measures navigation errors, not task success — grade: marketing.**

## The through-line

Three separate literatures, one shared shape. Minimalism has the **only** credible causal evidence that document design changes user outcomes (d≈1.12, but N=288 total, from 1985–1994, on word processors, meta-analysed by a never-peer-reviewed conference paper co-curated by the theory's own authors) — and its own meta-analysts suspect the active ingredient is redundancy reduction, not the theory. Modern software-documentation research has **larger samples and no outcome variable at all**: it catalogues defects. DORA had the outcome variable, published a headline multiplier, then **found the opposite the next year, then no effect the year after, then removed the construct**. And the agent-era work has finally produced clean experiments — which say **schemas matter, prose overviews don't**. Anyone citing "2.4x" or "40% faster" as a live fact about documentation in 2026 is quoting a 2021 finding DORA has since retracted, or a 1987 experiment with nineteen typists.
