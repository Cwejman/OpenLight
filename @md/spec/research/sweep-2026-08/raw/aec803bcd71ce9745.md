## (a) FOLKLORE VERDICTS

**(a) "10x more time reading than writing" — Robert C. Martin, *Clean Code* (2008), Ch. 1. Grade: FOLKLORE.**
Verified verbatim against the full book text (Internet Archive OCR, item `clean-code-a-handbook-of-agile-software-craftsmanship`). The passage reads: *"You get the drift. Indeed, the ratio of time spent reading vs. writing is well over 10:1. We are constantly reading old code as part of the effort to write new code."* Critically, the preceding paragraphs establish its provenance explicitly and honestly: *"Have you ever played back an edit session? In the 80s and 90s we had editors like Emacs that kept track of every keystroke… When I did this, the results were fascinating. The vast majority of the playback was scrolling and navigating to other modules!"* — followed by a narrative vignette of "Bob" scrolling. It is Martin's recollection of watching Emacs session playbacks, with no instrumentation, no N, no measurement. Chapter 1's bibliography contains exactly two entries — Beck's *Implementation Patterns* (2007) and Knuth's *Literate Programming* (1992) — neither of which concerns the ratio. There is no citation, and I found no earlier numerical source for "10:1"; the ratio appears to originate with Martin. Note the passage is *ambiguous about what it measures*: the playback observation is about scrolling/navigation, not reading comprehension.

**(b) "Maintenance is 40–80% of cost" / "programmers spend 50% of time on comprehension". Grade: SPLIT — maintenance-share PARTIALLY-TRACEABLE; the 50%-comprehension half is FOLKLORE.**
The two claims have different pedigrees. The *maintenance-share* claim traces to real survey work: Lientz, Swanson & Tompkins, "Characteristics of Application Software Maintenance", CACM 21(6):466–471, 1978 (DOI 10.1145/359511.359522) — questionnaire to 120 organisations, **69 respondents**, not 487. The 487-organisation figure belongs to the later work: Lientz & Swanson, *Software Maintenance Management* (1980) and "Problems in Application Software Maintenance", CACM 24(11):763–769, 1981 (DOI 10.1145/358790.358796), whose abstract confirms **487 data processing organizations**. Note the 1978 abstract itself already launders folklore: *"Rough estimates of the total systems and programming resources consumed range as high as 75–80 percent in each category"* — presented as background rumour, not as their finding. The oft-cited 21/25/50/4% corrective/adaptive/perfective split lives in the 1980 book, which is lending-restricted on IA and which I **could not verify from primary text**.

The *comprehension* half is the folklore part. **Fjeldstad & Hamlen, "Application Program Maintenance Study: Report to Our Respondents"** is effectively unretrievable: it has no DOI, does not appear in Crossref, OpenAlex, or DBLP, and the containing volume (*Tutorial on Software Maintenance*, Parikh & Zvegintzov eds., IEEE CS Press, 1983 — OpenLibrary `/works/OL22684307W`) has no Internet Archive scan. **It is cited-but-unread, and the citations themselves prove it**: Minelli et al. cite it as *"in Tutorial on Software Maintenance, G. Parikh and N. Zvegintzov, Eds. IEEE, 1982, pp. 13–30"*; Xia et al. cite the same item as *"in Proc. Guide, 1983, Art. no. 48"*. Same claim, incompatible bibliographic records — the signature of a reference copied between papers rather than consulted. Xia et al. are admirably candid about the whole chain, writing that Zelkowitz et al., Fjeldstad & Hamlen, and Corbi *"all report that program comprehension activities take more than half of the time spent on software maintenance **based on anecdotal evidences**."* Zelkowitz, Shaw & Gannon (*Principles of Software Engineering and Design*, Prentice Hall, 1979) is a textbook, not a study, and is not on IA. Corbi, "Program Understanding: Challenge for the 1990s", IBM Systems Journal 28(2):294–306, 1989 (DOI 10.1147/sj.282.0294) is a real indexed article but paywalled; **I could not obtain its text** and cannot say what it cites.

**(c) "~5% of time writing new code" — Peter Hallam. Grade: FOLKLORE (but honestly labelled).**
Retrieved in full from the Wayback Machine: *"What Do Programmers Really Do Anyway? (aka Part 2 of the Yardstick saga)"*, blogs.msdn.com/peterhal, 4 January 2006, reproducing a 2002 internal Microsoft email. Hallam writes: *"Here is the result of my observation way back in August of 2002."* His figures for himself ("Einstein" persona, C# compiler dev): **New Code 2%, Modifying Existing Code 20%, Understanding Code 78%.** For his junior "Elvis" persona: **New Code 5%, Modifying 25%, Understanding 70%.** He then writes *"No, I am not making this up"* and follows with several paragraphs of pure argument — no instrumentation, no survey, no participants. The widely-quoted "5% writing new code" is the *hypothetical persona* number, not even his own. Zero measurement.

**(d) Other propagated figures.** First, a genuine **mis-citation inside the peer-reviewed literature**: both Minelli et al. and Xia et al. cite Ko et al. (TSE 2006) as showing "understanding a program occupies around 35 percent of the total time". The primary text says something else entirely: *"developers to spend, on average, 35 percent of their time performing **the mechanics of navigation** within and between source files"* — elaborated as *"19 minutes (35 percent of the time not spent answering interruptions)"* spent recovering task contexts, iterating search results and returning from navigations. That is navigation overhead, not comprehension. Second, **Stripe/Harris Poll, "The Developer Coefficient" (2018)** — the source of "developers waste 17.3 hours/week on maintenance". Verified from the report PDF: methodology is *"More than 1,000 developers and more than 1,000 C-level executives in the United States, U.K., France, Germany, and Singapore"*; developers self-estimated 17.3 h/week on maintenance (13.5 technical debt + 3.8 bad code) against a 41.1-hour week, while executives estimated 20.9. Real survey, but self-reported estimates with no published sampling frame or intervals — PARTIALLY-TRACEABLE. Third, **Erlikh, "Leveraging Legacy System Dollars for E-Business", IT Professional 2(3):17–23, 2000** (DOI 10.1109/6294.846201) is the load-bearing citation behind "maintenance is 55–95% of total cost" (Minelli cites it as [29]); it is a trade-magazine piece and I could not obtain it — treat as untraced.

## (b) WHAT IS ACTUALLY MEASURED

**Minelli, Mocci & Lanza, ICPC 2015 (DOI 10.1109/ICPC.2015.12) — verified from the primary PDF.** **N = 18 developers**, recruited by a call on the Pharo-dev mailing list, described as *"both professionals and academics"*. **738 sessions, 197h 14m** of active development, 4.85M low-level + 206k meta events, captured by **DFlow**, their non-intrusive profiler for the **Pharo (Smalltalk) IDE**. The headline is real: **Understanding = 69.85% average** (editing 4.90%, navigation 3.56%, UI fiddling 13.81%, outside-IDE 7.88%). But *what "comprehension" means here is the crux*: "Basic Understanding" is defined as **all inter-spree time gaps longer than a 1-second "reaction time"** — i.e. idleness is imputed to comprehension. The authors flag this themselves: *"the fact that small periods of idleness… are mapped to program understanding is an explicit assumption that we made, but indeed they could be mini interruptions unrelated to development, like the programmer checking his phone."* Two further caveats: (i) the data is extremely concentrated — D1, D2, D3 account for 619/738 sessions and ~86% of recorded hours (Xia et al. state that only 7 of the 18 were professionals and that >85% of data comes from three PhD students); (ii) the reported 69.85% is an *unweighted per-developer mean*, so D14's single 1h52m session (94.08%) counts as much as D1's 89 hours. I recomputed the time-weighted figure from Tables III/IV: **68.5%** — so the headline survives weighting, even though the sample does not really support 18 independent observations.

**Xia, Bao, Lo, Xing, Hassan & Li, IEEE TSE 44(10):951–976, 2018 (DOI 10.1109/TSE.2017.2734091) — verified from the primary PDF. This is the strongest study in the area.** **N = 78 professional developers** (83 installed the tool; 78 analysed) across **7 real projects** at two Chinese IT companies (Insigma Global Service, >500 employees; Hengtian, >2,000), monitored for **two weeks / 10 working days each**, totalling **3,148 effective working hours**. Experience mix: 22% (18) >5 years, 42% (35) 3–5 years, 36% (30) <3 years. Method: their **ActivitySpace** framework collects HCI/interaction data **across all applications, not just the IDE**, with an inference model extended from Minelli's; validated against manual analysis of two developers (tool 58.26% vs. manual 58.31%, discrepancy <0.23%). Headline: **57.62% comprehension**, navigation 23.96%, other 13.40%, **editing 5.02%** — ranging 51.80% (project G) to 64.05% (project F). A key contribution is *where* comprehension happens: web browsers 27.26%, IDEs 19.95%, document editors 10.38% (differences significant, one-way ANOVA F=32.4, p<0.001) — i.e. a large share of "reading" is reading documentation and Stack Overflow, not source code. Experience effect is large and monotonic: **low/medium/high experience = 66.37% / 55.97% / 44.43%** comprehension time (one-way ANOVA F=79.4, p<2.2e-16; pairwise Cohen's d "large"). One flaw worth noting for citation hygiene: the RQ1 body text says *"across the 5 projects"* while the abstract, contribution list and result box all say 7 — a stale-draft artefact.

**Meyer, Barton, Fritz, Murphy & Zimmermann, IEEE TSE 2017 (DOI 10.1109/TSE.2017.2656886) — verified from the ZORA green-OA PDF.** **N = 20 professional developers at 4 companies**, monitoring application deployed for **an average of 11 full workdays in situ**. This one reframes everything, because it measures the *whole workday* rather than IDE time: **Coding (defined as "reading/editing/navigating code") = 21.0%**, Email 14.5%, Other-RDP 12.0%, work-related browsing 11.4%, Other 11.4%, read/write documents 6.6%, planned meetings 6.5%, planning 4.8%, work-unrelated browsing 5.9%, informal meetings 3.4%, code reviews 1.3%, version control 0.7%, debugger 0.4%. Developers averaged **8.4h (±1.2) spans with 4.3h (±0.5) active input** per day; **24.4% of the day is collaborative**. So Xia's 58% and Meyer's 21% are not in conflict — Xia's denominator is effective working time on development, Meyer's is the whole day including meetings and email.

**Other in-situ evidence.** Ko, Myers, Coblenz & Aung, TSE 32(12):971–987, 2006 — 31 Java developers recruited from the local (CMU) community, *"including both undergraduate and graduate students and staff programmers"*, with analysis focused on **the 10 most Java-experienced**; 70-minute lab session, two debugging + three enhancement tasks; the real finding is the 35% navigation-mechanics figure quoted above. Roehm, Tiarks, Koschke & Maalej, ICSE 2012 — **observational study of 28 professional developers from 7 companies**; qualitative, no time percentages, but finds developers *"try to avoid program comprehension"* and that *"face-to-face communication is preferred to documentation"*. Sadowski, Stolee & Elbaum, FSE 2015 (DOI 10.1145/2786805.2786855) — survey + log analysis at Google: developers conduct **an average of five search sessions with 12 total queries each workday**. **Astromskis, Janes & Mairegger, "Patterns of developers behaviour: A 1000-hour industrial study", JSS 2017 (DOI 10.1016/j.jss.2017.06.072)** — identified but **paywalled; I could not obtain it and report no numbers from it**.

**Documentation-reading time specifically.** Uddin & Robillard, "How API Documentation Fails", IEEE Software 32(4), 2015 (DOI 10.1109/MS.2014.80): I retrieved the author preprint. Two IBM surveys, **323 professionals total** — exploratory (69 respondents, 9.9% response rate, 47 developers/15 architects/3 consultants/3 managers/1 tester, experience 1–40 yrs, mean 13.1) and validation (**254 respondents, 23.8% of a 1,064-person frame in IBM Canada and Great Britain**; 203 developers, 51 architects; experience 1–45 yrs, mean 13.17, SD 9.9). Question 2 of the validation survey was exactly *"Approximately how many hours on average per week do you spend consulting API documentation?"* — **but the answer is not reported anywhere in the preprint**, whose analysis covers only frequency, severity and priority. I flag this as UNVERIFIED and defer to the sibling agent; if a figure is circulating, it may not come from this paper.

## (c) EYE-TRACKING: READING ORDER

**Reading is non-linear, and gets less linear with expertise.** Busjahn, Bednarik, Begel, Crosby, Paterson, Schulte, Sharif & Tamm, **"Eye Movements in Code Reading: Relaxing the Linear Order"**, ICPC 2015, pp. 255–265 (DOI **10.1109/ICPC.2015.36**) — note the task brief's title, "relating reading to writing", is not this paper's title. Verified from the GCU author manuscript. **Populations: 14 novices** attending a Java beginners' course at Freie Universität Berlin (all but two were university students, **none in computer science**, ages 19–33, 7 female), studied longitudinally across six course modules with attrition producing *"a sparse dataset in later modules"*; plus **6 professional software engineers** at Berlin companies (ages 26–49, one female, **5–28 years' experience**). Results: novices' eye movements followed linear "Story Order" **~80% on natural-language text vs ~70% on source code** (Module 1 code still 75%); **experts showed only ~60% linear eye movements on code**, characterised by more saccades skipping intermediate words and lines. Statistics are non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank (W = 21 and 0 on matched samples) because the sparse data precluded normality tests. Directionally solid; N=6 experts is very small.

**Population caveat across the field.** Sharafi, Soh & Guéhéneuc, "A Systematic Literature Review on the Usage of Eye-tracking in Software Engineering", IST 67:79–107, 2015 (DOI 10.1016/j.infsof.2015.06.008) — verified from the author PDF at ptidej.net. Covers **1990 to end of 2014**, automated search via Engineering Village, **36 relevant publications** (9 journal, 2 workshop, 25 conference). Tallying the participant lines in its study summaries: roughly **28 studies used students (or students + faculty) versus about 6 using professionals**, with typical N between 10 and 30. So the eye-tracking base rate is student subjects on short snippets. Siegmund et al., ICSE 2014 (DOI 10.1145/2568225.2568252), the fMRI work, is the same shape: **17 participants**, short snippets, contrasted against syntax-error location; it validates fMRI as a comprehension measure and identifies five activated regions (working memory, attention, language processing) — it says nothing about time allocation.
