## (a) What design rationale research established

The DR tradition is a chain of representations, each verified here against primary metadata or full text.

- **gIBIS** — Conklin & Begeman, *ACM TOIS* 6(4), 1988, "gIBIS: a hypertext tool for exploratory policy discussion," DOI 10.1145/58566.59297 (a CSCW'88 companion exists, 10.1145/62266.62278). ~1,090 citations (OpenAlex). Graphical IBIS: issues/positions/arguments as typed nodes and links.
- **QOC** — MacLean, Young, Bellotti & Moran, "Questions, Options, and Criteria: Elements of Design Space Analysis," *Human-Computer Interaction* 6(3–4), 1991, DOI 10.1207/s15327051hci0603&4_2 (375 Crossref citations). Metadata verified; **I did not read the full text**.
- **DRL** — Lee. **UNVERIFIED**: I could not obtain a primary source. Note a discrepancy worth flagging: Shipman & Marshall's own text renders it "Lee's design representation language (Lee, 1990)," whereas the term usually expanded is *Decision* Representation Language. Treat any expansion as unconfirmed.
- **Shipman & McCall**, "Integrating different perspectives on design rationale," *AI EDAM* 11(2), 1997, DOI 10.1017/s089006040000192x (58 citations). Metadata verified; **abstract/full text not retrieved** (Cambridge Core returned 500).
- **Burge & Brown**, "Software Engineering Using RATionale" (SEURAT), *JSS* 2008, DOI 10.1016/j.jss.2007.05.004; ICSE'08 tool demo 10.1145/1368088.1368215. Metadata only — **no empirical content verified**.
- **Tang, Babar, Gorton & Han**, "A survey of architecture design rationale," *JSS* 2006, DOI 10.1016/j.jss.2006.04.029. Abstract verified: **81 valid responses, practitioners**. Finding: practitioners say DR matters and report using it, but report barriers to capture. **Opinion-survey.**

## (b) Why capture failed — evidenced vs argued

The canonical theoretical account is **Shipman & Marshall, "Formality Considered Harmful," CSCW 8(4), 1999, pp. 333–352** — I retrieved the full 10-page PDF (archived from the authors' site) and read it. Critically, **it is an experience-reflection essay, not a measurement study**. Its own abstract: "This paper reflects on experiences designing, developing, and working with users of a variety of interactive computer systems." Grade: **opinion-essay grounded in system-building experience** (336 S2 citations).

Its four mechanisms, verbatim section headings: **3.1 Cognitive Overhead**, **3.2 Tacit Knowledge**, **3.3 Enforcing Premature Structure**, **3.4 Different People, Different Tasks: Situational Structure**.

Load-bearing passages:

- Cognitive overhead: "formalisms are often difficult for people to use because they need to take many extra steps (and make additional decisions) to specify anything. These extra decisions may involve chunking, naming, linking, and labeling." Two concrete write-offs are reported — the Virtual Notebook System profile matcher ("Work on the profile matcher was discontinued because the effort required… overcame the usefulness of the service") and an ATMS-over-Toulmin-structures experiment where "by the time contradictions had been identified… the user had performed a significant portion of the network evaluation. In this case, the additional processing done by the ATMS mechanism did not compensate the user for the initial effort." These are **narrated, not measured**.
- Tacit knowledge: "experts cannot reliably give an account of their expertise" (quoting Mittal & Dym 1985); "When such introspection becomes necessary… it necessarily interrupts the task; the introspection structures and changes it." The sharpest example: McCall's observation that design students cannot produce IBIS-style argumentation *even though videotapes show their natural discussions already follow IBIS structure* — "post hoc analysis is very different from generation."
- Premature structure: quoting Malone (1983) — "Leaving them out means I don't have to characterize them."
- On DR systems specifically (§2.2), they classify PHI and itIBIS experiences as "**limited successes**. The methods resulted in long-term cost reductions, but success relied on social pressure, extensive training, or continuing human facilitation." And: Conklin & Burgess Yakemovic "had little success in persuading other groups to use itIBIS outside of Burgess Yakemovic's development team."

**Is capture cost ever measured? Essentially no.** I found no study that instruments capture effort. The closest primary data points are descriptive: Karsenty's capture required **two dedicated scribes** (a company documentation specialist plus the author) processing **four audiotaped two-hour meetings** over a nine-month project phase, producing a document with **31 questions, 46 options, 25 criteria, 12 justifications** — and it was done by scribes precisely because "the design office chief… did not believe that the designers would take the time to document the DR themselves." The capture bottleneck is thus **richly evidenced as a phenomenon and argued as a mechanism, but not quantified.** Grade: **argued, not measured.**

## (c) Does recording *why* pay? The actual empirical record

Thin. Two studies manipulate or examine rationale availability against an outcome; only one is a controlled experiment.

**Strongest study — Bratthall, Johansson & Regnell, PROFES 2000, LNCS 1898, DOI 10.1007/978-3-540-45051-1_14, "Is a Design Rationale Vital when Predicting Change Impact? A Controlled Experiment on Software Architecture Evolution."** Abstract verified verbatim from the publisher page. **N = 17 subjects "from both industry and academia"** (mixed population — not purely students, not purely professionals). Realistic change tasks on **two complex embedded real-time systems**; retrospective DR document as treatment. Result: "for **one of the systems**, there is a significant improvement in **correctness and speed** when subjects have access to a design rationale document… **For the other system the results were inconclusive**." Qualitative analysis agreed DR helped. **No effect size is stated in the abstract; I did not obtain the full text, so effect magnitudes are UNVERIFIED.** Grade: **single-study, partially positive, split result, small N, never replicated** — this is the entire quantitative case for "recording why pays."

**Karsenty, CHI 1996, pp. 150–156, "An Empirical Evaluation of Design Rationale Documents"** — I read the **full primary text** (archived CHI'96 electronic proceedings). **N = 6 experienced professional designers (3 engineers, 3 technicians)**, all external to the project, at Aerospatiale (mechanical engineering). Task: understand and assess a past design, with blueprints plus a QOC DR document. Results: 138 questions coded from 6 sessions; **DR questions were the most frequent category (63% for engineers, 49% for technicians)**; and **only 41% of designers' DR questions (31 of 76) were answered by the DR document**, with the discrepancy attributed to analysts (14), readers' misconceptions/inappropriate questions (19), the project team (11), and QOC itself (1). Two usage modes were observed — "opportunistic" and "extensive." Karsenty's own conclusion is deflationary: "it is not because such documents… are available that they will be consulted, even if they contain information needed by designers!" Grade: **observational, no control condition, no outcome measure, N=6.** Note it contradicts Herbsleb & Kuwana on why-question frequency, which Karsenty attributes to situation (design meetings vs. reuse).

**Adjacent lead, unverified content:** Abbattista, Lanubile, Mastelloni & Visaggio, "An experiment on the effect of design recording on impact analysis," ICSM 1994, DOI 10.1109/icsm.1994.336769 — metadata only; **N and population UNVERIFIED**. This is the most promising unexamined thread.

**Everything else is opinion-survey.** Tang et al. 2006 (N=81 practitioners) measures belief, not outcome.

## (d) ADR state of evidence, 2024–2026

Nygard's 2011 blog post is origin, not research — correct. The empirical layer is recent, observational, and mostly *unflattering*.

- **Buchgeher, Schöberl, Geist, Dorninger, Haindl & Weinreich, IEEE Access 2023**, DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2023.3287654 — MSR study of ADRs on GitHub. Abstract verified via DOAJ: "adoption of ADRs is still low, although the number of repositories using ADRs is increasing every year. About **50% of all repositories with ADRs contain just one to five ADRs**, suggesting that the concept has been tried but not yet definitively adopted"; Nygard's template dominates. **Exact repository count UNVERIFIED** (IEEE Xplore returned 418/403 throughout).
- **De Santana, Figueiredo, Peixoto, Durão, Prazeres, Machado, Silveira Neto & de Almeida, ICSA 2026**, DOI 10.1109/ICSA66085.2026.00040, "Architecture Decision Records: Adoption, Impact, and Developer Engagement in Open-Source Software." Abstract verified via Semantic Scholar. **921 GitHub repositories, 5,800 ADRs**; PyDriller + SonarQube. Headline: "ADR-related variables exhibit **predominantly small correlations** with code quality and productivity metrics, suggesting that ADR adoption is associated with, **at most, modest observable effects at scale**." And: "**about 63% of ADRs are opened directly with a status of 'accepted' or equivalent, bypassing the deliberative context that ADRs are intended to capture**." Grade: **observational, large-N, correlational, null-ish.** This is the single most important 2026 datapoint.
- **Ahmeti, Linder, Groner & Wohlrab, ECSA 2024** (LNCS), DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-70797-1_22 — action research at one company with a microservice system: **7 interviews**, ADRs introduced and observed over **3 months**. Documentation-culture, knowledge-transfer and prioritization challenges "well addressed"; distributed-system documentation challenges remained open. Grade: **single-site action research**; outcomes are perceptions, not measures.
- **Nogueira, Silva & Conte, arXiv 2604.27333 (Apr 2026)**, "One Size Fits All? An Empirical Comparison of ADR Templates" — DESMET feature analysis by the two authors, then a **crossover controlled experiment with N = 33 undergraduate software engineering students** in a Software Architecture course, convenience sampling, block randomization. Nygard's template outperformed MADR on Overall Score. Grade: **single-study, student population**; note it measures template comprehension/usability, **not** whether rationale helps a later reader.
- **LLM/ADR intersection (2024–2026)** is now the bulk of activity: Dhar, Vaidhyanathan & Varma, ICSA 2024 (arXiv 2403.01709); DRAFT (arXiv 2504.08207); Gupta, Dhar, Feitosa & Vaidhyanathan, arXiv 2604.03826 — corpus from **750 OSS repositories**, finds a **3–5 record recency window** best for ADR generation context; Su, Bakhtin, Ahmad, Esposito, Lenarduzzi & Taibi, arXiv 2602.07609 — **980 ADRs across 109 GitHub repositories**, LLMs detect explicit code-inferable decision violations well, fail on implicit/deployment-oriented ones. All of these treat ADRs as *input to* or *output of* models; none measure human downstream benefit.
- One eye-catching but weak item: Kitayama, TechRxiv 2026 (DOI 10.36227/techrxiv.177205025.54351571/v2), claims ADRs-as-LLM-memory cut development time 10.1% (496 vs 552 min) and that ADR-carried *rationale* produced 16–25 E2E tests where identical instructions in CLAUDE.md produced zero. **N = 1 project, 3 conditions, single run, non-peer-reviewed preprint.** Grade: **folklore-adjacent single case** — provocative, cite only as a hypothesis.

## (e) Adjacent: commit messages and comments

- **Tian, Zhang, Stol, Jiang & Liu, "What Makes a Good Commit Message?", ICSE 2022 (arXiv 2202.02974)** — verified abstract. Sample of **~1,600 messages from five highly active OSS projects**; **~44% "could be improved"** on a Why/What framework. **Observational**; no downstream outcome.
- **Dhaouadi, Oakes & Famelis, arXiv 2403.18832 (2024)** — labelled dataset of Linux Kernel OOM-killer commit messages: **98.9% of commits contain sentences with rationale information**, and experienced developers report rationale in **~60% of sentences**. Observational; interesting counterweight to "developers never write why."

Neither ties why-recording to a measured maintenance outcome.

## (f) What I could not establish

1. **No effect sizes for Bratthall et al.** — full text not obtained; only the publisher abstract.
2. **Abbattista et al. ICSM 1994** — N, population, and result completely unverified. This is the most likely place a second controlled experiment exists.
3. **Shipman & McCall AI EDAM 1997** — abstract not retrieved; its three-perspectives argument is asserted in secondary literature only, and I will not paraphrase it.
4. **DRL / Lee primary source** — not obtained.
5. **Buchgeher's exact repository counts** — IEEE Xplore blocked throughout (403/418).
6. **No replication anywhere.** Every outcome-linked study is N-of-one. There is **no meta-analysis, no replicated experiment, and no measurement of capture cost** in the entire literature I could reach.
7. **QOC and gIBIS primary texts** were not read; metadata only.
8. OpenAlex exhausted its daily budget mid-session; DBLP returned 503; ACM DL and ScienceDirect returned 403 to every route including a real browser.

**Bottom line for the design-rationale-capture question:** the *failure* of structured capture is well-theorized and vividly documented but never costed; the *benefit* of recorded why rests on a single 17-subject experiment that succeeded on one of two systems, plus one 6-person observational study that found only 41% of readers' why-questions were answerable from a professionally-scribed QOC document; and the 2026 large-scale ADR evidence (921 repos) finds at most small correlations with quality, with 63% of ADRs skipping the deliberation they exist to record.
