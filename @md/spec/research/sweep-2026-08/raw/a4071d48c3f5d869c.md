## (a) Expertise reversal in real experts — the verdict

**Essentially no field evidence.** The strongest thing I can put in front of you is a negative census rather than an argument.

The canonical statements are Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (*Educational Psychologist* 2003, `10.1207/s15326985ep3801_4`) and Kalyuga (*Educ Psych Review* 2007, `10.1007/s10648-007-9054-3`). The decisive new document is **Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters & Brod, *Learning and Instruction* 2025** (`10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142`, open access via pedocs) — a PRISMA meta-analysis of **60 experimental studies, 176 effect sizes, N = 5,924**. Headline effects (verified in the PDF): low-prior-knowledge learners d = **0.505** [0.260, 0.750] favouring high assistance; high-prior-knowledge learners d = **−0.428** [−0.647, −0.209]; difference-of-differences d = **0.971** [0.631, 1.312], k = 88, j = 60. Heterogeneity is severe (I² = 87.55% for the high-knowledge side).

The critical detail is their moderator coding. "Educational status" has exactly five levels — Primary Education, Secondary Education, **Vocational Training**, Higher Education, Other — and the cell counts are Primary 2 studies (4 effects), Secondary 18(27), **Vocational Training 3(4)**, Higher Education 22(28), Other 4(13), Several 11(16). **There is no category for practising professionals, because there were no such studies to code.** The nearest proxy, vocational training, gives a high-prior-knowledge estimate of **d = −0.23, 95% CI [−1.41, 0.95], p = 0.702** — a null with a confidence interval spanning 2.4 d units. The authors' own illustrative example of a "large expertise difference" is Lemarié et al. 2017 comparing *hairdressers at the end of their vocational training* with psychology students. That is the ceiling of ecological validity in this literature.

Two further checks. PubMed holds **31 records total** for the phrase "expertise reversal"; a search for worked-examples RCTs with residents vs attendings returns **zero**. There is no medical field demonstration.

Two findings from genuine professionals actually point the *other* way. Martínez, Mollica & Gibson (PNAS 2023, below) found **no interaction** between text register and legal training in 105 practising lawyers (recall P = 0.360; comprehension, no evidence lawyers were disproportionately better, P = 0.638) — expertise raised the whole curve but did not buffer against complexity. Chervak & Drury (2003) similarly report that experience level did not interact with instruction language. Neither is a test of *assistance*, so neither refutes expertise reversal; but the only professional-population data available shows expertise as a main effect, not a moderator.

One more result from the meta-analysis matters for you: **the effect is asymmetric.** Adding assistance for novices is a larger effect than removing it for experts. "Strip guidance because your readers are experts" is the weaker half of an already student-bound finding.

Grade: **replicated in students; entirely absent in professionals.**

## (b) Aviation

**Degani & Wiener, NASA CR-177549 (May 1990)** — read from the primary PDF. Method, verified: jumpseat observation of **42 different crews, 72 flights (legs), ~140 flight hours** at one US carrier (B-737/B-757), plus A-310/A-320 simulator observation; face-to-face interviews with **10 captains and 5 first officers** from seven major US carriers, coordinated by ALPA; ASRS/NTSB/ICAO incident reports; FAA/NTSB and manufacturer interviews. There are **no experiments and no comparative outcome data.**

The output is 16 numbered guidelines in Appendix A, explicitly framed as "not specifications." The ones relevant to long-document design: **(7)** subdivide a long checklist into "smaller task-checklists or chunks that can be associated with systems and functions"; **(8)** sequence items by *geographical* cockpit organisation in a logical flow, with a pictorial flow scheme for training; **(10)** put the most critical items as close as possible to the *beginning* — and where this conflicts with (8) and (9), "this guideline (10) should take precedence"; **(4)** responses should portray desired status or value, not just "checked"/"set"; **(6)** an explicit completion call as the last written item; **(11)** duplicate critical items across task-checklists; **(12)** avoid tight coupling, provide buffers.

On method they distinguish **challenge-response** (more accurately "challenge-verification-response") from the **do-list** ("call-do-response"), plus combined, with a tabulation across 20 airlines worldwide; most carriers favour challenge-response or combined. **They present no evidence that either produces better performance** — indeed they quote the NTSB *asking* whether any method produces better performance. The 1993 *Human Factors* paper (35(2), `10.1177/001872089303500209`) is a condensation of the same work.

Grade: **observational / expert-analytic. Influential, unreplicated, never experimentally tested.**

**Burian, NASA/TM-2014-218382** (NTRS 20190002686) is the QRH study — and it is a **table-top document analysis, not a user study**: 11 QRH/checklist sets and 2 ECLSs (B777 ECL, A320 ECAM) across five types (A320, B737NG, B777, CRJ700, EMB190) from North American carriers. Verified figures: memory-item-bearing checklists per QRH range 1–22; total memory items per QRH range 1–87; of 239 memory items coded, 79% challenge-response, 8% sentence-form, 12% not action items at all (conditionals, timers, notes); 24 of 190 challenge-response items require both crew to confirm. Navigation findings: flow lines are hard to follow across pages and when nested; exclusive conditional sets are easier when marked *as a set*; some checklists chain jumps for no evident reason. Crucially, Burian tags the load-bearing usability questions — optimal QRC location, Do-Confirm vs Read-Do, error rates by item type — as **"Further Research Needed."** NASA's most detailed QRH analysis says the evidence does not exist.

**ASD-STE100.** I fetched the standard's own "about" page: it cites **no studies, trials, or evaluations**, only adoption and "feedback received from the users." It is a prescriptive standard. But it is not *entirely* unevidenced: Chervak, Drury & Ouellette, *Simplified English for Aircraft Workcards* (HFES Proc. 1996, `10.1177/154193129604000502`), and Chervak & Drury, *Effects of job instruction on maintenance task performance* (Occupational Ergonomics 2003, `10.3233/oer-2003-3204`) — the latter reports that Simplified English **reduced task errors** while a hybrid version **increased** them, on an internal-combustion-engine maintenance task, with no interaction with participant experience. **Both are paywalled; I could not verify N or effect sizes — mark UNVERIFIED.** So: two small studies, one lab, ~30 years old, proxy task, for a standard mandated across an industry.

## (c) Medicine

All three surgical-checklist numbers verified from PubMed primary abstracts.

**Haynes, NEJM 2009;360(5):491-9.** 8 hospitals in 8 cities; **3,733 patients pre, 3,955 post**; death **1.5% → 0.8%, P = 0.003**; inpatient complications **11.0% → 7.0%, P < 0.001**. Before-after, **no concurrent control**.

**Urbach, NEJM 2014;370(11):1029-38.** 101 hospitals, **109,341 pre / 106,370 post** procedures in 3-month windows. Adjusted mortality 0.71% → 0.65%, **OR 0.91 [0.80–1.03], P = 0.13**; complications 3.86% → 3.82%, **OR 0.97 [0.90–1.03], P = 0.29**. Null.

**Haugen, Ann Surg 2015;261(5):821-8** — the only randomized trial, a stepped-wedge cluster RCT in 2 hospitals, 5 surgical clusters; **2,212 control vs 2,263 checklist procedures**; complications **19.9% → 11.5%, P < 0.001**, ARR 8.4 [6.3–10.5]; LOS −0.8 days [0.11–1.43]; mortality fell significantly in **1 of 2** hospitals, overall 1.6% → 1.0% **not significant**.

The pattern is coherent: **the artifact does not carry the effect; the implementation does.** Where checklists are introduced as an engaged programme they work; where mandated by policy across a system they do not. Bosk, Dixon-Woods, Goeschel & Pronovost, "Reality check for checklists" (*Lancet* 2009;374:444-5) made exactly this argument — but it is a **two-page Comment with no data**. Opinion, influential.

**Guideline format.** The best professional-population evidence is **Grol et al., BMJ 1998;317(7162):858-61**, verified: **61 GPs, 12,880 decisions**, 47 recommendations from 10 national guidelines, 12 attributes. Overall compliance 61% (7,915/12,880). **Vague/non-specific recommendations followed in 36% (826/2,280) vs clear recommendations 67% (7,089/10,600).** Also controversial 35% vs non-controversial 68%; requiring change to routines 44% vs not 67%; evidence-based 71% vs not 57%. This is **observational and confounded** — specificity, controversy, and required behaviour change are entangled attributes of the same recommendations. It is correlational, not a manipulation.

**Michie & Johnston, BMJ 2004;328(7435):343-5** is an *Education and debate* piece — **three pages, no primary data**. Opinion, frequently miscited as evidence.

The one **randomized** format trial in practising clinicians: **Brandt et al., BMJ Open 2017;7(2):e011569** — **181 practising physicians** (156 internal medicine, 25 GP) across 7 hospitals and 2 primary-care settings in Lebanon, Norway, Spain, UK, randomized to a multilayered vs standard narrative guideline format. **Preference: 72% [65–79] vs 16% [10–22].** But **correct understanding 72% vs 58%, P = 0.06**, and appropriate clinical action **98% vs 92%, P = 0.10** — both non-significant. Strong preference, unproven comprehension gain. This is the single most transferable data point in the whole sweep, and it is a null on the outcome that matters.

Supporting: Kristiansen et al., *Chest* 2015;147(3):754-763 (format development; feedback from 27 stakeholders, think-aloud user testing with **47 practising physicians** from six countries). Kastner's GUIDE-M (*Implementation Science* 2015, `10.1186/s13012-015-0225-1`) and Shiffman's GLIA (`10.1186/1472-6947-5-23`) are **model and instrument development, not outcome trials** — no evidence that scoring higher on them changes adherence.

**Think-aloud on physicians reading guidelines:** Kilsdonk et al., *Int J Med Inform* 2016;86:10-19 — **13 healthcare professionals (6 physicians, 7 physician assistants)** preparing two fictitious consults from a paper guideline. Finding: the paper guideline **did not support practitioners in finding patient-specific recommendations**; the failure was retrieval and navigation, not comprehension. Small and qualitative, but a real professional population reading a real long document.

**Eye-tracking physicians reading guidelines: a PubMed search returned zero records.** I could not establish that this literature exists.

## (d) Legal / standards

**Charrow & Charrow (1979), *Columbia Law Review* 79(7), `10.2307/1121842`** — venue, year, authors and 325 citations **verified**; the article is paywalled with no accessible abstract, so **N and effect sizes are UNVERIFIED.** Do not quote numbers from it without the PDF.

The genuinely strong material is recent and uses professionals:

**Martínez, Mollica & Gibson, *Cognition* 2022;224:105070** (`10.1016/j.cognition.2022.105070`): corpus analysis of **≈10 million words** showing contracts are disproportionately loaded with low-frequency jargon, **center-embedded clauses**, passives and non-standard capitalisation relative to nine baseline genres; two experiments, **N = 184**, found these features reduced recall and comprehension **"even for experienced readers,"** with **center-embedding inhibiting recall more than other features**.

**Martínez, Mollica & Gibson, PNAS 2023;120:e2302672120** (PMC10266064, full text read): two preregistered experiments. **Experiment 1: n = 105 lawyers**, compared against a reanalysis of **n = 108 laypeople** on identical materials. Main effects of register *and* legal training on recall and comprehension, but **no register × legal-training interaction** (recall P = 0.360; comprehension, P = 0.638 for lawyers being disproportionately better). **Experiment 2: n = 102 lawyers** rated simplified contracts **equally enforceable** and higher on overall quality, appropriateness of style, author hireability and likelihood of client signature. Results robust to years of practice experience. This is the best-designed professional-reader experiment in the entire sweep, and its finding is that **domain expertise does not neutralise bad prose.**

On lawyers' actual reading process at scale, on contract-review workflow studies, and on navigating interlinked standards corpora — **I found nothing empirical.** Crossref queries returned only doctrinal and law-and-economics work.

## (e) What transfers, and what does not

**Transfers (professional-population evidence):**
1. **Syntactic complexity taxes experts too.** Center-embedding and long-distance dependencies degrade recall in lawyers as much as in laypeople (PNAS 2023, n = 105; Cognition 2022, N = 184). This is the single best-evidenced principle. Expertise is a main effect, not a shield.
2. **Specificity correlates with use.** Vague recommendations 36% vs clear 67% (Grol, 61 GPs, 12,880 decisions) — observational and confounded, but large and in a real professional population making real decisions.
3. **Retrieval, not comprehension, is the failure mode.** Kilsdonk's practitioners could read the guideline; they could not *find* the applicable recommendation. Burian's pilots face flow lines across pages and unexplained jump chains. For long professional documents read repeatedly, navigation architecture is the dominant variable — and this is the one place aviation and medicine independently converge.
4. **Chunking into function-bounded units, and critical-items-first.** Degani & Wiener's guidelines (7) and (10), including the explicit ruling that criticality *beats* spatial/logical ordering when they conflict. Observational, but derived from 140 hours of watching experts and from accident reports.
5. **Preference is not comprehension.** Brandt's 181 physicians overwhelmingly preferred the multilayered format (72% vs 16%) while understanding moved only 58% → 72%, P = 0.06. Any format claim resting on practitioner preference surveys should be graded down accordingly.

**Does not transfer:**
- **Expertise reversal as a design rule for professionals.** Zero field studies; the vocational-training proxy is 3 studies with a CI spanning 2.4 d; the effect is asymmetric against the "strip guidance for experts" reading.
- **The document-as-intervention model.** Haynes → Urbach → Haugen is the cleanest natural experiment available on whether a well-designed procedural document changes outcomes when deployed at scale. It does not, absent implementation work. Designing the artifact is necessary and radically insufficient.
- **Read-and-do vs challenge-response.** Thirty-five years after Degani & Wiener catalogued them across 20 airlines, there is still no comparative performance evidence. Any claim here is folklore.
- **Simplified Technical English as validated.** Two small paywalled studies from one lab on a proxy task, against an industry-wide mandate. Grade: **prescriptive standard with a thin, unverified, thirty-year-old evidence base** — weaker than "no evidence," but far weaker than its status implies.

The generalisation gap you flagged in the education literature is not closed by the professional literature — it is simply unaddressed.
