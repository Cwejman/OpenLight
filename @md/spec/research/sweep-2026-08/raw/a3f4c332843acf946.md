## THE OVERREACH LIST

1. **"Two levels, three at most."** Nothing verified supports it. Miller 1981 found 8² optimal *at 64 items*; Kiger's fastest was 4×16; Larson & Czerwinski's winner was 16×32 — 512 items, two levels, **thirty-two-wide**. Every result is a fixed-item-count optimum, and the winning breadths (8, 16, 32) all exceed the working-memory numbers the rule is derived from.
2. **Cowan's "4" as a design ceiling.** He states four boundary conditions under which chunking is *blocked*; a navigable hierarchy meets none of them.
3. **Chase & Simon as evidence for a chunk limit.** They *assumed* Miller's 7±2 and tested a hypothesis inside it. N=3.
4. **"7±2".** Miller called it "a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence" and distinguished two different spans.
5. **Dunbar CI "16 to 109".** The real 95% CIs are 4–520 and 2–336. The paper's conclusion is that no number can be derived, not that the number is smaller.
6. **Romer as a compounding proof.** Unbounded growth is an assumed linearity in A, by his own admission; and he explicitly denies that population size suffices.
7. **Pimentel percentages on the wrong denominator** (863,878 attempted valid executions, not 1.4M).
8. **Luhmann as an existence proof for shallow structure.** The archive documents up to four sub-levels, 108 divisions in ZK I, and one division with 120 subdivisions.
9. **Ausubel/advance organizers as a strong effect.** d = .21; and Stone found the high-effect studies violated Ausubel's predicted variable levels.
10. **GitLab's 605,000 words** — no primary source.
11. **Graphic organizers 1.24 vs 0.80** — no primary source.
12. **Wikipedia's "50 kB split trigger"** — still in WP:Summary style verbatim, but WP:SIZERULE itself now runs on word counts, and the number is a "starting point," not a trigger.

## SAFE FORMULATIONS (usable verbatim)

- Cowan (2001) argues for "a single, central capacity limit averaging about four chunks," observable only when experimental conditions block recoding into larger chunks; he later restates it as 3–5 items in young adults.
- Miller (1956) reported a span of about seven items but suspected the number "is only a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence," and noted that recoding into chunks raises effective capacity.
- Chase and Simon (1973), studying three players, found that chess masters' recall advantage disappeared on randomly arranged boards, and framed their chunk counts inside Miller's already-assumed span.
- D. P. Miller (1981) found goal-acquisition time over 64 targets to be U-shaped across depth, minimised at two levels of eight.
- Larson and Czerwinski (1998), with 19 experienced web users and 512 items, found the three-level 8×8×8 structure significantly slower and more disorienting than either two-level structure, with no reliable difference between 16×32 and 32×16.
- Lindenfors, Wartel and Lind (2021) report 95% confidence intervals of 4–520 and 2–336 for human group size and conclude that "a cognitive limit on human group size cannot be derived in this manner."
- Romer (1990) models technology as "a nonrival, partially excludable good" whose instructions, once created, "can be used over and over again at no additional cost."
- DeChurch and Mesmer-Magnus (2010) meta-analysed 231 correlations from 65 studies (3,738 groups, ~18,240 members), finding corrected correlations of .38 between team cognition and team performance and .35 with team process.
- GitLab states that its handbook, "printed… consists of over 2,000 pages of text," and still operates handbook-first, with internal-only content in a separate internal handbook.
- The Niklas Luhmann-Archiv reports approximately 90,000 slips across two collections (ZK I ≈ 22,000, 1952–1960; ZK II ≈ 67,000, 1961–1997), with up to four sub-levels and no strictly hierarchical ordering.
- Pimentel et al. (2019) attempted 863,878 executions of valid Jupyter notebooks drawn from 1,159,166 unique notebooks in 264,023 GitHub repositories; 24.11% ran without error and 4.03% reproduced the stored outputs.
- Luiten, Ames and Ackerson (1980, *AERJ* 17(2)) meta-analysed 135 studies and reported a mean effect size of .21 for advance organizers on learning.
- Stone (1983) analysed 112 studies and found that "levels of some variables in the high-effect-size studies were not as predicted by Ausubel's model."

## STILL UNVERIFIED

- **Kiger 1984's actual text.** Elsevier/ScienceDirect returns 403 + CAPTCHA; no abstract in Crossref, OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, or Unpaywall; no OA copy. The finding above is Larson & Czerwinski's peer-reviewed description of it, which is secondary. Needs library access.
- **Miller 1981 N and population.** SAGE paywall on the HFES proceedings; the abstract says only "four experimental groups."
- **Larson & Czerwinski's exact age/occupation breakdown** beyond "experienced computer and web users" from Microsoft's participant pool.
- **GitLab's ~605,000 words.** Absent from every GitLab page I fetched, live and archived.
- **"Graphic organizers ES 1.24 vs expository 0.80."** Stone's full text is not on ERIC (`e_fulltextauth = 0`) and *J. Experimental Education* is paywalled; the numbers appear in neither abstract nor ERIC record. Assume fabricated or blog-laundered until a page locator turns up.
- **Post-2001 challenges to Cowan.** I confirmed Cowan's own 2010 restatement (3–5, young adults) and that the 2001 piece is a BBS target article with open commentary, but I did not have the search budget to chase the discrete-slots vs continuous-resource literature. Treat "challenged since" as likely but unverified here.
