## 7. SE FOLKLORE (revised — trace complete)

**"Developers spend 10x more time reading than writing." Grade: FOLKLORE.** Verified verbatim against the full text of Martin's *Clean Code* (2008), Ch. 1: *"Indeed, the ratio of time spent reading vs. writing is well over 10:1."* Its stated provenance is in the preceding paragraphs and is honest about itself: *"Have you ever played back an edit session? In the 80s and 90s we had editors like Emacs that kept track of every keystroke… When I did this, the results were fascinating. The vast majority of the playback was scrolling and navigating to other modules!"* No instrumentation, no N, no measurement. Chapter 1's bibliography has exactly two entries (Beck 2007, Knuth 1992), neither about the ratio. No earlier numerical source exists; the figure appears to originate with Martin. Note the passage does not even measure what it is cited for — the playback observation is about *scrolling and navigation*, not comprehension.

**"Maintenance is 40–80% of cost" / "50% of time on comprehension." Grade: SPLIT.** The maintenance-share half is PARTIALLY-TRACEABLE to real survey work — Lientz, Swanson & Tompkins (CACM 1978, **69 respondents of 120 organisations**) and Lientz & Swanson (CACM 1981, **487 organisations**). But the 1978 paper already launders rumour in its own abstract: *"Rough estimates of the total systems and programming resources consumed range as high as 75–80 percent."* The famous 21/25/50/4 effort split lives in the 1980 book, which is lending-restricted and unverified at source.

The comprehension half is the folklore. **Fjeldstad & Hamlen (1983)** — the load-bearing citation — is effectively unretrievable: no DOI, absent from Crossref, OpenAlex and DBLP, containing volume unscanned. **It is cited-but-unread, and the citations prove it**: Minelli et al. cite it as *"IEEE, 1982, pp. 13–30"*; Xia et al. cite the same item as *"Proc. Guide, 1983, Art. no. 48."* Incompatible records for one claim is the signature of a reference copied between papers. Xia et al. are candid: Zelkowitz et al., Fjeldstad & Hamlen and Corbi all report the >50% figure *"based on anecdotal evidences."*

**"~5% of time writing new code." Grade: FOLKLORE, honestly labelled.** Retrieved in full from the Wayback Machine (Hallam, blogs.msdn.com, 4 Jan 2006, reproducing a 2002 internal email). His self-observation: New Code 2%, Modifying 20%, Understanding 78%. The circulating "5%" is his *hypothetical junior persona* number, not even his own measurement. He writes *"No, I am not making this up"* and follows with argument, not data.

**A mis-citation inside the peer-reviewed literature.** Both Minelli et al. and Xia et al. cite Ko et al. (TSE 2006) for "understanding a program occupies around 35 percent of the total time." The primary text says: developers spend 35% of their time on *"the mechanics of navigation within and between source files."* That is navigation overhead, not comprehension — and it is the number most often laundered into the reading-vs-writing argument.

**Plus the four field-specific items I verified myself** (unchanged from the delivered report): checklists are no better than ad hoc reading, twice, in both populations; inspection meetings produce no net defect-detection gain, twice; "developers skip the prose" traces to a hedge rather than a measurement; and Ciolkowski's PBR meta-analysis reports *"strong indicators of researcher bias"* in the very literature that supplies the 21%→38% figure.

**What *is* measured.** Three instrumented studies, and they do not conflict once you check the denominators:

- **Xia et al., IEEE TSE 2018** — the strongest. **78 professional developers**, 7 real projects at two Chinese IT companies, **10 working days each, 3,148 effective working hours**, monitored *across all applications* (not just the IDE), tool-vs-manual validation discrepancy <0.23%. **Comprehension 57.62%**, navigation 23.96%, **editing 5.02%**. Crucially, a large share of "reading" is not code: browsers 27.26%, IDEs 19.95%, document editors 10.38% of comprehension time (ANOVA F=32.4, p<0.001).
- **Minelli et al., ICPC 2015** — the 70% figure is real (69.85%, 68.5% time-weighted) but fragile: **N=18** "professionals and academics," >85% of data from three PhD students, Pharo IDE only, and "comprehension" is *defined* as every inter-action gap over one second, which the authors themselves flag may be "the programmer checking his phone."
- **Meyer et al., IEEE TSE 2017** — **20 professionals, ~11 workdays each**, whole-day denominator: **Coding (read/edit/navigate) 21.0%**, email 14.5%, browsing 11.4%, documents 6.6%, meetings 9.9%. Xia's 58% and Meyer's 21% are compatible: different denominators.

**Reading order is non-linear and less linear with expertise.** Busjahn et al., ICPC 2015 (correct title: *"Relaxing the Linear Order"*): **14 novices** (non-CS university students) followed linear story order **~80% on prose vs ~70% on code**; **6 professional engineers** (5–28 years) showed **~60%** linearity on code. Directionally solid, N=6 experts. The field's base rate: Sharafi et al.'s SLR of 36 eye-tracking studies is roughly **28 student-based to 6 professional**, typical N 10–30.

**One cross-verified null worth flagging.** Both the folklore agent and I independently retrieved the Uddin & Robillard preprint and confirmed that their validation survey **asked** "approximately how many hours on average per week do you spend consulting API documentation?" — and **never reports the answer**. If a per-week documentation-reading figure is circulating attributed to that paper, it does not come from it.

## Addition to §6 — a real expertise effect, in professionals

Xia et al. found comprehension time falls monotonically with experience: **low / medium / high experience = 66.37% / 55.97% / 44.43%** of working time (ANOVA F=79.4, p<2.2e-16; pairwise Cohen's d "large"), across 78 professionals over 3,148 instrumented hours. This is the best-powered expertise finding in the sweep, and it is **not** expertise reversal: experts spend proportionally less time comprehending, which is what expertise is supposed to buy. It sits alongside, not against, the three professional studies finding that experience does not moderate *documentation difficulty* — experts read faster, and are blocked by the same content defects as everyone else.

**The bottom line is unchanged, and now better supported:** invest in correctness, completeness, currency, findability, normative worked examples, and an explicit purpose-specific reading procedure. Do not invest in format, layering depth, or stripping guidance for expert readers — and do not cite the 10:1 ratio.
