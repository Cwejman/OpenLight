# KNOWLEDGE STRUCTURE — the cognitive-science floor under six writing principles

## 1. HEADLINE

- **The expertise-reversal effect is real and strong (d = 0.971 for the interaction, 60 studies, 5,924 participants, no publication bias) — but it is asymmetric and one-sided in a way that matters here.** Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters & Brod (2025, *Learning and Instruction* 98:102142) found low-prior-knowledge learners gain from assistance (d = 0.505) far more reliably than high-prior-knowledge learners are *harmed* by it (d = −0.428 overall, but **non-significant within every single education stratum**: secondary −0.10, p = .72; higher education −0.09, p = .75; vocational −0.23, p = .70). The "helping experts hurts them" half is largely an artifact of comparing populations that differ in education level, not expertise within a population. Scaffolding a document for the novice costs the resident expert much less than the folklore claims.
- **Ausubel's mechanism did not survive; the effect did, weakly.** Stone's (1983) meta-analysis of 112 studies found advance organizers help — but "levels of some variables in the high-effect-size studies were not as predicted by Ausubel's model." Graphic and concrete organizers outperform abstract expository prose ones. The principle "the opening should be *deliberately more abstract*" is the part of Ausubel that the evidence specifically failed to confirm.
- **Prequestions buy attention, not comprehension.** A preregistered meta-analysis found g = 0.54 (k = 97) for material the questions covered and **g = 0.04 (k = 91) for everything else in the same text.** Framing a document as an answer to a stated question improves learning of exactly what you named and does nothing for the rest.
- **Narrative beats exposition by the largest margin in this entire literature.** Mar et al. (2021, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*): >75 samples, >33,000 participants, stories better understood and better recalled than essays, "robust… not moderated by various study characteristics." Meanwhile seductive details (irrelevant-but-interesting) reliably harm learning. The operative variable is *relevance*, not concreteness — which partly vindicates and partly misstates the project's "felt beats abstract for motivation, expository for laws."
- **Text-structure effects evaporate on delay.** Bogaerds-Hazenberg et al. (2021, *Reading Research Quarterly*, 44 studies): immediate g = 0.25 comprehension / 0.38 recall / 0.58 summarization — and **all delayed effects non-significant.** Pyle et al. (2017, RRQ, 21 studies, 4,254 students): high-quality studies d = 0.70 [0.42, 0.98] vs. low-quality d = 1.13 — study quality nearly halves the effect.
- **Hypertext research is genuinely unflattering, and the fix is hierarchy, not fewer words.** DeStefano & LeFevre (2007) found link-driven decision-making impaired reading; a 2020 study of 54 biology teacher-trainees found network structures produced far more disorientation than hierarchical ones (F(1,50) = 17.23, p < .001, Cohen's f = 0.58) and much less coherent reading sequences (f = 1.35), hurting low-prior-knowledge readers most (d = 2.47 vs 1.58).
- **The design effects shrink exactly where documents live.** Noetel et al. (2021, *Review of Educational Research*; 29 reviews, 1,189 studies, 78,177 participants): "Good design was more important for more complex materials, and in **system-paced** environments (e.g., lectures) than **self-paced** ones (e.g., websites)." A reader who controls their own pace recovers much of what bad design costs.

## 2. PRINCIPLE-BY-PRINCIPLE VERDICT

### 1. Reader's order governs — why before how, opening more abstract than body
**QUALIFIED — the ordering claim is supported and small; the abstraction claim is unsupported and possibly backwards.**

The ancestor is Ausubel's advance organizer. Luiten, Ames & Ackerson (1980, *AERJ*, 135 studies) and Stone (1983, *J. Experimental Education*, 29 reports / 112 studies) both find positive effects on learning and retention. The effects are small — the Luiten figure is conventionally cited around d ≈ 0.2, which I could not verify against the primary source in this session and should be treated as unconfirmed. Stone's finding that high-effect studies did *not* match Ausubel's predicted variable levels is the important one: the empirical effect is real, the theoretical mechanism (subsumption under a more abstract, more inclusive frame) is not what produces it. Secondary summaries report graphic organizers at ES ≈ 1.24 versus expository organizers ≈ 0.80 — I could not verify these numbers at source, but the ordering (concrete/graphic > abstract prose) recurs everywhere and is echoed by a 2007 web-course study where concept-map organizers consistently beat text outlines and the overall AO effect was null.

Supporting the *placement* half strongly: Meyer's (1975) levels effect — the type and structure of relations among concepts "dramatically influenced comprehension when they occur at the top third of the structure," while the same relations low in the structure "affected comprehension minimally." Where an idea sits in the hierarchy is load-bearing for what gets comprehended and recalled.

Two qualifications bite. First, the prequestion result: purpose-framing improves learning of the framed content (g = 0.54) and not the surrounding content (g = 0.04). "Why before how" is an attention allocator, not a comprehension multiplier. Second, expertise reversal: an organizer is assistance, and the high-prior-knowledge reader gains least from it (though, per Tetzlaff et al., is measurably harmed less than assumed).

**Verdict: SUPPORTED for motivation-before-machinery ordering and for putting the governing relation at the top level. CONTRADICTED for "deliberately more abstract" — the evidence favours a concrete, graphic, or scenario opening over an abstract one.**

### 2. The fold — self-standing bounded unit, split on reasons-to-exist not length
**QUALIFIED — bounded units are supported; the split criterion has no evidence and the size question has none either.**

Rey, Beege, Nebel, Wirzberger, Schmitt & Schneider (2019, *Educational Psychology Review*): 56 investigations, 88 pairwise comparisons — significant segmenting effect, small-to-medium for retention and transfer, reduced cognitive load, increased learning time. Noetel et al. (2021) list segmentation among the principles with robust meta-analytic support.

But the mechanism is *learner-controlled pacing*, not conceptual self-containment, and the same meta-meta-analysis says design effects are weaker in self-paced environments — which is what reading a document is. The evidence for "self-standing" as such comes obliquely from the hypertext literature (below): a unit whose comprehension requires following links out is worse.

On size: there are **no numbers**. Miller's 7±2 is the immediate-recall span for chunks in serial-recall tasks; Cowan's revision puts effective working memory nearer 4; neither is a prescription for section length, and treating either as one is a misapplication. No study in this literature identifies an optimal document or section length.

**Verdict: SUPPORTED for bounded, individually digestible units. NO EVIDENCE for the "two reasons to exist" split criterion or for any specific size, and the project should stop implying the psychology supplies one.**

### 3. The reference — a link states what is there before you follow it; text reads complete with links deleted
**SUPPORTED — the best-evidenced of the six, though by inference from hypertext research rather than by direct test.**

DeStefano & LeFevre (2007, *Computers in Human Behavior* 23:1616–1641) reviewed the field and concluded that "the increased demands of decision-making and visual processing in hypertext impaired reading performance," that learning is better with **fewer** links, and that readers low in working memory and prior knowledge are the ones disadvantaged. The 2020 disorientation study cited above shows that *hierarchical* link structures consistent with the domain largely rescue this, while network structures do not. Conklin's "cognitive overhead" (1987) is the conceptual ancestor and remains conceptual — it is not an empirical finding.

The specific claim that a link should announce *outcome* rather than *topic* has no direct experimental test I could find; it is a plausible extrapolation from the signalling literature (cues that tell the reader what a segment contains improve selection) rather than an established result. The "how settled it is" component — epistemic status in the link — has no evidence at all.

**Verdict: SUPPORTED for minimising navigation dependency and for hierarchical, predictable link structure. NO EVIDENCE for the outcome-vs-topic phrasing rule or the settledness marker, though neither is contradicted.**

### 4. One home, many paths — every fact authoritative in exactly one place
**CONTRADICTED as justified by cognitive load theory; NO EVIDENCE as a comprehension rule.**

The redundancy effect does not say what the principle needs it to say. Kalyuga, Chandler & Sweller (2004, *Human Factors*, 3 experiments, 25 technical apprentices) demonstrated that presenting *identical* content in written and spoken form **simultaneously**, within one working-memory episode, degrades learning. It is a claim about concurrent channels under time pressure, not about a fact appearing authoritatively in one document and being restated in another read weeks apart. Noetel et al. confirm "verbal redundancy" as a robust effect — again, same-moment duplication.

Pushing the other way: McNamara et al.'s (1996) high-coherence texts *were* the more explanatory, more elaborated, partly restating ones, and they won for low-knowledge readers. Distributed restatement is the mechanism behind spacing, one of the most robust effects in learning science.

The one carve-out the principle already makes — restatement permitted only at a coarser level of abstraction — is roughly what the advance-organizer literature endorses, since an organizer *is* a restatement at a higher level that measurably helps.

**Verdict: The single-source discipline may be excellent for maintenance and correctness. It is not a comprehension finding, and citing the redundancy effect for it would be a misreading. The coarser-abstraction carve-out is the only part with empirical backing.**

### 5. Status is part of the writing — settled/proposed/open, marked in place
**NO EVIDENCE in this literature.**

Nothing in the advance-organizer, signalling, cognitive-load, or comprehension-model literatures addresses epistemic status markers. The nearest adjacent body is multiple-document comprehension and sourcing (Britt, Rouet, Bråten), where readers who attend to *source* build better documents models — but that concerns attribution and reliability of sources, not author-assigned settledness labels on claims, and I did not verify it to this report's evidence standard. Treat this principle as unevaluated rather than supported.

### 6. Every stopping point pays; delete a section that only sets up other sections
**QUALIFIED, and the deletion clause is CONTRADICTED.**

"Prefix usability" is not a studied construct. The nearest support is Meyer's levels effect — top-level ideas carry disproportionate comprehension and recall, so a reader who stops early has captured the most load-bearing material. That is consistent with the principle but is not a test of it.

The deletion clause runs directly into Mayer's **pre-training principle**, which is exactly a segment that exists only to set up other segments (naming and characterising components before explaining the process), and which appears among the meta-analytically supported multimedia principles in Noetel et al. (2021). Advance organizers are the same shape: a section whose sole function is to prepare the reader for later sections, with a small but replicated positive effect. Applying principle 6 literally would delete two instructional elements the evidence supports.

**Verdict: SUPPORTED in spirit (front-load the load-bearing relations); CONTRADICTED in the specific rule that setup-only sections should be deleted.**

## 3. WHAT THE SIX PRINCIPLES MISS ENTIRELY

- **Signal words and connectives.** The single cheapest supported intervention. Richter, Scheiter & Eitel (2016, *Educational Research Review*, 27 studies, 45 comparisons, N = 2,464): r = .17 [.11, .22] for comprehension and transfer, moderated by prior knowledge. Alpizar et al. (2020, *ETR&D*, 29 studies, 44 effects, N = 2,726): retention d+ = 0.387, comprehension d+ = 0.317, transfer d+ = 0.327 — larger for low-prior-knowledge readers. Meyer's structure-strategy work identifies explicit signalling words ("the problem is", "in contrast", "as a result") as the cue that switches weak readers out of a list strategy into structural processing. No principle mentions them.
- **Genre.** Mar et al. (2021): the narrative advantage across 33,000+ participants is larger and more robust than almost anything else here, and it is unmoderated. The principles have no position on genre beyond a motivation carve-out.
- **Prompting the reader to generate.** Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi & Winne (2018, *Educational Psychology Review*, 64 reports, 69 effects): inducing self-explanation yields g = 0.55. Documents can carry such prompts; none of the six principles contemplate the text asking anything of the reader.
- **Adaptivity as such.** Tetzlaff et al. (2025) frame expertise reversal as the cornerstone of *adaptivity* — the recommendation is explicitly to vary assistance with reader knowledge. The project's rejection of forking prose for novice/expert is not supported by this literature; it is merely not refuted, because skippability-as-adaptation has not been tested against forking.
- **Durability.** Bogaerds-Hazenberg et al. (2021) found every delayed effect non-significant. Structural interventions that look strong at immediate test may buy nothing a week later.
- **Reader goals.** Relevance instructions and reading purpose reliably reshape what gets encoded; the principles treat the reader's order as a property of the text alone.

## 4. THE GENERALISATION GAP

This is itself a finding, and it is severe.

**Populations.** McNamara et al. (1996) — junior high school students. O'Reilly & McNamara (2007) — 143 college students. Bogaerds-Hazenberg et al. (2021) — grades 4–6. Pyle et al. (2017) — grades 2–12. The segmenting, signalling, and seductive-details meta-analyses are dominated by undergraduates in laboratory sessions. Even the expertise-reversal meta-analysis, the most professionally relevant here, contains only 3 vocational-training studies out of 60, and its "expert" is typically a student in the upper half of a median split on a pretest — not a staff engineer with five years in a codebase.

**Materials.** Short passages, typically a few hundred to a couple of thousand words, read once, in a single 15–30 minute session. Almost nothing in this literature studies a 5,000-word specification, let alone a corpus of interlinked ones read repeatedly over months.

**Outcomes.** Immediate free recall, multiple choice, and "transfer" measured minutes after reading. The project's top test — decide a case the text does not cover — maps onto Kintsch's situation model, and McNamara et al. (1996) is the key result: "the rewards to be gained from active processing are primarily at the level of the situation model rather than at the superficial level of text-base understanding." That is the right theoretical target, but it has been operationalised as inference questions and sorting tasks on biology passages, not as engineering judgment.

**Direction of the gap.** It runs against the project. Professionals reading specs are the high-prior-knowledge, self-paced, goal-directed readers — and that is precisely the corner where signalling effects shrink, advance-organizer effects shrink, coherence effects reverse or vanish, and Noetel's meta-meta-analysis says design matters least. The one genuinely relevant professional study I found — Kalyuga, Chandler & Sweller's 25 technical apprentices on technical instruction — concerns simultaneous audio/visual redundancy, not document architecture.

**Honest grading of replication status.** Expertise reversal: meta-analytically supported, no publication bias detected (Egger z = 0.568, p = .57; z = 0.299, p = .77). Segmenting, signalling, seductive details, self-explanation: meta-analytically supported but moderator-heavy and inside a field where the Open Science Collaboration (2015) replication rate was 36%. Reverse cohesion: replicated but *reversed under conditions* (see below). Cognitive load theory: survived by amputation — Sweller dropped germane load as a separate category in response to the unfalsifiability critique, and Skulmowski & Rey (2021, *Educational Psychology Review*) document five design factors that raise extraneous load while still improving learning, directly challenging "minimise extraneous load." Disfluency-as-desirable-difficulty: **refuted** — Weissgerber et al. (2024) direct replication null, and a 2025 multiverse analysis in *Learning and Instruction* found no replicable moderation by working memory capacity.

**The coherence gap, stated correctly.** McNamara, Kintsch, Songer & Kintsch (1996): low-knowledge readers benefit from coherent text; high-knowledge readers benefit from *minimally* coherent text, because poor writing forces compensatory inference — and the benefit appears at the situation-model level, not the textbase. But O'Reilly & McNamara (2007) added the third variable: the low-cohesion benefit was **restricted to less-skilled, high-knowledge readers**; skilled comprehenders with high knowledge did better with the *high*-cohesion text, and the interaction was confined to text-based questions. So: deliberately under-explaining does not help your best readers. It helps knowledgeable readers who are poor at comprehension. That is not a licence to write badly for experts.
