# Encounters

*Research register. Written 2026-08-29 from the 45 raw reports of the 2026-08-26 sweep — [`raw/`](raw/) — and from nothing else; no page was fetched, no finding re-verified. It is a walk among the people and works the sweep found, arranged so each encounter gives the next one more meaning. It is not a scorecard of this project's claims; the project is only the shore the walk departs from. Every link is an offer, never a missing piece — the sentence around it says what opening that door would give, and the text reads whole with every door shut. Two cautions travel with everything here, inherited from the sweep itself: the agents' search budgets ran out early, so the evidence over-samples what could be fetched directly (papers, repos, APIs — not blogs, not paywalled journals); and two agents independently caught automated summarisers fabricating verbatim quotations and sample sizes, so every figure below is worth exactly what its agent's own verification note says, and that note is carried in the sentence.*

## Why this reads as a story

One finding in the sweep licenses the form of this document, so it goes first. Mar, Li, Nguyen and Ta's 2021 meta-analysis — more than 75 samples, more than 33,000 participants — found that stories are better understood and better recalled than essays, an advantage the sweep's comprehension agent describes, from the paper's own framing, as "robust… not moderated by various study characteristics," and calls the largest margin in the entire comprehension literature. The agent carried the figures from secondary reads of the meta-analysis, not a from-scratch audit, so hold the effect size loosely — but the direction is the best-replicated thing in a field where most effects evaporate on delay. The literature on how people learn from text says, about itself: tell it as a story.

So: the people, in the order that compounds.

## I. The people who bet against text

Four teams spent the last decade building the position that code — and by extension knowledge — should not live as text files. By 2026 all four have written their own endings, and the endings are the most instructive documents any of them produced.

**Paul Biggar** built Darklang: a language whose programs lived in a structured online editor, never as files. In June 2025 he wrote the company's obituary himself, and the sweep's agent read it at the source: *"Dark Inc has officially run out of money."* The cause he names is not the market or the money but the medium: *"it became very obvious that our product was not the right one for the era of coding agents. Our online structured editor didn't make sense when the LLM is generating the code."* His colleague Stachu's earlier post-mortem is blunter still — *"our custom in-browser editor was bad, and disjointed from our users' 'normal' development flows"*; *"Too fancy for our own good"*; and the detail that stays with you: *"the editor was ~50% of our codebase."* Half of everything they built was the thing that killed them. The successor company, employee-owned, open-sourced the language and ships fast — pre-1.0, pivoted to AI. Both confessions are worth reading whole, in Biggar's and Stachu's own voices: [the goodbye post](https://blog.darklang.com/goodbye-dark-inc-welcome-darklang-inc/) and [the overdue status update](https://blog.darklang.com/an-overdue-status-update/) — opening them gives you the only complete commercial post-mortem the structured-editor movement has produced.

**Jonathan Edwards** spent twenty years on Subtext, the research counterpart to Darklang's commerce, and in autumn 2025 wrote his own reckoning, which the agent read at [subtext-lang.org/retrospective.html](https://www.subtext-lang.org/retrospective.html): *"It is fair to say that Subtext was a series of overambitious failed experiments… I kept running into tar pits of interlocking hard problems with high-dimension spaces of solutions."* His diagnosis is not engineering but theory: *"What Subtext needed was a Theory of Change"* — the way languages have lambda calculus and databases have relational algebra, structured editing has no mathematics to steer by. His vision statement asks, of his whole community, *"Are we even a field?"* He is still working — a system called Baseline, with Tomas Petricek; the journal asked for major revisions in late 2025. The retrospective is short and unsparing; opening it gives you the research movement's own account of why it stalled, from its most persistent member.

**Unison** — the language that stores code in a database rather than files, whose site says *"Other tools try to recover structure from text; Unison stores code in a database"* — shipped 1.0 in November 2025, eight years in. Three months later its newest blog post announced a consulting group, in words the agent quotes verbatim: *"We aim to be cash flow positive as a business in the next 12 months and could use your help in getting there."* Not a wind-down — but the product alone does not fund the company. And **feenk**, whose Glamorous Toolkit is the aesthetic's sharpest living statement (*"Objects, not text"*), runs on the same model: hands-on consulting, near-daily releases, and a repositioning that turns the AI era into their argument rather than their obituary: *"Now that AI can generate code ever faster, it becomes impossible to ignore that the real bottleneck is making sense of systems."*

The pattern, which the agent that gathered these states as its own conclusion: three independent structure-editor ventures converged on the same funding answer within a year — consulting, consulting, insolvency — and the one commercial post-mortem names the structured editor itself as the thing LLMs invalidated. Hold that. The next encounters keep returning to it from unexpected sides.

## II. The man who bet against the computer

Bret Victor's Dynamicland is the deepest opposition the sweep found to any project that would make knowledge richer for machines — not "structure is hard" but structure is *wrong*. The adversarial agent, hunting for the strongest case against the structure thesis, ranked Dynamicland's first: their stated design principle is *"to maximize agency, minimize what the computer knows,"* and their objection to AI is that *"the 'smarter' the product, the less the user needs to understand"* — outsourcing understanding, they call it, "in its most virulent form." Their goal is a system *"fully visible and understandable top-to-bottom."* A rich typed substrate that programs consume and humans mostly don't read is, on this view, another opacity — not a cure for one.

The status report is poignant: dynamicland.org's newest linked material is from 2024, the progress reports stop at 2022, the archive page says of itself *"This is only complete through May 2016,"* and the lab's Mastodon has four posts. But Victor's own page still reads, in the present tense: *"I am (now and forever) making Dynamicland. Currently, we are building a dynamic library."* Whether anything has moved inside is one of the sweep's honestly-marked unknowns — absence of public posts is not proof of inactivity. The [2024 FAQ](https://dynamicland.org/2024/FAQ/) is where the philosophy is stated at full strength; opening it gives you the one first-principles attack that a structured-knowledge project has to be able to answer, from the person who has run the counter-experiment for a decade.

Notice what just happened across two encounters: Darklang died because its structure was opaque to the new machine readers, and Dynamicland objects to structure because it is opaque to human readers. The same charge, from opposite shores. Geoffrey Litt — whom you are about to meet — put the synthesis in a 2026 post title the adversarial agent flagged: *"Understanding is the new bottleneck."*

## III. The lab in the middle

Ink & Switch turned ten in April 2026, and the sweep keeps arriving at it from different directions — the tools-for-thought agent, the local-first agent, the who-else-argues-this agent — because it sits at the centre of this whole territory: the lab that holds both Victor's demand for human agency and the structuralists' demand for a real substrate, and ships prototypes instead of positions.

Its manifesto is the [Malleable Software essay](https://www.inkandswitch.com/essay/malleable-software/) (Litt, Horowitz, van Hardenberg, Matthews, June 2025) — the fullest published version of the argument that applications are enclosures: apps as "avocado slicers," and *"When data is instead shared among applications, it empowers end-users to compose tools in more flexible ways."* On AI it is deliberately cool: *"AI code generation alone does not address all the barriers to malleability"* — "like bringing a talented sous chef to a food court." Opening it gives you the app-enclosure argument made completely, with its historical lineage (OpenDoc, Smalltalk, the filesystem) — the closest thing in the wild to one half of an argument this project makes, made by people who stop before the other half.

Its working system is **Patchwork** — which one agent flags as, almost word for word, a shipped version of an interface model this project holds: tools declare which datatypes they can render, documents can pin a tool, and composition is a query over the registry. It began as version control for writers and became a malleable-software runtime, and its 2026 notebook entries carry two lines the sweep singles out. From the *Progressions* note: Patchwork *"made it relatively easy to compose tools in ways their authors didn't expect."* And from chee's *chitter chatter* note, the sentence that could serve as this whole lineage's epigraph: *"the closer we can make the loop, the less the distinction between what is written and what runs, the better it gets."* The [Patchwork notebook](https://www.inkandswitch.com/patchwork/notebook/) is a door worth opening slowly — six 2026 entries, each short, each a lab note from people building the thing rather than arguing it.

Two departures mark the era's mood. Geoffrey Litt, the malleable-software author, now works at Notion — the research argument's most visible mind, employed by the most successful block-and-database monolith. And Andy Matuschak, the field's most prominent tools-for-thought researcher, went on "para-academic leave" in late 2025 to build Pico, *"a conservatory for human attention"* — the person who spent a decade on note systems is no longer building note tools. His parting analysis, [*Apps and programming: two accidental tyrannies*](https://andymatuschak.org/tat), holds the field's sharpest one-liner about the new machines: *"Coding agents without a composable architecture give you zero-to-one silo apps."* Opening it gives you the argument that generation without composability just rebuilds the enclosure faster.

## IV. The mathematics that said no

Inside Ink & Switch's decade sits a smaller story with a harder ending, and it matters more to this project than any other single encounter: **Cambria**, the 2020 attempt to solve schema evolution — how typed, versioned data survives its own types changing — with bidirectional lenses. The lab lists it as Completed, 2020. The repo's last push was mid-2024. No successor exists anywhere; the schema-evolution agent, sent to find one, reports that every shipping sync engine solves migration by forcing clients to upgrade — Zero's default handler *reloads the page*.

The [Cambria essay](https://www.inkandswitch.com/cambria/) is unusually honest, and the deep-read agent quarried it: the team's first architecture — translate on write — is confessed as *"a flawed strategy… too much translation work was happening eagerly on write,"* and their pivot to schema-tagged writes plus translate-on-read is, the agent notes, structurally identical to what Avro has done in production for eighteen years. Their flagship `convert` operator *"can't guarantee a useful consistency relation"* — it is not, technically, a lens. Performance *"has not yet been measured… in any formal way."* And they *"found it too unstable to be our only system of record during the project."* Opening the essay gives you the most candid account in existence of what happens when the lens idea meets real data — including an appendix that enumerates six ways to convert a scalar to an array and concludes *"there is no ideal solution."*

Behind Cambria stands the mathematics, and the mathematics had already answered. Foster, Pierce and colleagues' TOPLAS 2007 lens paper — read by the agent at [the primary PDF](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~bcpierce/papers/lenses-toplas-final.pdf) — states that the composability law you would need for safely chained migrations (PutPut) is dropped deliberately, because the useful operators *"fail to satisfy it for reasons that seem pragmatically unavoidable."* The agent's summary is the load-bearing sentence: lenses did not solve the view-update problem; they gave it a type system. The research field itself has gone quiet — the Bx workshop shows no proceedings after 2022 on dblp, and one of its own late papers observes that *"it sometimes seems that every paper with 'lens' in its title defines at least one new type of lens."*

Meanwhile, the systems that actually survived schema change all did the same thing, and it is the opposite of translation. Protobuf forbids reusing field numbers — the documented consequences of reuse are *"A parse/merge error (best case scenario), Leaked PII/SPII, Data corruption"* — and removed `required` from the language entirely, the canonical lesson that a constraint you can never relax is a liability that compounds. Datomic: *"You can never alter `:db/valueType`."* ATProto's federation rule, written for a network where nobody can be forced to upgrade: *"Types can not change. Fields can not be renamed"* — breaking changes must take a new name. The agent's verdict, earned across all of them: **nobody merges structure; they serialize it.** The one live counter-current is Jazz v2's idea of treating schema version as one more branch dimension, which the sweep grades announced-alpha — the most promising unproven idea in the field, from a tiny unfunded team in Canterbury.

## V. The note-takers' evacuation

While the researchers worked, a whole commercial generation built tools for personal knowledge — and in 2025–26 that generation dispersed, in a pattern the products agent documents ruthlessly.

Logseq, the outliner that abandoned plain files for a database, finally shipped the rewrite in July 2026 after a thirteen-month release hole — into a market that had just re-valued the thing it gave up. The top Hacker News comment: *"too little, too late."* Long-time users, in the thread, name their reason for leaving: agents can no longer read the files. This is the strangest twist in the whole sweep, and several agents converge on it independently: **plain Markdown became a moat in 2026 — because coding agents can read it.** The medium the structure movement spent twenty years trying to escape was handed a second life by the machines everyone assumed would transcend it. Obsidian — files, plus typed views *over* files — is the category's default winner, and its AI story is a CLI so agents can reach the vault, not an assistant inside the app.

Around that centre, the venture-funded middle evacuated. Tana split itself in two, the money following an agentic meeting product while the outliner goes to maintenance. Mem is now "your AI chief of staff." Notion killed Notion Mail for "agent-based email workflows." Kosmik's site says *"Kosmik will sunset on May 31st 2026"* after eight years. Dendron is dead — users still filing issues into 2026 with nobody home. Roam, which once defined the category, is a functional zombie: alive, unmarketed, no funding news since 2020. And the era's best-read self-critique — Joan Westenberg's *"I deleted my second brain,"* 598 points on HN — now 404s at its original URL, which the agent records, dryly, as itself a link-rot data point.

What is arriving to replace them says the quiet part in its tagline: Hubble, the best-received 2026 entrant, is *"an open-source notetaking app for you and your agents."* The audience of the personal knowledge tool is no longer only the person.

## VI. The writers

Now go back fifty years and meet the people who did this for a living before software ate it: the technical writers, whose story the sweep tells in three generations and one bankruptcy.

The founding experiment is John Carroll's **Minimal Manual** (1987), and the agent that pulled the primary PDF found it better than its reputation: real randomized experiments — nineteen temp-agency office workers in the first, *"the MM subjects required 40% less learning time… t(17) = 3.06, p < .01"* — the only genuinely causal evidence in the entire documentation literature that document design changes what readers can do. It replicated in the early 1990s, most notably at the hands of its own sternest critic, Hans van der Meij, who had written in 1992 that *"it is not yet settled that a minimalist approach is the most effective one because critical experiments have hardly been conducted"* — and then ran the experiments and confirmed the effect (d ≈ 1.0). His and Carroll's *Ten Misconceptions* reply carries the line every skimmer of minimalism should be made to read: misconception number one is that minimalism means brevity, and *"Wantonly slashing text and leaving other design characteristics unchanged will not lead to a minimalist design… if it really did produce better instruction, it would be the mother of all panaceas."* Then the line simply stops. A 2021 review searched six technical-communication journals for 2014–2019 minimalism research and *"came out empty-handed."* Van der Meij himself moved to instructional video. The best empirical program the field ever had was replicated, absorbed, and abandoned — total N across the meta-analysis, which was itself never peer-reviewed: 288 people, learning 1980s word processors.

The living heir is **Diátaxis** — Daniele Procida's four-quadrant framework, the most-adopted documentation theory of the era — and the sweep's encounter with it yields two findings that invert its public image. First, Procida himself disclaims the reading most adopters act on: *"Diátaxis is not a scheme into which documentation must be placed — four boxes,"* and a clean four-way file split is *"a typical outcome of the good practice, not its end."* The best failure report agrees from experience: Kayce Basques, technical writer on Google's Pigweed, on the record — *"We tried adopting Diataxis on pigweed.dev as a literal blueprint and it resulted in too much fragmentation… Users and teammates found it annoying to have to jump back-and-forth so much."* They retreated to sections within pages. Second, the framework moved in 2026 *by subtraction*: Procida deleted the site's only page on complex hierarchies — *"Ugh, I don't like that page and I have actually deleted it… There is a real problem there, and that page doesn't do a good enough job of dealing with it. I have something cooking that is much, much better"* — and as of the sweep's fetch, nothing has replaced it. The one page addressing what happens when the four boxes meet a real multi-axis corpus is a hole with a promise in it. (The framework cites no research, reports no metrics anywhere in five years of Canonical-scale adoption, and its quality page explicitly rejects measurement; the sweep grades it widely-adopted practice, opinion as assessment.)

And the institution: the **Society for Technical Communication** — the profession's body since the 1950s, 25,000 members at its peak — ceased operations in January 2025 and filed Chapter 7, membership down to about 4,500. DITA 2.0, the structured-authoring standard, has been in beta since 2021; the sweep measured its repo and found the specification has, empirically, **one committer**. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics projects 1% growth for technical writers over the decade and names generative AI as a demand limiter. The profession that spent forty years learning how to structure knowledge for readers is dissolving at the exact moment its problem became everyone's problem.

## VII. The tracers

Threaded through every generation above is a rarer figure: the person who chases a famous number to its source and finds nothing there. The sweep employed several of these hunts, and their trophies rearrange what a whole field believes about itself.

The masterpiece of the genre is Laurent Bossavit's chase of the **1:10:100 defect-cost ratio** attributed to the "IBM Systems Sciences Institute" — the number underneath every argument that understanding late costs a hundred times more. The organisational-knowledge agent confirms the chase at [the Morendil gist](https://gist.github.com/Morendil/ebfa32d10528af04e2ccb8995e3cb4a7): the Institute was an IBM customer-education unit in Los Angeles, roughly 1967–1982 — a training programme, not a research body. No study, no data, no N. The agent's verdict: *cite nothing to it, ever.* And the confession that seals it comes from the curve's own author: Boehm and Basili, 2001, in a paper the agent read at the source, quietly added the word "often" and wrote that for small non-critical systems the factor is *"more like 5:1 than 100:1."* The 171-project study that tested the delayed-issue effect directly (Menzies et al., 2016) found *"no evidence"* for it at all.

The same shape, everywhere the tracers looked. Jakob Nielsen's card-sorting sample-size numbers — r = 0.90 at 15 users, quoted by every UX tool for twenty years — *appear nowhere in the paper they cite*; the agent pulled Tullis & Wood 2004 from the Wayback Machine and found no table of coefficients, only a graph Nielsen read values off. Robert Martin's "developers spend 10x more time reading than writing" traces, in *Clean Code*'s own preceding paragraphs, to Martin watching Emacs session playbacks in the 80s and noticing *"the vast majority of the playback was scrolling"* — no instrumentation, no N, and the observation is about navigation, not reading. The "50% of maintenance is comprehension" claim rests on Fjeldstad & Hamlen 1983, a report the agent proved is cited-but-unread: its two canonical citers give mutually incompatible bibliographic records for it — the signature of a reference copied between papers rather than consulted — and the report itself is unretrievable, so nobody can confirm it contains the figure at all. Dunbar's number: the 2021 deconstruction's actual 95% confidence intervals run 4 to 520 — the paper's own conclusion being that *"specifying any one number is futile."* Miller's 7±2, in Miller's own 1956 words: *"I suspect that it is only a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence."*

Two reflexive catches complete the picture, because the sweep caught the same disease in its own instruments. One agent's automated summariser **fabricated** a sample size for Szulanski's stickiness study — "N=22, 10 companies," against the paper's real 271 questionnaires across 122 transfers in 8 firms — and invented verbatim quotes for another paper; the agent caught it only by extracting the primary PDFs itself. And the most-cited number in the AI-context world — Anthropic's "150,000 tokens to 2,000, a 98.7% saving" — turns out, on the adversarial agent's read of the actual post, to be *an illustrative hypothetical*, a worked example with no methodology, now circulating as a measurement. The mechanism it illustrates is independently supported at one to two orders of magnitude smaller effect. The tracer's lesson generalises: in this territory, the more perfectly a number fits the argument, the more likely nobody ever measured it.

## VIII. What the measurers found instead

So what happens when someone actually measures? The sweep's most valuable property is that it kept finding the same answer in unconnected fields, and the answer is not "structure works" or "structure fails." It is: **the effect never lives where the artifact's designers put it.**

Medicine ran the cleanest natural experiment. The WHO surgical checklist cut mortality nearly in half in the original eight-hospital study (Haynes 2009, verified from the abstract: 1.5% → 0.8%, p = 0.003). Then Ontario mandated it across 101 hospitals and got a null (Urbach 2014: over 200,000 procedures, no significant effect). Then the only randomized trial (Haugen 2015, stepped-wedge) found it works — where introduced as an engaged programme. The agent's synthesis: *the artifact does not carry the effect; the implementation does.* Aviation says the same thing from the other side: the founding checklist-design study (Degani & Wiener, NASA, 1990 — read from the primary PDF: 42 crews observed, zero experiments) produced sixteen guidelines that are pure expert observation, and thirty-five years later there is still no comparative performance evidence for the field's most basic design choice; the NTSB is on record *asking* whether any method produces better performance.

The professional-reader studies converge on where the failure actually is. Practising physicians overwhelmingly preferred a layered guideline format over narrative — 72% versus 16% — while their comprehension moved non-significantly (Brandt 2017, the only randomized format trial in practising clinicians, verified from the open-access text): preference is not comprehension. The think-aloud study of clinicians reading a real guideline found they could understand everything they found — the failure was *finding the applicable recommendation*. Grol's 61 GPs across 12,880 real decisions followed vague recommendations 36% of the time and clear ones 67% — observational and confounded, but the largest professional-population signal in the sweep, and it is about specificity, not structure. And the lawyers: Martínez, Mollica and Gibson's preregistered PNAS 2023 experiments — 105 practising lawyers, full text read by the agent — found that legal training raises the whole curve but does **not** buffer against bad prose (no register-by-training interaction), and that lawyers rated simplified contracts *equally enforceable and higher quality*. Expertise is a main effect, not a shield; and the "expertise reversal" design rule — strip guidance for expert readers — turns out, in the one meta-analysis with a moderator table (Tetzlaff 2025, open access, read at source), to rest on a literature containing **zero studies of practising professionals**.

Design rationale, the field that spent the 1990s building structured why-capture: its entire quantitative case for benefit is one 17-subject experiment that succeeded on one of two systems, plus Karsenty's six professional designers at Aerospatiale, whose professionally-scribed rationale document answered **41%** of the why-questions they actually asked. Karsenty's own deflationary close: *"it is not because such documents… are available that they will be consulted, even if they contain information needed by designers!"* The modern echo is exact: of ADRs mined at scale, roughly 51% are written once and never touched again, and the ICSA 2026 impact study of 921 repositories found *"at most, modest observable effects"* — with 63% of ADRs *born accepted*, skipping the deliberation they exist to record. Fifteen years of a practice, one study that looked for an effect, and the effect it found is a shrug. (The single positive result anywhere in that lane is a 2026 n=1 preprint in which ADRs served *to an LLM* cut development time 10% — and identical instructions carried as rationale produced end-to-end tests where the same instructions in a config file produced none. Unreplicated; cite as a hypothesis. But notice whom the rationale finally helped.)

The organisational layer agrees. Szulanski's 122 best-practice transfers — the real figures, after the fabricated ones were caught — found the barriers to knowledge moving are not motivational but structural: the receiver's absorptive capacity first, causal ambiguity second, the arduousness of the relationship third. Microsoft's onboarding study found mentorship outranking documentation in developers' own accounts. Wohlrab's six automotive companies found the one honest predictor of whether an artifact stays current: *prescriptive* artifacts — ones something downstream consumes — get maintained; descriptive ones rot. And DORA, the industry's flagship survey, ran the full arc in public: documentation quality 2.4× (2021), then *negatively* associated (2022, "at odds with previous reports"), then "No effect" two years running, then the construct quietly dropped in 2025 — with the 2025 report explicitly retreating from the word "effects" altogether.

And then the newest readers arrived and re-ran the whole pattern in eighteen months. AGENTS.md files — adopted by tens of thousands of repositories on vendor advice — *"do not generally improve task success rates, while increasing inference cost by over 20% on average"* in the strongest controlled study ([the ETH Zürich evaluation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11988), a preprint, but the agent verified its claims verbatim and a second independent ablation agrees on the null). What *does* help an agent: navigational pointers — the one positive result isolates the mechanism as getting the agent to the right files, with patch quality flat. What retrieval research found in parallel: prepending a document's own heading hierarchy to each chunk lifted retrieval nearly 24% (single-author preprint, replication package published), while natural-language summaries of code answered 4 of 45 behavioural questions where the source answered 27 — a property of the representation, not the summariser, since a frontier model's summaries scored exactly as poorly as a 3B model's. And Chroma's Context Rot report — 18 models, the sweep's most under-cited finding — showed models scoring *better on shuffled haystacks than on logically coherent ones*. [The report](https://www.trychroma.com/research/context-rot) is worth reading in its own charts; opening it gives you the empirical case that composition, not length, is what degrades a context — and a caution against assuming a well-organised document is automatically a well-retrieved one.

Across surgery, aviation, law, engineering and now machines, the measured story is one story. The artifact is never the lever. Navigation, specificity, implementation, and whether anything downstream consumes the writing — those are the levers. Every field discovered this separately, and none of them, on the sweep's evidence, has read the others.

## IX. The industrialists

There is one more class of witness: the teams who ran these ideas at scale, with money, and wrote down what it cost. Their reports are engineering blogs, not papers — but they are the only people in this walk who operated typed contracts under real load, and their convergences are the closest thing the territory has to laws.

Five companies independently built server-driven UI — the interface as data over typed contracts — and independently hit the same wall: a client you cannot force to upgrade. Plaid's spec says it flatly: adding a new pane kind is a major version, *"which require[s] a client SDK upgrade."* All five converged on the same fix, capability negotiation, and none solved shipping a new component to an old client. DoorDash's report carries the finding that contradicts the obvious design: *"Fallback components were unnecessary… when we version components, we typically have a very specific, new design in mind."* Lyft named its second tier honestly — Semantic Components exist *"as an escape hatch from server-driven land,"* and *"sometimes the dividing line… can be hazy."* The most rigorous attempt was Uber's Screenflow, whose CI *recompiled every screen against every previously shipped runtime version* — the correct answer, costing a compiler, a monorepo, and a build matrix — and whose department was shut down in 2020 with sixteen flows in production. [The Screenflow retrospective](https://artem-tyurin.medium.com/screenflow-an-unfinished-attempt-at-a-cross-platform-server-driven-ui-at-uber-749c1bc1d89) is written by an engineer who was there; opening it gives you the fullest account of what validating every call against every shipped signature actually takes. And the freshest datum cuts both ways: Retool, in June 2026, abandoned its own proprietary visual abstraction for React and TypeScript because the abstraction *"prevented LLMs from working fluently"* — which the analysing agent reads precisely: not a verdict against deriving UI from typed contracts, but against *closed, non-inspectable* derivation. The contract survived as the moat; the opaque runtime died.

Google's Zanzibar — the authorization system behind Drive and Docs — is the costed experiment on transitivity. [The USENIX paper](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/atc19-pang.pdf), in the authors' own words: *"Recursive pointer chasing during check evaluation has difficulty maintaining low latency with groups that are deeply nested or have a large number of child groups."* Their fix, Leopard, denormalises the transitive closure at the price that one tuple write *"may yield potentially tens of thousands of discrete Leopard tuple events."* The clone ecosystem spent five years imposing arbitrary depth limits and is now abandoning them as the wrong metric — OpenFGA, July 2026: *"A shallow recursive graph can be more resource-intensive than a deep linear one."* Fan-out, not depth, is the cost. And the era's twist: latent transitive permissions were harmless while discovery was manual; Microsoft's own docs now concede, of Copilot oversharing, that their mitigation *"is not intended or scalable for long-term use"* and *"isn't a security boundary"* — the sweep's phrasing: the AI didn't create the leak, it enumerated it. Google Drive's answer was to *remove* the ability to narrow access below a parent folder. The industry is being dragged, product by product, toward flat, one-hop permission — with, as the agent notes, no citable advocate ever having argued for it. The practice exists; the argument is unclaimed.

The knowledge-representation ledger reads the same way. Wikidata — the largest collaborative typed knowledge base — was measured carrying about 2.39 million classes simultaneously marked first- and second-order, root cause *"editor misunderstandings of instance-of and subclass-of"*: the transitive hierarchy is the part that rots under many hands. SKOS, the most successful W3C vocabulary, made its hierarchy non-transitive *on purpose* in 2009. Schema.org's own 2026 usage data: of 958 types, twelve reach 10M+ domains and 77% sit below a thousand — the open web converged on a dozen types no matter how many were offered. And Cyc, the forty-year hand-built ontology, spent by the account the agent read [an estimated $200M and 2,000 person-years](https://yuxi.ml/essays/posts/cyc/) and answered global consistency with microtheories — which proliferated rather than consolidated. Opening that essay gives you the deepest cautionary tale about owner-scoped contexts: they are the right fence, and they multiply.

One last industrial fate, almost a fable. Datalog — the recurring dream of a clean logical query surface — was dropped by XTDB so quietly the website no longer mentions it; died with Cozo and DDlog; and was replaced inside Rust's borrow-checker after the Datalog formulation proved *"a non-starter"* on performance — [the Rust team's own post](https://blog.rust-lang.org/2026/08/04/enabling-polonius-alpha-on-nightly/) is the closest thing to a credible practitioner post-mortem the paradigm has. The agent's epitaph: Datalog wins as an internal representation and loses as a surface. Every idea in this section has a version of that epitaph. The structure survives; the structure's *visibility* is what keeps dying.

## X. The ones arriving now

The walk ends at the newest arrivals — the people building, right now, in the eighteen months the sweep covers, the layer this project lives in. Three encounters and one absence.

**Letta** — born as MemGPT, the flagship of the "memory OS" thesis — retired its own server in 2026 (twelve commits in five months where there had been over a thousand, the archive commit dated and verified) and rebuilt as a coding-agent harness whose memory is *"tracked via git"*: the memory company concluded that files under version control were the substrate after all. Around it, every serious memory vendor converged within six weeks on the same three features — background consolidation, cross-harness memory, provenance — while the platform vendors absorbed the layer from above: Anthropic's memory stores now ship immutable versions *"attributed to the session,"* which is provenance-over-the-store as an API primitive. The delta agent's warning travels with this: the flip side is that server-side compaction makes recorded context non-reconstructible — *"reproducibility is being traded away for context economy, deliberately."* Nobody shipping today can say "this completion came from exactly this context, re-derivable." That sentence is the open ground.

**Kendall Clark** — formerly of Stardog, now Pentad Labs — is the one person the who-else-argues-this agent found stating two of this project's positions almost word for word: a fact as a five-slot record whose last two slots are *context* and *lineage*, *"rather than burying context within narrative transcripts,"* so that *"nothing an agent has seen, said, or done is lost."* It is a design-essay series plus a pre-release product, not a shipped substrate — but it is a live mind on the same trail, and [PLRN/020](https://pentad.ai/PLRN/020/) is the door; opening it gives you the closest single statement to this project's context-and-provenance claims that anyone else has published, written by someone who spent a career in knowledge graphs first.

**The pendulum itself** is the third encounter, and it has two faces the sweep insists be held together. The public face: three years of structured extension mechanisms each replaced by something less structured, ending in Agent Skills — folders of Markdown — a story told best by [Sawyer Hood's essay](https://www.sawyerhood.com/blog/llm-extension), whose conclusion is the strongest published case against typed media: *"we will go back to extending our agents with the most accessible programming language: natural language."* Opening it gives you the opposition at full strength, well written, from inside the practice. The measured face, from the adversarial agent's audit: skills adoption is enormous (~53,000 specifications on one marketplace) while the only controlled benchmark found them *lowering* task success at 72–394% more tokens — and MCP, in the same season it was supposedly losing to Markdown, deleted its conversational surface and made its typed layer *stronger*: mandatory discovery, typed cacheable results, richer schemas. The agent's synthesis, the most useful sentence the sweep produced: text won the payload; it did not win the substrate. **Type the edges — identity, permission, provenance, freshness, address — keep the vocabulary tiny and declared, and leave the content as prose.** Every winning 2026 system it audited fits that sentence; every loser typed the body or induced the types.

And the absence. Two of them, actually, and they are this walk's real destination. The who-else agent, after mapping every neighbour: the specific historical argument — Unix enclosed by applications, the web enclosed by platforms, AI now being enclosed by harnesses, each medium losing its composable whole the same way — *is claimed by nobody it could find*; its most confident absence, with the honest caveat that its index was HN-shaped. And the adversarial agent, after auditing forty papers: *the crux experiment does not exist* — no one has run LLM-assisted, human-declared structure head-to-head against a well-written prose document, same corpus, same frontier reader. Every study it found beat extracted structure or beat no structure; none faced good prose. The one experiment the whole argument turns on is unrun.

That is where the sweep leaves you: among neighbours who each hold a piece — Patchwork the interface, Pentad the provenance, Jazz the branching schema, SKOS the humble hierarchy, the industrialists the costs, the measurers the mechanisms, the tracers the hygiene — and a field whose best-attested regularities are that stories carry, that navigation is the failure mode, that prescriptive writing survives and descriptive writing rots, and that the decisive experiment and the unifying argument are both, as of August 2026, unclaimed. The shore you depart from is not crowded.

## Doors left standing

The doors already offered above are the ones this walk leans on. A few more stand at its edges, unopened, for later sessions — each one sentence on what opening it gives:

- [Formality Considered Harmful](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1008716330212) (Shipman & Marshall, 1999; full text archived [here](http://web.archive.org/web/20011126150107id_/http://www.csdl.tamu.edu:80/~shipman/cscw.pdf)) — the 27-year-old essay that every capture-cost finding in this walk keeps re-deriving, including the observation that design students could not *produce* structured argumentation even when videotapes showed their natural discussion already followed it; the sweep grades it experience-essay, not measurement, and it has never been refuted.

- [The Martínez lawyer experiments](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2302672120) (free full text via PMC10266064) — the best-designed professional-reader study in the entire sweep; opening it gives you the methods behind "expertise is a main effect, not a shield," preregistered and read at source by the agent.

- [Degani & Wiener's checklist study](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19910017830/downloads/19910017830.pdf) — Appendix A is the sixteen guidelines, including the explicit ruling that criticality beats logical ordering when they conflict; observational, unreplicated, and still the founding document of a whole safety discipline.

- [The Tetzlaff expertise-reversal meta-analysis](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2026/34113/pdf/Learn_and_Instr_2025_Tetzlaff_u.a._A_cornerstone_of_adaptivity.pdf) (open access) — the moderator table proving no professional samples exist is the door; opening it retires "strip guidance for experts" as a design rule with the primary evidence in hand.

- [Nick Cameron on Rust RFCs](https://www.ncameron.org/blog/the-problem-with-rfcs/) — the only critique of a heavyweight proposal process that comes with numbers, from a former core-team member; the sweep's own 2026 counts (215 open RFCs, median age 905 days) show everything he named got worse, and that every project that "fixed" its process did so by shrinking what the process must decide.

- [The Cyc essay](https://yuxi.ml/essays/posts/cyc/) — offered above for the microtheory lesson; it also carries the fullest independent history of the one project that tried to hand-build the whole web of knowledge, which makes it the longest-run baseline any knowledge project can compare itself against.

- [Jai Juneja's "Why AI company brains fail"](https://www.minimumviablefounder.com/p/why-ai-company-brains-fail) — the economics of structure from a practitioner: the three-orders-of-magnitude up-front cost of graph indexing, and entity resolution named as the unsolved problem that turns extracted structure into confidently wrong structure; opinion-grade, and the sharpest version of the objection this project's *declared*-not-inferred stance exists to answer.
