# The name of the unit

*Working record of the search for the knowledge unit's name, 2026-09-01. "Fold" is condemned but stays in use until a successor is ruled; the join's name — link — is settled and not part of this search. Method: dialogue with the author, then four parallel blind sweeps (document tradition, nature, etymology, crafts), each briefed with the spec and the rejection list, verified against real sources.*

## What the name must carry

Less than it once seemed. Two rulings narrowed the search: the whole-that-is-part nature belongs to the holon idea at the chunk layer, and the why-and-confidence duty is carried by the code's two laws. So the name only has to say: **a self-standing piece of understanding, readable alone — essence at its surface, detail kept within.** It must be a folk word (or teachable in one sentence), and a logical mind who knows the word's real meaning must find the usage apt — never strained. That last test is what killed fold: paper is linear, a crease is not a space, and the picture misleads.

## The live candidates

- **Passage.** Folk, zero teaching. Two traditions behind it: the everyday text sense (a bounded, quotable stretch), and the sea sense — a planned voyage, berth to berth, complete in itself yet composing the longer journey. Passed-*through* honestly names that reading is linear while it happens — the author counts this a virtue, not a flaw. Its one shadow: "a passage from the book" feels like an excerpt, a dependent fragment.

- **Ground.** The author's own candidate. The folk idiom is the exact picture: you cover ground, then move to new ground. "Grounds" already means reasons — "on what grounds?" — so the why lives in the word. And the code already says "each piece becomes ground for the next" and "the ground stays true": naming the unit ground makes the unit and what-you-stand-on one concept, which is the compounding thesis itself. Weaknesses: "write a ground" is awkward as a countable noun, and the word says nothing of surface and depth.

- **Brief.** Latin *brevis* (short) → *breve*, an official short writing that stands for a larger matter: the papal brief, the legal brief — a whole case compressed for someone who must act on it. The living verb is the point: *to brief someone* is to hand them, quickly, the understanding they need to act — compressed hand-over in one word. Its flaw: shortness implies things were left out, and the unit keeps everything within.

## The dialog test (author, 2026-09-01)

The word must work in live speech — a workshop, an agent session — the way fold was brutally used: fold that down, fold it in, fold it up. A name that only works on paper fails; the mechanics get navigated out loud, so the name needs a working verb family. The candidates, tested:

- **Brief** has the strongest verb family, and it is exact: *brief me* is handing the essence (reading the surface, in one act of speech), *debrief* is harvesting a finished stretch into the unit — the fold-down act already has a folk verb — and *briefed* names the state of holding it. "Debrief this session into a brief" is a complete instruction today.

- **Ground** has the richest idiom family: *cover ground* (work through it), *break new ground* (open a new unit), *go over that ground again* (re-read), *common ground* (shared understanding), *ground that claim* (give it its why), *on solid ground* (trusted). Weaker at the making act — "ground it" stretches.

- **Passage** speaks the navigation of live work, which is the register a session actually uses while it moves (author's catch): *enter a new passage*, *go back to the previous passage*, *we're in this passage now* — plus *pass over* (skip), *pass through* (read), *pass on* (hand to the next). Still no verb for the making — compressing work into one.

- So the three split by register: **passage** speaks the moving, **ground** the standing, **brief** the handing.

- Noted for whichever word wins: the open-up act already has a universal dialog verb — *unpack* ("let's unpack that") — and capsule's verb *encapsulate* is the one live folk-technical verb for the compress act, even with the noun set aside.

## Weighed and set aside

- **Lemma** — the last surviving word that holds the full structure in one piece: statement at the surface, proof kept within, taken (the Greek root: *the thing taken*) so the next person builds on it without re-reading, nesting at every scale. Set aside because it is non-folk and, worse, means different things in different fields (math's proven result, linguistics' headword). Modern English split its meaning into *takeaway* (the taking), *a given* (the taken-for-granted), and *result / finding / fact* (the settledness) — and none of the fragments kept surface-with-depth.

- **Capsule** — folk and international, a finished measure taken whole or skipped, "time capsule" giving reach-back for free. But it seals its contents — no essence at the surface — and its self-contained-whole meaning leans toward the holon layer, not the knowledge unit.

- **Crystal** — nature's best mechanics: formed by compression without loss ("thoughts crystallized"), interior order expressed at the faces and visible through them, cleaving into smaller crystals of the same shape. The author finds it weird as a name; kept here as the best organic analogy found.

- **Fact / claim** — too specific: they name single assertions, not a piece of understanding. **Finding** — presumes something was searched for and found, which is not always how a unit is made.

- **Holon** — Greek *holos* (whole) + *-on* (part): the one word coined to mean a whole that is also a part, Janus-faced, nested with the same law at every level. Reserved: it fits the chunk exactly (every chunk is a dimension and is placed — whole and part, with no epistemic duty), and renaming chunk → holon is an open ruling of its own.

- Earlier rounds, with their kill reasons: **fold** (wrong picture — linear paper, a crease is not a space) · **room / door** (loose space imagery, chosen for having doors) · **article** (etymology "little joint" — names the connection, not the unit; though its lineage is real: codes of law have been made of articles for two millennia) · **bud** (nature's literal fold — botany calls a bud's leaf-packing folding — but far-fetched as a name) · **logos, tenet** (not folk) · **note, card, entry, topic, piece** (small, bland, or a fragment at root) · **cell, seed, tree** (collisions; a seed must be grown before it gives anything).

## What the sweeps taught, beyond candidates

- **Traditions split unit-naming into segment words and joint words.** Article, Sanskrit *parvan*, Hebrew *perek* name the joint; Sanskrit *kāṇḍa* (the bamboo segment between joints) and Chinese *pian* 篇 (one bound bundle of bamboo slips — still the living word for "a piece of writing") name the segment. Link already owns the joint; the world's best segment words do not naturalize into English.

- **The meaning-atoms almost never co-occur in one word.** Trade words (ingot, coin, bale) compress but cannot nest; document words (brief, digest, tract) stand alone but leak losslessness; only proof and bookkeeping — lemma, and *sum* (the total written at the top of the column, the column kept beneath) — ever held essence-at-surface with depth-retained with nesting, because those are the two human practices that compress finished thought without discarding it.

- **English named this thing once before and ruined the word:** *tabloid* — an 1884 trademark for compressed medicine tablets, transferred within twenty years to condensed news "with all the interesting points put prominently forward."
