The card-sorting sweep landed. It **changes the verdict** on section 4 — there is a negative result I had not found. Corrected and consolidated below.

## 1. CARD SORTING — the evidence exists, and it's largely negative

**Tullis & Wood (2004) primary recovered** (UPA 2004, Minneapolis, 7–11 June 2004; Tom Tullis/Fidelity + Larry Wood/BYU; PDF via web.archive.org, retrieved 2026-08-26). Design: open sort of **46 cards** from Fidelity's usability-dept intranet, run online in WebCAT; 172 employees, 4 dropped → **n=168**. Random subsamples (2…70), 10 each, correlated against the full-168 similarity matrix.

- **The paper contains NO table of correlation coefficients.** Figure 1 is a graph with error bars and no printed values. The only stated recommendation, verbatim: *"it may not be cost effective to spend resources to gather information from more than 20-30 participants."*
- The paper's *other*, near-never-cited table is more damning: mean % of the 17 base clusters' pairs **separated** relative to the full-sample tree — **N=5: 69%, N=10: 50%, N=15: 45%, N=20: 48%, N=40: 35%**. At n=40, a third of clusters still break. *Grade: paper-only, non-peer-reviewed, 128 citations.*

**Nielsen's "15 users / r=0.90" — FOLKLORE.** https://www.nngroup.com/articles/card-sorting-how-many-users-to-test/ (Nielsen, 18 July 2004, unrevised as of 2026-08-26) gives **r = 0.75@5, 0.90@15, ~0.93@20, 0.95@30, 0.98@60**. **These numbers appear nowhere in Tullis & Wood's text — they were read off Figure 1's graph.** Every downstream repetition (Maze, UXtweak, Netizen, hundreds of blogs) treats them as published data. **FLAG AS FOLKLORE.** The supposed Nielsen-vs-Tullis disagreement is also not real: same dataset, different cost threshold — Nielsen concedes 30 gives 0.95 and just argues it isn't worth double the money. NN/g has published nothing new on card-sort sample size in 22 years.

**THE CRITICAL QUESTION — answered, and the answer is worse than "absent":**

- **Schmettow & Sommer, *Behaviour & Information Technology* 35(6):452–470, 2016, doi 10.1080/0144929X.2016.1157207 — NEGATIVE RESULT, the single most important paper in this whole brief.** Card sort n=27 on municipal websites, then n=50 task performance across structures of varying congruence with the elicited mental model. Linear mixed-effects verdict: **"the match between mental model and website structure has no effect on browsing performance."** The authors themselves discuss "the failure to validate card sorting." *Grade: paper-only, peer-reviewed.*
- The only positive: **Ntouvaleti & Katsanos, CHI EA 2022, doi 10.1145/3491101.3519734** — card-sort structure beat the incumbent on first-click success, time, SEQ, SUS. But the comparator was a **legacy IA selected for having known usability problems**, not an expert alternative. **8 citations** as of Aug 2026 — that is the size of this literature.
- Tullis & Wood assert small-sample trees beat "speculation by a designer who is not a potential user." **That claim is untested in their own paper and, as far as anyone can find, anywhere.**

**Reliability problems are documented, not speculative:**
- **Katsanos et al., CHI EA 2019** (doi 10.1145/3290607.3312999; arXiv 1903.08644, 19 Mar 2019), 6 sorts / 140 participants. The abstract says "highly similar"; the **actual Spearman r between distance matrices is 0.419–0.930** (Travel1 vs Travel3 = 0.419), navigation-scheme agreement 63.2%–92.7%. Use the numbers, not the abstract.
- **Cluster-analysis artefacts**: Paea, Katsanos & Bulivou, *IJHCI* 40(2):203–223, 2022 (doi 10.1080/10447318.2022.2112077) and *Interacting with Computers* 2022 (doi 10.1093/iwc/iwac022) — K-means on card-sort data is "too sensitive to initial category centers"; **the IA you get is partly an artefact of algorithm choice**. Follow-up *IJHCI* doi 10.1080/10447318.2026.2646433 (16 Apr 2026). Dendrogram cut-points: Sauro, MeasuringU, 3 Apr 2012 — "notoriously subjective."
- **Closed sorts need far fewer people**: Dougalis & Katsanos, ACM Greek SIGCHI 2025 (doi 10.1145/3749012.3749050), 191 participants → **8–12 suffice**.
- Spencer, *Card Sorting* (Rosenfeld Media, 2009) is method guidance, no original studies. *Opinion/craft.*

## 2. TREE TESTING — one vendor anecdote, one real study

Origin: **Dave O'Brien, "Tree Testing," *Boxes and Arrows*, 5 Dec 2009** (O'Brien disclosed working with Optimal Workshop). Metrics Success/Speed/Directness; ≥30 participants, ~10 tasks. **The entire empirical content is one anecdote: an NZ government site, 31% → 67% success. No control, no replication.** Predecessor: Donna Spencer, "Card-Based Classification Evaluation," *Boxes and Arrows*, 7 Apr 2003 — "10 minutes from 20 users," no controls.

Optimal Workshop's current 101 guide (accessed 2026-08-26) **publishes no benchmark thresholds** — any "80% success is good" figure is **FOLKLORE**; the only published heuristic is O'Brien's "8/10 earns a beer" (2009). NN/g's tree-testing article (Laubheimer, 6 Aug 2023, reviewed 19 Aug 2026) cites no studies and concedes the experience differs from the real design. OpenAlex title search for "tree testing" returns forestry and neuropsychology.

**The one real validity study — read this:** Kuric, Demcak & Krajcovic, "Validation of information architecture: Cross-methodological comparison of tree testing variants and prototype user testing," ***Information and Software Technology* 183:107740, July 2025, doi 10.1016/j.infsof.2025.107740** (CC-BY). **180 participants**, testing whether the tree-testing instrument itself changes navigation behaviour vs prototype testing. Elsevier blocked full-text retrieval. Caveat: authored by a tool vendor (UXtweak Research). *Grade: paper-only.*

## 3. AI card sorting, 2024–2026

- **Sauro, Schiavone & Lewis, MeasuringU, 30 Jan 2024**: ChatGPT vs **200 humans**, 40 Best Buy products. Both produced 5 categories. Item match **63%/77%/65% (mean 68%)**; **kappa .60** across runs (chance 21%). *Paper-only, n=1 study.*
- **Kuric et al., "Card Sorting Simulator," arXiv 2505.09478 (14 May 2025)**: **28 real practitioner studies, 1,399 participants**, four LLMs. Best **NMI 0.73 / ARI 0.48 (Claude 3.5 Sonnet)**; similarity-matrix correlation only **0.42–0.50**; degrades with scale (NMI 0.77→0.65) and label difficulty (0.78→0.64). Notable paradox: **prompting for an aggregate clustering beat simulating individual synthetic participants.** The authors observe simulated sorting is "more analogous to card sorting by experts" than by users — precisely the comparison nobody has validated. *Paper-only, vendor-authored.* NN/g has **no** AI card-sorting content.

## 4–7. The other threads (unchanged, recapped)

- **Facets**: Yee/Swearingen/Li/Hearst, CHI 2003 — 32 students, 35,000 images. **90% preferred faceted; complete-recall 21%→77% (US woodcuts), 57%→81% (French aquatints); empty result sets 82 vs 26**; confidence t(62)=2.18 p<.05, satisfaction t(62)=3.78 p<.001. **But it was 10× slower (0.3s vs 3.7s/step), got +3 minutes, and the baseline was keyword search, not a hierarchy.** Fagan, *ITAL*, June 2010: transfer to real catalogs "inconclusive."
- **Polyhierarchy**: normatively blessed — Z39.19-2005(R2010) §8.3.4 + all-and-some test §8.3.1; SKOS §8.6.9 Ex. 38 ("arises naturally in poly-hierarchical KOS"), `skos:broader` non-transitive §8.6.6. **No user study on whether it helps or hurts findability exists.** The polar bear book's position is explicitly "purist vs pragmatist," and its "if too many items are cross-listed, the hierarchy loses its value" has no threshold and no study — **FOLKLORE**. Measured costs are machine-side only (Google Search Central, updated 18 Dec 2025).
- **Information scent**: Pirolli & Card 1999, *Psych Review*, **1,277 cites**; genuine predictive models (Chi et al. CHI 2001, 419 cites; TOCHI 2003, 116). **IA's strongest empirical thread** — but Bloodhound never shipped, NN/g's article cites one source and zero numbers, and the polar bear book mentions scent once, in a footnote. Breadth/depth: **Larson & Czerwinski, CHI 1998** — medium beat broadest-shallow; neither extreme wins.
- **Folklore**: **"3 clicks" tested and falsified** — Porter, UIE, 16 Apr 2003, 44 users/620 tasks/8,000+ clicks, "no more likelihood of quitting after three clicks than after 12." **"7±2 menus"** misapplies Miller — Nielsen, 6 Dec 2009: menus are recognition, not recall.
- **IA for machines**: llms.txt (Howard, 3 Sep 2024; **v2 10 Aug 2026**) — shipped-niche, adoption self-reported, **no retrieval-quality evidence**. GraphRAG (arXiv:2404.16130, v2 19 Feb 2025) — claims comprehensiveness/diversity gains, LLM-as-judge, no numeric benchmark in abstract. "Semantic layer" (Enterprise Knowledge, 1 Feb 2024) — one case study, **zero measured outcomes**. Arango pivoted his practice to AI (Feb 2025); no 5th polar bear edition, and the 4th (2015) mentions **neither ISO 25964 nor SKOS**.

## Revised bottom line

The chain for IA's core research methods is: a non-peer-reviewed 2004 conference paper with one dataset → a 2004 blog post that **invented the correlation numbers everyone quotes** → a 2003/2009 pair of practitioner blog posts whose entire empirical base is one 31%→67% anecdote → **a 2016 peer-reviewed study that failed to validate card sorting against browsing performance** → one 2022 CHI extended abstract (8 citations) that validates card sorting only against a deliberately broken legacy IA.

**Card sorting and tree testing are shipped-and-widely-adopted methods resting on a paper-only, largely vendor-authored evidence base, and the single most direct validity test returned a null result.** Meanwhile the two things IA *does* have evidence for — information scent and faceted recall — are both narrower than the practice claims: scent never shipped as a tool, and facets won on recall and confidence, not speed, against a keyword baseline rather than a hierarchy.
