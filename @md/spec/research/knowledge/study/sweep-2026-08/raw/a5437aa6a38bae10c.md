## 1. Tullis & Wood (2004) — primary source recovered

Full PDF: `http://web.archive.org/web/20231028060632if_/https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Thomas-Tullis/publication/254164354.../How-Many-Users-Are-Enough-for-a-Card-Sorting-Study.pdf` (retrieved 2026-08-26). UPA 2004, Minneapolis, 7–11 June 2004. Tom Tullis (Fidelity) + Larry Wood (BYU).

Design: open sort of **46 cards** (Fidelity usability dept's intranet), run online in WebCAT; 172 employees, 4 dropped for incomplete data → **n=168**. Random subsamples of size 2, 5, 8, 12, 15, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70 — **10 samples each** — correlated (similarity-matrix correlation) against the full 168 matrix.

**Critical finding: the paper contains no table of correlation coefficients.** Figure 1 is a graph with error bars and no printed values. Recommendation, verbatim: *"it may not be cost effective to spend resources to gather information from more than 20-30 participants."*

The paper's *other* table is more damning than the correlation curve and is almost never cited: mean node-separation of the 17 base clusters. **Mean % of base pairs separated: N=5 69%, N=10 50%, N=15 45%, N=20 48%, N=40 35%.** Even at n=40, a third of the base clusters break relative to the full-sample tree. Grade: **paper-only** (conference proceedings, non-peer-reviewed, 128 citations).

## 2. Nielsen's "15 users" — FOLKLORE flag

nngroup.com/articles/card-sorting-how-many-users-to-test/ (18 July 2004, Jakob Nielsen; still unrevised, accessed 2026-08-26) gives r = **0.75@5, 0.90@15, ~0.93@20, 0.95@30, 0.98@60**. **These exact numbers do not appear in Tullis & Wood's text** — they are read off Figure 1's graph. Every downstream repetition (Maze, UXtweak, Netizen, uxpsychology.substack.com 7 Jun 2022, hundreds of blogs) treats them as published data. **FLAG AS FOLKLORE.**

The Nielsen/Tullis "disagreement" is **not real**: same dataset, different cost threshold. Nielsen explicitly concedes 30 gives 0.95 and argues it isn't "worth twice the money."

NN/g has published nothing new on sample size since. Current topic page (accessed 2026-08-26): newest card-sort items are 2024 — Tankala, "Pushing Users Beyond Terminology Matches" (14 Jun 2024, nngroup.com/articles/card-sorting-terminology-matches/), "Card Sorting vs. Tree Testing" (23 Feb 2024), Laubheimer "Tree Testing" (6 Aug 2023, reviewed 19 Aug 2026). **No AI/LLM card-sorting content at NN/g.**

## 3. Donna Spencer, *Card Sorting* (Rosenfeld Media, 2009)

OpenLibrary confirms: Spencer, *Card Sorting: Designing Usable Categories*, 2009. It is **method guidance, not research** — no original studies, no comparative data. Grade: **opinion/craft**. Her genuinely original contribution is earlier: "Card-Based Classification Evaluation," *Boxes and Arrows*, **7 April 2003** (via wayback) — the technique that became tree testing, developed at the Australian Bureau of Statistics, "10 minutes from 20 users," no controls.

## 4. THE CRITICAL QUESTION — the evidence does not exist

**No published study compares card-sort-derived IA against designer/expert-derived IA on task success.** I searched OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, Crossref, and the citation graph. The nearest things:

- **Ntouvaleti & Katsanos, CHI EA 2022** (doi 10.1145/3491101.3519734, 27 Apr 2022): within-subjects; card-sort structure beat the **existing** structure on first-click success, task time, SEQ, SUS. But the comparator is a legacy IA *selected for having known usability problems* — not an expert alternative. Whole paper has **8 citations** as of Aug 2026; that's the size of this literature.
- **Schmettow & Sommer, *Behaviour & IT* 35(6):452–470, 2016** (doi 10.1080/0144929X.2016.1157207) — **NEGATIVE RESULT, and the most important paper here.** Card sort n=27 on municipal websites, then n=50 task performance across structures of varying congruence with the elicited mental model. Linear mixed-effects: **"the match between mental model and website structure has no effect on browsing performance."** Authors discuss "the failure to validate card sorting."
- Tullis & Wood assert small-sample trees beat "speculation by a designer who is not a potential user." **That claim is untested in their paper and, as far as I can find, anywhere.**

## 5. Tree testing / Treejack

Origin: Dave O'Brien, "Tree Testing," *Boxes and Arrows*, **5 Dec 2009** (wayback; O'Brien disclosed he works with Optimal Workshop; quotes colleague Sam Ng: "analytics for a website you haven't built yet"). Metrics: Success, Speed, Directness. Recommends **≥30 participants, ~10 tasks** (learning effect). **The entire empirical content is one vendor anecdote: an NZ government site, old tree 31% success → new tree 67%.** No control, no replication.

Optimal Workshop's 101 guide (optimalworkshop.com/101-guides/tree-testing-101/results-overview, accessed 2026-08-26) defines success = % landing on correct destination, directness = % choosing without backtracking, and **publishes no benchmark thresholds**. Any "80% is a good success score" figure you see is **FOLKLORE**; the only published heuristic is O'Brien's "8/10 earns a beer" (2009).

**Academic literature on tree testing is essentially nonexistent** — OpenAlex title search for "tree testing" (accessed 2026-08-26) returns forestry and Pyramids-and-Palm-Trees neuropsychology. Closest recent: Soares et al., *IEEE Access* 2025 (doi 10.1109/access.2025.3595447), tree testing applied to an agricultural ontology, 11 tasks, no live-performance validation.

**The one real validity study:** Kuric, Demcak & Krajcovic, "Validation of information architecture: Cross-methodological comparison of tree testing variants and prototype user testing," *Information and Software Technology* **183:107740, July 2025**, doi 10.1016/j.infsof.2025.107740 (CC-BY). Vendor summary (uxtweak.com/research/, accessed 2026-08-26): **180 participants**, testing whether the tree-testing instrument itself changes navigation behavior vs prototype testing. Elsevier blocked full-text retrieval — **read this paper**; it is the only published attempt to link tree-test scores to prototype task performance, and it is authored by a tool vendor (UXtweak Research). Grade: **paper-only**.

## 6. Stability, artefacts, critiques

- **Katsanos et al., "Cross-study Reliability of the Open Card Sorting Method," CHI EA 2019** (doi 10.1145/3290607.3312999; arXiv 1903.08644, 19 Mar 2019). 6 sorts, 140 participants. Abstract says "highly similar." **Actual Spearman r between distance matrices: 0.419 to 0.930** (Travel1 vs Travel3 = 0.419). Navigation-scheme agreement **63.2%–92.7%**. The abstract oversells; low-end instability is substantial. Use the numbers, not the abstract.
- **Cluster-analysis artefacts are documented.** Paea, Katsanos & Bulivou, *IJHCI* 40(2):203–223, 2022 (doi 10.1080/10447318.2022.2112077) and *Interacting with Computers* 2022 (doi 10.1093/iwc/iwac022): K-means results on card-sort data are "too sensitive to initial category centers" — the IA you get is partly an artefact of algorithm choice. Follow-up: "Empirical Comparison of K-Means Initialization Methods for Card Sorting Data Analysis," *IJHCI*, doi 10.1080/10447318.2026.2646433 (16 Apr 2026). Dendrogram cut-points: Sauro, MeasuringU, 3 Apr 2012 (measuringu.com/card-sorting/) — "notoriously subjective, especially when there isn't high agreement between users."
- **Labels-not-content critique**: NN/g Tankala (14 Jun 2024) — participants keyword-match because it's cheaper cognitively. **No data cited; opinion grade.**
- **Card-subset instability**: Kuric et al., arXiv 2509.03232, accepted *IJHCI* Jan 2026 — 160 participants; 60% random card subsets give comparable similarity matrices but **different thematic categories**, and need 25–35 participants.
- **Closed sorts need far fewer people**: Dougalis & Katsanos, ACM Greek SIGCHI 2025 (doi 10.1145/3749012.3749050) — 191 participants, 45 cards, 7 categories, resampled 1→191: **8–12 participants suffice**.

## 7. 2024–2026: AI card sorting

- **Sauro, Schiavone & Lewis, MeasuringU, 30 Jan 2024** (measuringu.com/comparing-chatgpt-to-card-sorting-results/): ChatGPT vs **200 humans**, 40 Best Buy products. Both produced 5 categories. Item match **63% / 77% / 65% (mean 68%)** across three runs; **kappa .60** across runs (chance 21%); per-category overlap 43%–100%. Grade: **paper-only**, single sort, n=1 study.
- **Kuric, Demcak & Krajcovic, "Card Sorting Simulator," arXiv 2505.09478 (14 May 2025)**: **28 real practitioner studies, 1,399 participants**, four LLMs. Best agreement **NMI 0.73 / ARI 0.48 (Claude 3.5 Sonnet)**; Gemini 2.0 Pro 0.72/0.46; GPT-4o and DeepSeek V3 0.68/0.42 and 0.68/0.41; similarity-matrix correlation only **0.42–0.50**. Accuracy degrades with scale (NMI 0.77 low-card-count → 0.65 high) and label difficulty (0.78 simple → 0.64 difficult). **Paradox worth noting: prompting for an aggregate clustering beat simulating individual synthetic participants.** Documented failure modes: duplicate cards, over/undercategorization, asymmetric similarity matrices, infinite looping, silent label rewriting. Authors themselves frame the output as "preliminary feedback," and observe simulated sorting is "more analogous to card sorting by experts" than by users — which is precisely the thing nobody has validated (§4). Grade: **paper-only prototype**, vendor-authored.
- **Optimal Workshop**: Optimal 3.0 and an MCP server (blog, accessed 2026-08-26) — AI *analysis* of your own study data and repository querying, **not synthetic participants**. Grade: **shipped-niche**.

## Bottom line for the spec

The chain is: a non-peer-reviewed 2004 conference paper with one dataset (grade: paper-only) → a 2004 blog post that invented the numbers everyone quotes (FOLKLORE) → a 2003 practitioner blog post and a 2009 vendor blog post with one 31%→67% anecdote that constitute the whole empirical base for tree testing (opinion/shipped-niche) → a 2016 peer-reviewed study that **failed to validate** card sorting against browsing performance → one 2022 CHI extended abstract with 8 citations that validates card sorting only against a deliberately broken legacy IA. **Card sorting and tree testing are shipped-and-widely-adopted methods resting on a paper-only, largely vendor-authored evidence base, with the single most direct validity test returning a null result.**
