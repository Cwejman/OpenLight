# ORGANISATIONAL KNOWLEDGE: DOES INSIGHT TRAVEL?

## 1. HEADLINE

- **Szulanski's core result survives and it is the opposite of the folk story.** Across 122 best-practice transfers in 8 firms, the barriers that dominated were *knowledge-related* — the recipient's lack of absorptive capacity (weight 0.54), causal ambiguity (0.34), and an *arduous relationship* between source and recipient (0.33). Motivation ranked low. Insight fails to travel not mainly because people won't share, but because the receiver can't absorb, the sender can't explain, and the channel between them is painful.
- **The "100x" curve has a signed confession from its own author.** Boehm & Basili (IEEE Computer, 2001) added the word "often" and stated the escalation factor for small, non-critical systems "to be more like 5:1 than 100:1" — and that good architecture flattens it even for large critical systems. The project's motivating cost claim is directionally right and an order of magnitude softer than folklore.
- **Requirements-quality research has been formally audited and found evidentially empty.** In a review of 57 publications, 47.5% of claimed impacts were merely *hypothesized*, 92% of impacted activities were selected ad hoc, and 29.8% of papers claim a quality factor with no stated impact at all. The authors call it "anecdotal or folkloric."
- **The strongest hard evidence in this whole lane is that org structure predicts defects better than any code metric.** On Windows Vista (3,404 binaries, >50M LOC), organisational metrics predicted failure-proneness at 86.2% precision / 84.0% recall, beating code churn, complexity, dependencies, coverage and pre-release bugs. Conway's Law has real teeth.
- **Documentation that is not load-bearing rots — and practitioners know it.** In six automotive companies, artifacts that *generate* something downstream (prescriptive) get maintained; artifacts that merely *describe* do not. This is the single most actionable finding for a documentation-structure project.
- **Transactive memory's headline effect is partly a measurement artifact.** A 2026 meta-analysis (44 studies, 103 effect sizes) found r = .44 overall — but r = .77 with self-report outcomes versus r = .38–.39 with observer ratings or embedded metrics.
- **No credible study shows better documentation *causes* better outcomes.** DORA's documentation findings are cross-sectional, self-reported, and DORA itself hedges to "we can speculate."

## 2. WHAT IS ACTUALLY ESTABLISHED

**Knowledge stickiness (single study, well-designed, never properly replicated).** Szulanski 1996, *Strategic Management Journal* 17: 271 returned questionnaires spanning 122 transfers of best practice in 8 companies, canonical correlation. Findings above. Three honest caveats visible in the paper itself: the headline analysis used **only 87 of the 271 observations** (raised to 142 in a robustness re-run, which preserved the rank order of the top three); canonical correlation "does not provide indications of statistical significance" for individual weights (author's own footnote); and the author states the sample "may be too small to ensure the stability of any but the three largest canonical weights." Grade: **strong single study**, top-three ranking robust, everything below rank three unreliable.
https://josephmahoney.web.illinois.edu/BADM%20545_Spring%202008/Paper/Szulanski%20(1996).pdf

**Absorptive capacity (replicated, but contingent).** Cohen & Levinthal 1990 established that a firm's ability to use external knowledge depends on prior related knowledge — i.e. absorption is a *function of what you already know*, not of how well the sender writes. It has held up in meta-analysis but with large contextual moderation: Stettler et al. 2025 (*J. Product Innovation Management* 42(1):18–47) finds effects on innovation roughly **twice as large in low-tech manufacturing/services as in high-tech industries**, and about twice as large in the smartphone era as pre-internet. A 2023 PLOS One meta-analysis explicitly challenges the assumption that ACAP unconditionally improves firm performance. Grade: **replicated construct, heavily moderated, weak as a universal law.**
https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jpim.12758 · https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0282321

**Conway's Law (real, and stronger than expected).** Nagappan, Murphy & Basili, ICSE 2008. Windows Vista, 3,404 binaries, >50M LOC, eight organisational metrics in a logistic regression, 50 random splits. Precision/recall: **organisational structure 86.2/84.0**; code coverage 83.8/54.4; code complexity 79.3/66.0; code churn 78.6/79.9; dependencies 74.4/69.9; pre-release bugs 73.8/62.9. The authors note engineers did not know they were being studied (limiting reverse-causal behaviour) but concede all data comes from one system. Grade: **strong single-system study, causally suggestive, not replicated at this scale.**
https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/tr-2008-11.pdf

**The mirroring hypothesis (directionally supported, tiny N).** MacCormack, Baldwin & Rusnak, *Research Policy* 2012: five matched product pairs (open-source vs commercial equivalents), measuring "propagation cost" — e.g. 47.1% vs 7.7% in one pair, >41% vs 8.25% in another. The loosely-coupled organisation produced the more modular product in **all five** pairs; the significance claim is a sign test, **p = 0.03125**. The authors' own limitations: only five pairs, no direct test of functional equivalence, and the design "does not disentangle which of these dimensions are most critical." Grade: **suggestive, badly underpowered.** The *inverse* Conway manoeuvre — deliberately restructuring teams to get an architecture — has **no** empirical support I could find; it is an inference from a correlational result.

**Boundary objects (framework + good qualitative evidence, no causal test).** Star & Griesemer 1989 is a historical case study, N=1. Carlile (2002, 2004) added the useful distinction between *syntactic* (different vocabulary), *semantic* (different meanings), and *pragmatic* (different **interests**) boundaries — the last requiring transformation, not translation. The best modern empirical work is Wohlrab et al., *J. Software: Evolution and Process* 2019 — design science across **53 practitioners in six automotive companies**. Two findings matter: (F1) practitioners robustly distinguish *boundary objects* from *locally relevant artifacts*, with ~90% questionnaire agreement (n=31; 94.12% among those with ≥1 year agile experience); (F2) **"Stakeholders' motivation to manage prescriptive models is higher than dealing with descriptive artifacts"** — artifacts that generate code or other artifacts get kept current; artifacts that only describe do not. Artifacts most named as boundary objects: requirements (17/31), architecture models (6), interface descriptions (6). Grade: **framework with solid qualitative support; no controlled test of whether boundary objects improve outcomes.**
https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.12131

**Grounding (theory with lab evidence).** Clark & Brennan 1991 established the *principle of least collaborative effort*: participants minimise **joint** effort, not sender effort — so the cost of producing a perfect utterance often exceeds the cost of collaboratively repairing an imperfect one. Their medium analysis (copresence, cotemporality, simultaneity, reviewability, revisability) predicts the handover problem directly: a document is maximal in reviewability and revisability but **zero in cotemporality and simultaneity**, so grounding failures cannot be repaired in the moment and compound silently. Grade: **theoretical framework with supporting laboratory experiments**, not field-tested on organisational handover.
https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1990s/Clark,%20H.H.%20_%20Brennan,%20S.E.%20_Grounding%20in%20communication_%201991.pdf

**Transactive memory (meta-analysed, measurement-inflated).** Fausett et al. 2026, *Small Group Research*: 44 studies, 103 effect sizes, TMS↔team outcomes **r = .44** [.36, .50] — but moderated by measurement: self-report **r = .77** vs observer ratings **r = .38** vs embedded metrics **r = .39**. A prior meta-analysis (Bachrach et al., 2019) finds TMS binds more strongly to *affective* than performance outcomes. Grade: **replicated but roughly half the headline effect is common-method variance.**
https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10464964261434540

**The cost of late discovery (established directionally, not numerically).** Boehm & Basili 2001, verbatim: *"Finding and fixing a software problem after delivery is often 100 times more expensive than finding and fixing it during the requirements and design phase."* Immediately qualified: *"For this updated list, we have added the word 'often'... One insight shows the cost-escalation factor for small, noncritical software systems to be more like 5:1 than 100:1."* Item 2 — *"Current software projects spend about 40 to 50 percent of their effort on avoidable rework"* — derives from a **behavioural analysis of COCOMO II cost drivers**, i.e. a model, not a measurement. Item 6: peer reviews catch 31–93% of defects, median ~60%. Grade: **the direction is established; every specific multiplier is soft.**
https://www.cs.umd.edu/projects/SoftEng/ESEG/papers/82.78.pdf

**Organisational learning failure (well-theorised, thinly measured).** Vaughan's *normalization of deviance* (Challenger, 1996) is a single deep historical case, later applied by its author on the Columbia board — enormously influential, formally untested. Morrison & Milliken's *organizational silence* (2000) is a conceptual model; reviews note there has been little direct empirical research on the **effects** of silence. Tucker & Edmondson's barriers — first-order problem solving, lack of integration, lack of institutionalisation — are the most operational finding here. Grade: **theoretical frameworks with case evidence.**

## 3. WHAT IS FOLKLORE

**The 1:10:100 / "IBM Systems Sciences Institute" ratio — confirmed folklore.** Laurent Bossavit's chase (the Morendil gist) searched IBM's own site, Google Scholar, and biographies of alleged affiliates. The Systems Science Institute was an **IBM customer/employee education unit in Los Angeles (3550 Wilshire Blvd), operating roughly 1967–1982** — "an educational organization and is a unit of IBM," per Claude W. Burrill. It was **a corporate training programme, not a research body**. No study, no data, no N. The granular chart (1x design / 6.5x coding / 15x testing / 100x maintenance) traces to a textbook footnote. **Verdict: cite nothing to it, ever.**
https://gist.github.com/Morendil/ebfa32d10528af04e2ccb8995e3cb4a7

**"70–85% of rework cost is due to requirements defects" (attributed to Leffingwell 1997) — unsubstantiated.** I chased it and could not reach a primary measurement; it propagates through requirements-management marketing literature. Its sibling **"50–60% of all defects originate in requirements"** is in the same condition.

**And here is the audit that kills the whole family.** Frattini, Montgomery, Fischbach, Mendez, Fucci & Unterkalmsteiner (arXiv 2309.10355) systematically reviewed 57 requirements-quality publications. Verbatim: evidence for impact is *"dominantly hypothesized (19/40 = 47.5%) and rarely either inductive (11/40 = 27.5%) or referenced (10/40 = 25%)... Previous studies have also noted this dominance of anecdotal, non-empirical evidence."* Impacted activities are elicited ad hoc in 92% of cases, *"usually justified by anecdotal or folkloric circumstances."* 29.8% of papers report no impact at all. **Treat any requirements-cost percentage as folklore until someone shows you the N.**

**Standish CHAOS success/failure rates — methodologically invalid.** Jørgensen & Moløkken-Østvold and Eveleens & Verhoef ("The Rise and Fall of the Chaos Report Figures," *IEEE Software*, 2010) show the definitions are one-sided (overruns counted, underruns ignored), sampling solicited failure stories, and study design is undisclosed. **Not usable for benchmarking.**

**"Team Topologies delivers a 25% reduction in context switching and 20% productivity gain."** I chased this and found no study — it circulates on marketing pages with no primary source. Team Topologies is **a framework-shaped assertion**: as one peer-reviewed DevOps taxonomy paper notes, the book "does not present scientific evidence in building their theory." The underlying *cognitive load* construct (Sweller) is well-established in learning psychology; its transfer to team boundary design is untested.

**DORA's "656% lift" from documentation quality.** Cross-sectional, self-reported survey, self-selected respondents. DORA's own 2022 write-up says: *"We can speculate about different things that may be at play... A combination of all of these factors and others may be what is causing such large effects."* **That is not a causal finding.** Reverse causation — high-performing organisations having the slack to write good docs — is untested and entirely plausible. Grade: **consultancy-claim.**

## 4. THE CLIENT↔DELIVERY BOUNDARY

The evidence converges on four things.

**It is a pragmatic boundary, not a vocabulary boundary.** Carlile's distinction is the sharpest available tool: client and vendor differ not merely in *terms* (syntactic) or *meanings* (semantic) but in **interests** — the vendor is paid for delivered scope, the client wants an outcome. Pragmatic boundaries require *transformation* of knowledge, in which both sides give something up. A glossary or a better spec addresses the syntactic layer only. This predicts that documentation improvements will underperform expectations precisely at contractual boundaries.

**"Arduous relationship" is a measured, ranked barrier — third of Szulanski's three.** He glosses it as the relationship serving "as a conduit for knowledge" and affecting "the recipient's ability to acquire knowledge *when needed*." A contract, procurement gate, or ticket queue is an arduousness-generator. Note the asymmetry: Szulanski's data says fixing incentives alone is *"inadequate or misled"* — yet incentive engineering is exactly what contracts do.

**Absorptive capacity is a property of the *client*, and it is not something the vendor can write its way around.** Cohen & Levinthal's central point is that absorption depends on the receiver's prior related knowledge. A client that has outsourced away its technical staff has, by construction, destroyed its own absorptive capacity. This is the most defensible mechanism in the whole lane for the project's named case — and it is a *structural* claim, not a documentation claim.

**Conway/mirroring means the boundary reproduces itself in the artifact.** If mirroring holds (five pairs, p=0.03), a client↔vendor contractual seam becomes an architectural seam — with propagation costs differing by a factor of five or six between loosely- and tightly-coupled organisational forms. The boundary you draw in the org chart is the boundary you will later pay to cross in the code.

The IS-outsourcing literature I could reach was thinner than hoped: mostly critical-success-factor surveys with convenience samples and self-reported success measures. One recurring claim — that **interpersonal** trust matters more than **inter-organisational** trust, with knowledge sharing mediating — is plausible and consistent with Szulanski, but I could not verify its primary N and grade it **unverified**.

## 5. THE CEILING QUESTION — BE HONEST

The evidence bounds a documentation structure more tightly than the project would like.

**Documentation is a real but second-order lever in onboarding.** Rastogi, Thummalapenta, Zimmermann, Nagappan & Czerwonka (ESEM 2015) surveyed **411 professional developers at Microsoft** plus version-control data across eight product groups, outcome = time to first check-in. "Lack of proper documentation" *strongly* increased time to first check-in in 5 of 7 product groups and moderately in the other 2 — the strongest of twelve listed factors, ahead of access/permissions, dependencies, and legacy code. **But** in the open-ended question, where developers named factors freely, the themes ranked by frequency were: **(1) Mentorship, (2) Documentation, (3) Process, (4) Access and permissions, (5) System setup.** Mentorship came first, and developers "who were not assigned mentors experienced that absence of mentor resulted in significant loss of time." Two hard caveats: the paper **obscures its time units for confidentiality**, so there is no absolute effect size; and the factor rankings are *opinion data*, not measured causal effects. Their measured correlations for ramp-up time were "negligible to weak," +0.10 to −0.39. Product group had no significant effect on median time to first check-in.
https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/rastogi-esem-2015.pdf

**Structure beats artifacts, on the best data available.** Organisational metrics out-predicted every code and process metric on Vista. Boehm & Basili's own escape hatch from the 100x curve is *architecture* — "good architectural practices can significantly reduce the cost-escalation factor even for large critical systems... by confining [fixes] to small, well-encapsulated modules." Neither points at documents.

**And the finding a documentation project must sit with: descriptive documentation does not get maintained.** Wohlrab et al.'s F2 is that motivation to maintain **prescriptive** artifacts (which generate code, tests, or other artifacts) is higher than for **descriptive** ones. Their guideline G5 is blunt: *"Produce locally relevant artifacts, especially those for documentation, as late as possible and only when they are actually needed."* Their G2 says boundary objects must be re-evaluated at frequent intervals because "it can happen that boundary objects lose their common identity." Clark & Brennan explain why the ceiling exists at all: a document has no cotemporality and no simultaneity, so the cheap repair loop that makes conversation efficient is unavailable, and the *least collaborative effort* solution is usually to go ask someone.

**Honest verdict:** a documentation structure can plausibly reduce the syntactic and semantic friction at a boundary. There is **no evidence** it can close a pragmatic (interest) boundary, no evidence it substitutes for mentorship, and good evidence that it decays unless something downstream consumes it. The ceiling is set by the receiver's absorptive capacity and by whether the artifact is load-bearing.

## 6. HOW THE CLAIM SHOULD BE RESTATED

The original — *"governance fails where insight cannot travel"* — is unfalsifiable and imports the 100x cost curve by implication. A defensible restatement:

> **Organisational structure is a stronger predictor of software failure than any property of the code itself** (Nagappan et al., Vista, 86% precision). **The dominant barriers to knowledge moving inside an organisation are not motivational but knowledge-related: the receiver's capacity to absorb, the irreducible ambiguity of the knowledge, and the arduousness of the relationship between the parties** (Szulanski, 122 transfers). **Where an organisational boundary is also a boundary of interest — as between a client and a delivery vendor — shared vocabulary is insufficient by construction; such boundaries require transformation of both parties' knowledge, not translation** (Carlile). **Late-discovered misunderstanding is more expensive than early-discovered misunderstanding; the multiplier is context-dependent, spanning roughly 5:1 for small non-critical systems to 100:1 for large ones, and good architecture compresses it** (Boehm & Basili, 2001, in their own words).

Three concessions the project should make explicitly: (a) drop every 1:10:100-family statistic; (b) state that the documentation→outcome link is correlational only and name DORA's own hedge; (c) frame the artifact as *one* mechanism alongside mentorship and relationship design, not as the mechanism.

## 7. WHAT I COULD NOT ESTABLISH

- **Any causal study of documentation.** No RCT, no quasi-experiment, no interrupted time series where documentation was the manipulated variable. This is a genuine gap, and stating it plainly is stronger than citing DORA.
- **A true replication of Szulanski 1996.** Szulanski's own later replication-perspective work (109 transfers, 8 firms) extends rather than independently tests it. Nobody has re-run the 1996 design in a different population.
- **Effect sizes from van Wijk, Jansen & Lyles (2008)** — the most relevant meta-analysis of inter- and intra-organisational knowledge transfer. Paywalled (403); I could not verify k or any coefficient.
- **The Boehm 1981 underlying dataset.** What the original TRW/GTE/IBM projects actually measured, and whether "cost to fix" included requirements churn, remains unverified by me.
- **Any credible primary source for the IS-outsourcing trust findings**, or for the Team Topologies performance numbers.
- **The "171 projects, 2006–2014" study** reported to find no support for early-defect-detection savings — I saw it referenced but could not reach the primary paper to check its N, method, or peer-review status. Treat as unverified.
- **Whether Star's own late critique** ("This is Not a Boundary Object," 2010) says what it is reputed to say — I could not fetch it.

The defensible core is Szulanski (knowledge-related, not motivational, barriers — with "arduous relationship" as the third-ranked barrier, directly implicating contractual boundaries), Cohen & Levinthal (absorption is a property of the *receiver's* prior knowledge — so a client who outsourced its technical staff structurally cannot absorb), Carlile (client↔vendor is a *pragmatic/interest* boundary that vocabulary cannot bridge), and Nagappan (org structure out-predicts every code metric for defects, 86.2% precision on Vista).
