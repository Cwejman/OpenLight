## 1. IETF / RFC Editor Model

**RFC 9280** (RFC Editor Model v3) published **June 2022**, IAB stream ([datatracker.ietf.org/doc/rfc9280](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/rfc9280/)). It split the old RFC Series Editor role: policy *definition* → RSWG (open to all) + RSAB (approval); policy *implementation* → RFC Production Center under IETF LLC. RSAB now: chair Alexis Rossi; Kutscher, Lear, Nottingham, Danyliw; ex-officio Daley, Mahoney ([datatracker.ietf.org/group/rsab/about](https://datatracker.ietf.org/group/rsab/about/), retrieved 2026-08-26).

**2024–26 output — three RFCs in four years**: RFC 9720 "RFC Formats and Versions" (**Jan 2025**, obsoletes RFC 7990; RSAB-approved 2024-09-05 at draft rev -12); RFC 9896 "SVG in RFCs" (**Jan 2026**, approved 2025-09-12); RFC 9920 "RFC Editor Model (Version 3)" (**Feb 2026**, obsoletes RFC 9280; approved 2025-09-29) ([datatracker.ietf.org/stream/editorial](https://datatracker.ietf.org/stream/editorial/)). Grade: **shipped-and-widely-adopted** (it governs the whole series) but throughput is ~0.75 output docs/year.

**The retrospective is the RFC itself.** RFC 9920 §1 states plainly "lessons have been learned about implementing this model" ([rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9920.txt](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9920.txt)). Concrete admissions: *no appeal pathway existed* for editorial-process disputes (§3.2.4, §4.4 add a unified appeal to RSAB); RFC 9280's absolute "once published, RFCs are not changed" was unworkable and is replaced by a reissuance policy. §9 also records why v1/v2 were abandoned: "lack of transparency, lack of avenues for community input into policy definition, and unclear lines of authority." Note the irony: RFC 9920 already has verified errata (2026-08-12, RSWG list). Grade: **paper-only retrospective, shipped fix**.

**Throughput data.** RFC 8963 (**Jan 2021**, [rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8963.html](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8963.html)) — Huitema, n=20 RFCs from 2018: mean **1,200 days** draft→RFC; ~999 days (83%) in WG, ~110 in IESG review, ~104 in production; AUTH48 mean 35 days (range 5–123). Conclusion: "80% of the delay is incurred in WG processing." **Caveat: n=20; the widely-repeated "IETF is 3–4× slower than 1998" line traces here and nowhere else — treat as a single small sample, not a series statistic.** My own count from [rfc-editor.org/rfc-index.txt](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc-index.txt) (retrieved 2026-08-26): RFCs published per year — 2012: 338, 2016: 310, 2020: 209, 2023: 173, 2024: 175, 2025: 208, 2026 (to Aug): 105. Volume roughly halved since 2012–16.

## 2. Rust RFCs — not reformed; routed around

No RFC-process reform RFC has merged since 2016 (search of rust-lang/rfcs PR titles). Instead: **RFC 2904** "major change proposal process for compiler team" (opened 2020-04-09, merged 2020-05-07) created MCPs — an issue in rust-lang/compiler-team, approved by *one* seconding team member + 10-day wait ([forge.rust-lang.org/compiler/proposals-and-stabilization.html](https://forge.rust-lang.org/compiler/proposals-and-stabilization.html)). **RFC 3392** Leadership Council (2023-02-22 → merged 2023-06-15). **RFC 3614** project goals (2024-04-18 → 2024-04-29, 11 days); current instance RFC 3935 "2026 project goals" (merged 2026-04-30).

**Original data, GitHub API, 2026-08-26:**
- rust-lang/rfcs: **4,003 PRs ever, 639 accepted RFCs** in `text/` (~16% acceptance); 1,112 merged PRs, 933 closed-unmerged, **215 open**.
- Open-PR backlog: **median age 905 days**, mean 1,048; 176 >1yr, 89 >3yr, **35 >5yr**; oldest open since 2017-12-21 (#2252).
- Last 59 accepted RFCs (2024-04→2026-08): **median 70 days** open→merged, mean 120, max 666 (#3641).
- 16 RFCs sit in *pending* FCP right now ([rfcbot.rs](https://rfcbot.rs/)) — oldest #3279, open since **2022-06-08**.
- Contrast MCPs: **585 total, 425 accepted, only 17 open**, 185 filed since 2025-01-01. The lightweight process clears; the heavyweight one silts up.

Best published critique: Nick Cameron (nrc, ex-Rust core), **"We need to talk about RFCs", 2022-02-19** ([ncameron.org/blog/the-problem-with-rfcs/](https://www.ncameron.org/blog/the-problem-with-rfcs/)) — one template for all domains, GitHub chronological threads, no follow-up tracking of whether an accepted RFC shipped, incentive to never close stalled discussions; cites "54 currently open RFCs from 2020 or older." Grade: **opinion**, but the only one with numbers, and my 2026 count (215 open, median 905 days) shows it got worse. I found **no** 2024–26 RFC-process retrospective from Matsakis (babysteps feed) or withoutboats (without.boats) — their recent writing is technical. Tomas Sedovic's program-management update (2025-10-14, [blog.rust-lang.org/inside-rust/2025/10/14/program-management-update-2025-09/](https://blog.rust-lang.org/inside-rust/2025/10/14/program-management-update-2025-09/)) mentions a CMU researcher interviewing members about the goals process — no results published yet.

## 3. Oxide RFDs

Source of record: Jessie Frazelle, **2020-07-24**, [oxide.computer/blog/rfd-1-requests-for-discussion](https://oxide.computer/blog/rfd-1-requests-for-discussion). Six states (prediscussion/ideation/discussion/published/committed/abandoned); explicitly "timely rather than polished." **No metrics of any kind are published** — not count, not cycle time, not outcomes. Tooling *was* open-sourced: `oxidecomputer/rfd-site` (created 2024-03-11, 211★) and `oxidecomputer/rfd-api` (2023-07-25, 94★), both actively pushed 2026-08-25/26. But **rfd.shared.oxide.computer/rfd/0001 302-redirects to /login as of 2026-08-26** — the public index shows "Results: 0". Adoption elsewhere: a handful of small repos copying RFD 1 verbatim (`514-labs/moosestack` rfd/0001, `ReysinProject/RFD`, `ar4mirez/samuel`) — no organization has published results. Grade: **shipped-niche**. ⚠️ **FOLKLORE**: the widely-repeated claim that RFDs demonstrably work at Oxide rests entirely on Oxide employees' podcast/blog assertion; there is no published measurement.

## 4. Python PEPs

PEP 1 is current, Steering Council is "final authority," BDFL-Delegate now a deprecated alias for PEP-Delegate ([peps.python.org/pep-0001/](https://peps.python.org/pep-0001/), last modified 2026-08-07). Only substantive 2024–26 amendment listed: **2026-02-02**, optional "Change History" section added. PEP 13 (governance) still Active, unchanged since 2018.

**Original data from [peps.python.org/api/peps.json](https://peps.python.org/api/peps.json) (2026-08-26): 738 PEPs** — Final 374, Rejected 132, Withdrawn 71, Draft 50, Active 39, Deferred 36, Superseded 25, Accepted 11. New PEPs/year: 2022: 25, 2023: 35, 2024: 34, **2025: 45**, 2026 (to Aug): 21. Of the 66 PEPs with machine-parseable resolution dates: median created→resolution **164 days for 2024+ (n=55)**; by resolution year — 2023: 107d, 2024: 122d, 2025: 117d, **2026: 268d (n=19)**. Caveat: the `resolution` header only became common recently, so this is a recency-biased sample.

The real 2023–26 change is **federation, not PEP reform**: PEP 729 Typing Council (resolved 2023-11-20), PEP 731 C API Working Group, PEP 732 Documentation Editorial Board, PEP 772 Packaging Council (created 2025-01-21, resolved **2026-04-16** — 451 days). Python's answer to PEP-process load is delegating decision authority to domain councils. Grade: **shipped-and-widely-adopted**. No published throughput retrospective found.

## 5. Kubernetes KEPs

KEP-0000 (the KEP defining KEPs): `creation-date: 2017-08-22`, `last-updated: **2020-02-18**`, status implemented, authors @calebamiles/@jbeda. Its own Drawbacks section pre-registered the failure modes: "additional process may create community resentment," "potential bottleneck on development velocity," barrier for non-Git and non-English-native contributors. PRR (KEP-1194, @johnbelamaric, created 2019-07-31) layered a production-readiness questionnaire on top.

**Original data — I pulled all 662 `kep.yaml` files from kubernetes/enhancements@master (2026-08-26):** implemented 295, **implementable 277, provisional 66**, withdrawn 10, replaced 6, rejected 3, deferred 1, superseded 1. So **343 KEPs are in a non-terminal state, but only 14 were ever explicitly killed.** Staleness: of those 343, **184 have `latest-milestone` older than v1.32** (nothing shipped since ~late 2024), 209 older than v1.35, and **82 have no milestone at all**. Milestone histogram shows 94 KEPs targeting v1.37 vs 47 at v1.36 — the front of the funnel keeps growing while the back never closes. Repo issues: **301 open / 1,212 closed**. `keps.k8s.io` does not resolve (DNS failure, 2026-08-26).

**"KEP fatigue" is documented but only as unresolved issues, not as an organizational retrospective.** The sharpest primary source: kubernetes/enhancements **#3399**, "Streamline KEP + PRR process for non-API changes," filed by SIG-CLI's Eddie Zaneski **2022-06-16** — "The KEP template has grown to a point where a majority of the sections are primarily focused on API server enhancements and are less relevant to others. This is a pretty negative experience for myself as a maintainer... we plan to eventually add colors to the output of kubectl. We want to do a KEP as a design doc for collaboration but the process seems heavy handed." 22 comments, then **closed as `not_planned` on 2024-12-30** — i.e. it rotted out under the stale-bot after 2.5 years. Compare #2988 "Consider simplifying KEP metadata and process" (2021-09-22 → closed completed 2022-02-19). Grade: **opinion / unresolved**; no SIG-Architecture published retrospective with data exists that I could find. ⚠️ **FOLKLORE**: "KEP fatigue" circulates as a term with no primary measurement behind it — the staleness numbers above are mine, computed today, not something the project publishes.

## Cross-cutting

The only *measured* claim in this whole space is RFC 8963 (n=20, 2018 sample). Everything else billed as evidence is either (a) a process document admitting its predecessor failed — RFC 9920 §1/§9, the strongest genuine retrospective here — or (b) a blog post. Jacob Kaplan-Moss, **2023-12-01**, "RFC processes are a poor fit for most organizations" ([jacobian.org/2023/dec/1/against-rfcs/](https://jacobian.org/2023/dec/1/against-rfcs/)) is the sharpest of the (b) category: RFC processes lack an explicit *decide* step, so they default to "no"; his evidence is two anecdotes (a 150+-engineer employer; an engineer who "would write RFCs so incredibly long that nobody would want to read the whole thing, thus making sure nobody objected"). Grade: **opinion**.

The consistent empirical pattern across all four open-source processes is the same and shows up in the numbers rather than in anyone's retrospective: **the heavyweight process has no exit path.** Rust — 215 open RFCs, median age 905 days, 0 open PRs carry the `postponed` label (100 were postponed historically, all closed). Kubernetes — 343 live KEPs, 14 ever killed, 184 stale. IETF — three Editorial-stream RFCs in four years. Python is the outlier and the one apparent success: it did not fix the PEP process, it **shrank the scope of what a PEP has to decide** by standing up four domain councils in 2023–2025. Rust independently did the same thing with MCPs (585 filed, 17 open) and project goals. Nobody has published a controlled comparison; the "route around it with a lighter process" move is the only intervention with a visible before/after in the data.
