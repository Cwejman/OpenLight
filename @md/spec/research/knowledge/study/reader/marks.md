# How it marks

One brief is one object, seen from several distances. Everything that names it carries the same identity, so a hover anywhere lights it everywhere at once and tells what it is.

## 1. The hover, and the card

A pointer on anything that names a room lights that room in every view, swings the figure's needle to it, and opens a card: its face, what it holds here and in its rooms, its paragraphs and how many run heavy, what it points at and what points at it, and whether it has been read or changed since.

The card is a summoned surface, so it is glass rather than a box: the ground shows through, blurred, its edges dissolved, with no shadow and no drawn border. A click pins it, and pinned it is actionable, offering its rooms, what points at it, the way in and the step out.

Where a room lies deeper than the division's floor it has no cell of its own, so the room that holds it is marked instead. The sync always resolves to somewhere real.

## 2. What has been read

What a reader has seen is kept in their browser, by a name that survives renumbering. The division dims as it is read and lights warm where a brief changed since it was last seen, and a bar says how much of the whole stands read, changed and unread.

## 3. The hue of a region

Six hues, assigned in fixed order to the regions of the substrate and never cycled, validated against this ground for colour blindness and contrast in both themes. Hue says which region a brief belongs to; depth says how far in it sits. In the prose the colour is a small mark beside a name and never the text itself.

## 4. Heaviness, seen before it is read

Every brief carries its own paragraphs as a texture, in the maps and beside every door, warm wherever one runs past five hundred characters. A reader judges difficulty from shape before spending any reading on it, and under the code that judgment is the verdict. This is the one thing the labs have failed most often, and a rule never fixed it. Measured: the piece this was built against carries eighty-four paragraphs past that mark.

## 5. Ground that moved after you did

A brief stands on what it links to, and git knows when each file last changed. Where something a brief points at was committed after the brief itself, the brief is marked: its ground moved and it was not written again. The mark is a word beside the name in the spreads, the tree and the rail, and the hover card says how many of the things it stands on changed after it did.

What the mark really measures is the distance between two states: the target as it stood when the brief was last written, which is what the brief was actually written against, and the target as it stands now. Resolving a link to its anchored state is not built; git holds everything needed for it, and until it is, the mark says that the distance exists without being able to show it.

It is a suspicion and not a verdict. A pointer can survive its target changing, and often does. But it is the cheapest honest signal a reader or a harness has for staleness, and it costs nothing but a read of the log. Seen, on this room: four briefs carry the mark at the close of the sitting that wrote them, which is what a sitting that revises its own ground should expect.

## 6. The stamp a file carries

A file says what it is at its head, and the reader reads it. Under the code, and of what kind: a brief is prose ordered by the gradient, and a record is an array of entries whose order is time. A record's parts are shown as entries in the order it declares, not as rooms to descend by importance, because reading a sequence by the gradient reads it wrongly.

This is what lets a directory be onboarded gradually to a public surface. What carries the stamp is knowledge and is served; what does not is a file the site leaves alone. The form of the stamp is [the practice's open](../10-comprehension/poc.md), not this program's.
