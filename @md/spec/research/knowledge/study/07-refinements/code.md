# The code

The code is a set of working rules for how knowledge is written and arranged. Their one aim: whoever comes to the knowledge, with whatever need, finds what serves that need at hand — the most important things first, the detail within reach. This document is the whole code. Reading it leaves you able to write under it: you will hold why it exists, the two pieces knowledge is built from, the laws and the principles that guide the writing, and how AI applies them.

---

## Why there is a code

What this code fixes shows up in many different places, and that is why it is hard to see as one thing. Underneath, the failure is always the same: something is understood, and it does not reach the ones who need it.

The most familiar place to feel it is a team. Each person holds the knowledge of their own role and grows rigid inside it. Fully comprehending each other is not easy, so mostly we act on partial comprehension. Some of those acts cost far more to undo than the full comprehension would have cost — and that gap is the waste this code goes after. The cost lands later and elsewhere, cut off from its cause: a year on, the mistake has shaped everything and is not called a mistake any more. It has become how things are. Any team, any governance structure, knows this.

The smaller faces are daily. You open your own notes and must rebuild the thinking they were supposed to hold. A model decides again, differently, what it already decided an hour ago. A newcomer reads the documentation and cannot stand on it: pieces are missing, or it will not come apart into pieces they can take in.

The code's answer starts with one move: the knowledge is written, and written into one shared space. Not just the documentation — the goals, the plans, the decisions, the record each working session leaves. In that space nobody has to hold everything, because everyone's understanding is joined there, connected to the goals it serves. And knowledge held that way works for whoever comes and whatever they come for: a teammate, a newcomer, a model, a new idea.

That is also what makes knowledge compound. A thing you learn is worth more joined to what you already know, and worth more again when someone else can stand on it: each piece becomes ground for the next. But connection does not happen by itself. The pieces need a shape that lets them meet, and this document's whole job is to describe that shape.

The shape is easiest to see in building something new. You start with the background and the goals. From them come the requirements, and the requirements grow into the architecture of a solution. The solution stands on more than the requirements: it stands just as much on the goals, on the background, and on the agreed principles, practices and taste of the builders. That makes it a web, not a chain. And the solution ends as a product whose details are written down too, so the knowledge reaches all the way to what is built.

Then a new idea, or a new person, arrives onto all the ground needed to comprehend — and, naturally, to contribute. Nothing has to feel complex on arrival, because place follows reach: the more something matters to everything else, the nearer the centre it sits. From there, each step outward carries a little less weight and a little more detail, until the far edge holds the finest points, waiting for the few who need them.

Whoever comes to that space — person or AI — comes as a first-class citizen of it. And this needs no new technology to be proven effective. Plain files in folders, tracked in version control, are enough to hold the structure, and the AI harnesses that already exist are its natural match: a fresh session holds nothing, so a session started against the structure becomes what it reads. Every session arrives grounded and reliable, and always up to date and can fully contribute in the system it self. The difference people blame on models is mostly a difference in ground.

The code is that shape, written as working rules. This document states it.

## The limit

Nobody can hold everything. A person forgets; an AI model's context window fills up and degrades as it fills. Better tools can widen what is held and cheapen the reaching, but they do not change how understanding works: you hold what matters, and on the rest you are briefed when its time comes. The whole code follows from taking this seriously: knowledge is written to be understood in parts and handed over in parts, never asking anyone to carry the whole.

## The brief and the link

Work can build on other work without any special shape — inside a monolith. Before Unix there were operating systems too, but to build on one you had to learn its insides and become part of it. Unix won by the opposite move: it made the interface to the world simple — small pieces that stand alone, one shared medium, one simple join. Programs, joined by pipes; by definition, not a monolith. The traditional web is the same shape: pages, joined by links. For knowledge, the piece is here called a **brief**, and the join is a **link**.

A brief is a piece of writing that stands on its own: its essence at the surface, its detail kept within. Everything it uses, it explains or links out, and following the link is optional. The name means what everyday speech means by it. To brief someone is to hand them, quickly, the understanding they need to act. A legal brief is a whole case, compressed for the person who must act on it. And the making has a folk verb too: when a stretch of work ends, it is debriefed — the ground that was covered gives up its conclusion, the conclusion goes to the surface, and the detail stays within for whoever needs it.

And a brief is brief. That is not a side effect of the name; it is a duty. The reader came to be briefed, not to relive the work — so what they gain must come at once, and they must never be force-fed. A brief that grows long is holding more than one gain, and it is broken down — into briefs nested within it, or briefs beside it — until each gain lands on its own. Nothing is lost in the breaking: the detail moves a level deeper, not away. Each nested brief is again whole on its own — a brief is a holon (a whole that is also a part of a greater whole), and this holds at every depth. A brief can be a whole file or a section inside one.

A link is the one way briefs join, and joining is what makes small pieces a larger whole. A brief is whole on its own, but not from nothing: it stands on other briefs. A reader who already holds that ground reads straight through; a reader who lacks it may not comprehend — and exactly that is what the link is for. The ground is named where it is used and linked, so the missing comprehension is always one step away, never restated. A link can also hold a door to more than the brief says. Either way it is an offer: the sentence around it says what is behind it, so every reader knows whether to follow it or read on.

## The two laws

Everything else the code asks comes down to two laws. Laws, not advice: each states a condition, and where it fails, knowledge stops compounding. Compounding has two sides — every brief is first handed to a reader, and after that it is ground that others stand on. One law for the hand-over, one for the ground.

### Law 1 — Nothing arrives before its ground

**Understanding is built in the reader's order: everything is given before it is used, and every stopping point leaves the reader whole.**

Everything is given before it is used — meaning before mention, purpose before machinery. Prose that runs ahead of itself turns reading into algebra: the text speaks of X before giving X, and the reader now carries K, L and M about X while X remains unknown. This is why a brief opens with what it is for and stays true to the law thourgh out. And where the subject truly forces the other order, the debt is declared in a line: such "algebraic borrowing" is allowed, but done with care, and with links when possible.

Every stopping point leaves the reader whole. A reader may stop anywhere — after the opening, after a surface, before a link — and what they have read must stand on its own. This is why the sentence around a link says what following it gives: going deeper is a choice made in the open, and stopping costs nothing that was not chosen.

### Law 2 — The ground stays true

**Whatever is stood on must hold for whoever stands on it next: every fact keeps exactly one home, and every claim carries its confidence.**

Every fact keeps exactly one home. Copies are how ground rots: three copies of a rule quietly become three rules. So a fact is stated properly once and pointed at from everywhere else — when it changes, its one home changes, and every pointer stays true.

Every claim carries its confidence. Confidence is part of the writing, never a feeling the reader is left to sense: an unmarked guess, read a year later, becomes law without anyone deciding. And settled never means forever ruled. Every piece rests on pieces that can move, and a new piece can arrive and change what the old ones meant — so "settled" means settled for now, given what it stands on.

## The principles

The laws state the conditions, but they are wide, and wide rules are easy to honor loosely. The principles below are the laws made specific: each one is an emanation of a law — the same rule, learned and tuned from empirical studies in transforming knowledge. They bind exactly as the laws bind. The first two stand first because they are the two most often broken, and breaking them quietly ruins everything else.

### 1. Every brief opens with its why, and the why is a win

The first thing a brief gives is what the reader gains by reading it. Not background. Not a list of its parts. The gain: what you will understand after reading that you did not before, and what that understanding is good for.

This is what engagement is. A text pulls the reader onward by handing them win after win, the biggest wins first, so meaning compounds as they read. Knowledge compounds, so importance has a shape: a few insights carry everything else, while most details matter only to their own subject. Place follows that shape.

A document is a holonarchy (a hierarchy of holons): briefs nested inside briefs, each whole on its own and part of the whole above. The document's opening is its topmost brief, and that brief carries the why of the whole: the most central insight, one organic piece of prose that holds everything nested below it. It may then say what the parts are, but each part is named by what it gives, never only by what it contains. A brief is not to be an outline of its contents, just organically tie together the outcome with simple direcrections to its contents how is needed.

The same holds at every level. Each brief down opens with its own why — a clear new win, one step more detailed, easy to take. The moment reading feels force-fed, the gradient broke: too much arrived at once, too high up. The edges hold the final details — the numbers, the fine points nobody should have to read unless they truly want to.

So a reader enters only as deep as they want. They get the best things first, and gradually less toward the edges. Stopped at any depth, they leave holding the most that could be held in that much reading.

### 2. Dead simple language

Write international English that a non-native speaker reads without friction. Use only words everyday speech already uses. Short sentences, one idea per sentence. A long sentence held together by dashes and semicolons is a failed sentence: split it into short ones. And a paragraph holds one point and stays light — a few sentences, taken in at a glance. A longer one is sometimes needed, but most long paragraphs are two points.

> More than 500+ chars in a paragraph leans towards too long, a hundred or two more is a red flag.

No academic, literary, or clever vocabulary — ever, even where it would be shorter. The test: a reader from outside this field of work follows every sentence on first read.

Simple is not terse: explain everything fully. Simplicity is about the words and the sentences, never about cutting explanation.

### 3. The document is briefs all the way down

A heading starts a brief. The text between a heading and its first subheading is that brief's surface: its why and its conclusion. Detail drops into the subsections beneath, where a reader who has what they need skips past, and one who wants more reads on.

The order law holds at every level: the briefs under one parent stand in reading order, each standing on the surfaces before it. A nested brief may be skipped; when a later part  depends on something the reader may have skipped, link to that heading, so the skipping reader knows where their ground is.

The document has one strong limitation: it is a line. At every level the briefs stand in one fixed order, and the order is not free: a brief may stand on the briefs before it, never on the ones after. So the best line is a build, each brief standing on the last. The knowledge itself is a web, and what the line cannot show, the links must state. As of now we do not have a technology for browsing a field of briefs dynamically; the document of nested headings has served for many centuries and is the practice the code refines.

### 4. Headings carry numbers

Number the headings, and nest the numbers with the headings: 1, then 1.1 and 1.2 inside it. In a nested document the numbers are the visual cue that tells a reader where they are while scrolling, and they give every brief a short address that can be spoken or written anywhere.

### 5. Bullets are light, and have no address

A bullet is a paragraph that cannot be broken up, and no link can reach it. Both facts say the same thing: hold bullets lightly.

A bullet list is a fine way to hold small things. A bullet longer than a sentence or two opens with its point in bold, then its body, so the reader decides from the bold alone whether to read on.

And a bullet stays even lighter than a paragraph. Past a few sentences it is not a bullet any more: it is a brief, and a brief gets a heading. So does anything a link may ever need to reach.

> More than 300+ chars in a paragraph leans towards too long, a hundred or two more is a red flag.

### 6. A link may point beyond what is written

The laws can sound as if they describe a finished thing. It never is: knowledge is built step by step, on work in progress, and the code is made for that. A brief may be written for something not yet solved — the question set down before its answer exists.

So a link does not need a target that already exists. A link to an external source not yet read and brought in is a valid link: it is the brief you did not write. The sentence around it still says what is behind it, and the text still reads whole without it.

And notice when you say much about an external source: that is a brief growing in the wrong place. Give the source its own brief — what you know so far, with the pointer to it — until the day it is digested into its own web of briefs.

## How AI applies the code

One pass is never enough. The limit above applies to the writer too: no mind holds a whole text and this whole code at once, a model included. So an agent working under the code never ships its first result. It checks the result against the code — every law, every principle — takes what the check finds, and rewrites. Then it checks again. It repeats this until it is confident the result breaks the code nowhere. However many rounds that takes is how many it takes.

And the brief is the unit of change. A small fix — a word, a name, one fact — may be edited in place. But an edit that changes what a brief says — its win, its argument, its order — is never patched in: the brief is rewritten whole, held in one head at one moment, for only then is its wholeness checkable. This is also how a model writes best: a brief generated in one turn comes out organic and whole; a brief patched over many turns comes out stitched. Many small edits, each harmless alone, together shift a brief's meaning while nobody holds the whole. The test: if the brief's opening would need to change, the whole brief does.

> There are many techniques for orchestration of inference-passes of LLMs, such as using different models or different limited grounds including reverse prompting. These techniques are not yet prescribed. More on it is to be defined as we progress.

## The code in practice

The code stands on briefs and links alone, and a brief is prose. Beyond that it is agnostic: it does not care what technology holds the briefs. Today they live as markdown files in folders, tracked in version control. How that is done well — how documents break into files, how folders nest, how links are written — has its own brief: [the markdown practice](), not yet written.

And the code itself obeys law 2: the studies behind each rule, and the refinements still owed, are kept in [the grounds](), not yet written.
