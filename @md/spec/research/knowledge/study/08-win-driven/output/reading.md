# How people read, and what it does to the code

This is what the reading and learning sciences say about the code's first law, its principle on the order of a holarchy, its prose principle and its story principle: which rules are carried, by how much, on whom, and where the code's readers fall outside what was measured. It stands on three sweep reports, [the cognitive-science floor](../../../../sweep-2026-08/raw/adc8fe8e64ae23f70.md), [the professional-reader sweep](../../../../sweep-2026-08/raw/a4071d48c3f5d869c.md) and [the citation audit](../../../../sweep-2026-08/raw/a3f4c332843acf946.md), which hold the paper links, the exact wording and the full numbers behind every finding here. Reading it, you know which of the code's rules about the brief rest on measurement and which on preference.

Sizes below are given as fractions of a standard deviation, the usual unit for comparing studies: a fifth is small, the average reader with the treatment doing better than 58 of 100 without; a half is medium; one is large. Preferred.

## 1. The why first is carried, and small

The code's thesis, the biggest win as early as the reader can hold it, has an ancestor in the advance organiser (a short passage before a text that frames what follows), and the evidence for that is positive and small. A meta-analysis of 135 studies found an average effect of a fifth of a standard deviation *[Luiten, Ames and Ackerson 1980]*. Measured, by others; verified against the primary abstract in the sweep.

The effect holds and its theory does not. A second meta-analysis of 112 studies found that the studies with the largest effects lacked the features the theory behind organisers (Ausubel's, that a more abstract frame subsumes the detail beneath it) predicted, and that organisers which were abstract and written-only did worse than concrete or graphic ones *[Stone 1983]*. So the win at the top should be concrete, in what the reader holds, which is what the code says, and not more abstract than the body, which a reader of the code might infer. Measured, by others; verified against the primary abstract.

Where an idea sits in a text decides what is understood from it. The ideas at the top of a text's outline, and the relations among them, change comprehension a great deal; the same ideas placed low in the outline change it little *[Meyer 1975]*. This is the one finding that carries the code's ordering rule directly, and it is old and has not been tested on documents longer than a few thousand words. Measured, by others; not re-verified in the sweep.

A stated purpose works as an allocator of attention, not a multiplier of understanding. A preregistered meta-analysis of prequestions (questions given before a text) found a gain of half a standard deviation on the material the questions covered, across 97 comparisons, and no gain on everything else in the same text, across 91; the cognitive-science report gives the study by its PubMed number, 37640836, not by author. The code's contract, the title and first paragraph saying what you will understand, is a prequestion; expect it to improve learning of what it names. Measured, by others.

Assistance helps the reader who lacks the ground, and harms the reader who has it less than folklore says. The finding that help which serves a novice can hinder an expert (expertise reversal) was meta-analysed in 2025 across 60 studies and 5,924 participants: low-prior-knowledge learners gain half a standard deviation from added assistance, while the harm to high-prior-knowledge learners is not significant inside any single education level *[Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters and Brod 2025]*. There is no category for practising professionals because there were no such studies. So the code's refusal to fork prose for novice and expert is not refuted, and the cheapest reading of the evidence is the code's own: scaffold for the novice, and let the expert skip. Measured, by others; the moderator table verified in the sweep.

## 2. The whole brief and the few links are carried

Bounded units help, and the mechanism is pacing. Segmenting, breaking a presentation into parts the learner takes one at a time, has a small-to-medium effect on remembering and on applying what was learned, across 56 investigations *[Rey and others 2019]*. The effect comes from the learner controlling the pace, and a meta-analysis of 29 reviews and 78,177 participants found design effects weaker in self-paced settings, such as reading a page, than in system-paced ones such as lectures *[Noetel and others 2021]*; a reader who controls their own pace recovers much of what bad design costs. Measured, by others.

Links cost, and hierarchy is what pays the cost back. A review of hypertext reading found that deciding whether to follow links impaired reading, that learning was better with fewer links, and that readers with less working memory and prior knowledge were the ones hurt *[DeStefano and LeFevre 2007]*. A study of 54 teacher trainees found a network of pages produced far more disorientation than a hierarchy of them, and far less coherent reading sequences, and hurt low-prior-knowledge readers most *[Amadieu and others 2020]*. This is the best-evidenced of the code's structural rules: a brief whole in itself, forward links few, and the holarchy's order as the reader's path. Measured, by others.

That a link should say what following it gives has no direct test; the code's rule stands on reason alone, and the hypertext finding above makes it plausible, since a reader who knows what a link gives need not follow it to find out. Reasoned.

Text-structure effects fade. A meta-analysis of 44 studies of teaching text structure found immediate effects of a quarter to half a standard deviation and every delayed effect non-significant *[Bogaerds-Hazenberg and others 2021]*. What a brief's shape buys is bought at the reading; what lasts is what the reader inferred, which is what [§5](#5-a-story-is-the-largest-effect-in-the-literature) is about. Measured, by others.

The rule that a fact has one home is not a comprehension finding, and the code should not borrow one. The redundancy effect sometimes cited for it, from three experiments on 25 technical apprentices, is about the same content in writing and speech at the same moment *[Kalyuga, Chandler and Sweller 2004]*, not about a fact restated in a document read weeks later. Restatement across time is a different thing, spacing, and is not what the redundancy effect measured. The single home is a law of the ground, and the restatement the code allows, the brief above saying its win in fewer words than the briefs beneath, is the organiser of [§1](#1-the-why-first-is-carried-and-small). Reasoned, on measured parts.

## 3. Nobody knows how deep or how wide

No study in this literature names a size for a section or a depth for a hierarchy. The three studies of menu depth and breadth that get cited for one were verified at their primaries by the citation audit, and each is an optimum at a fixed item count. Over 64 targets, two levels of eight was fastest *[D. P. Miller 1981]*. Over 64 targets again, four top-level items each holding sixteen was fastest, not reliably faster than eight by eight; the primary is paywalled and this is Larson and Czerwinski's account of it *[Kiger 1984]*. Over 512 items with 19 experienced web users, both two-level structures, sixteen by thirty-two and thirty-two by sixteen, beat eight by eight by eight on time and on how lost people got, with no reliable difference between the two *[Larson and Czerwinski 1998]*. Measured, by others.

The winning widths exceed every head-size number a depth rule is derived from. Eight, sixteen and thirty-two are all past seven, and the memory-span numbers themselves do not say what they are cited for; [the numbers not to cite, §3](folklore.md#3-the-limits-of-the-head) traces each. Reasoned, on the studies above.

The most famous hand-built knowledge structure is a counter-example to a shallow rule. The sociologist Niklas Luhmann's slip box, roughly 90,000 numbered paper slips built over 45 years and often cited for flat, atomic notes, ran to four sub-levels, with 108 divisions in the first box and one division of 120 subdivisions, and its archive says the arrangement was not strictly hierarchical. Seen, at the archive's own description.

No method for deriving a structure from its readers has been validated either. The one study that tested it, a card sort with 27 people (people grouping labelled cards into the categories they find natural) and then 50 people navigating structures of varying fit to those groupings, found the fit had no effect on browsing performance *[Schmettow and Sommer 2016]*; the only positive test compared a card-sorted structure to a legacy one chosen for known problems. Measured for the null, by others; the detail is in [the card-sorting sweep](../../../../sweep-2026-08/raw/ae8fc467393dd0407.md), which traces the method's whole evidence base.

People do prefer a hierarchy. Given better search, they still navigate folders by choice, so a hierarchy is something readers want and not only a filing artefact *[Bergman, Beyth-Marom and Nachmias 2008]*. The practice's lean of three heading levels is a preference, and it should stay marked as one. Measured for the first, by others; preferred for the second.

## 4. Plain words are carried, for experts too

Syntactic complexity taxes the expert as much as the novice. In two preregistered experiments, 105 practising lawyers and 108 laypeople read contracts written in legal style and in plain style; training raised recall and comprehension for everyone, and the legal style cost the lawyers as much as it cost the laypeople *[Martínez, Mollica and Gibson 2023]*. A further 102 lawyers rated the plain contracts equally enforceable and higher in quality. The corpus study behind it, ten million words, found clauses nested inside clauses the feature that hurt recall most *[Martínez, Mollica and Gibson 2022]*. This is the best-designed professional-reader experiment in the sweep, and it says the code's everyday words are not a concession to the newcomer. Measured, by others; full text read in the sweep.

Deliberately under-explaining does not help your best readers. Low-knowledge readers gain from a well-connected text; the reverse benefit, high-knowledge readers doing better with a loosely connected one because they must fill the gaps, was later found restricted to knowledgeable readers who were poor comprehenders, while skilled readers with high knowledge did better with the well-connected text *[McNamara, Kintsch, Songer and Kintsch 1996; O'Reilly and McNamara 2007]*. The code's "simple is not terse" is what this licenses. Measured, by others.

Specific beats vague. Among 61 general practitioners making 12,880 decisions, clear recommendations were followed 67 percent of the time and vague ones 36 percent, confounded with how controversial the recommendation was and how much it asked them to change *[Grol and others 1998]*. Measured, by others; observational.

The cheapest intervention is a signal word. Words that name the relation between points, "the problem is", "as a result", "in contrast", carry a small reliable effect on comprehension and on applying what was read, larger for readers with less prior knowledge *[Richter, Scheiter and Eitel 2016; Alpizar and others 2020]*; the code's prose principle does not mention them and should. Measured, by others.

## 5. A story is the largest effect in the literature

Stories are understood and remembered better than the same content as exposition, across more than 75 samples and 33,000 participants, and the effect is not moderated by any study characteristic tested *[Mar, Li, Nguyen and Ta 2021]*. Details that interest but do not serve the point reliably harm learning, across 68 effects *[Sundararajan and Adesope 2020]*. Measured, by others.

Of the six sources the code's story principle cites, the sweep reached two: the narrative advantage, above, and the harm of details that do not serve, which the code cites as Harp and Mayer and the sweep verified through the later meta-analysis. The chain-of-causes account, the story grammar, the grounding-by-situation study and the guard-lowering study were not fetched and stay owed, as [the principle itself says](../code.md#44-a-story-carries-the-causes). Seen.

## 6. Where the studies are and where the readers are

Almost every study above was run on students, on passages of a few hundred to a couple of thousand words, read once, tested within the hour. The expertise-reversal meta-analysis has three vocational studies out of sixty and no professionals; its "expert" is a student above the median on a pretest. Seen, in the studies' own samples.

The one professional literature that exists says the failure with a long document is retrieval, not comprehension. Thirteen physicians and assistants preparing consultations from a paper guideline could read it and could not find the applicable recommendation *[Kilsdonk and others 2016]*. Measured, small.

Preference is not comprehension. In the only randomised trial of guideline format in practising clinicians, 181 physicians preferred a layered format 72 to 16 percent, and correct understanding moved from 58 to 72 percent without reaching significance *[Brandt and others 2017]*. A format claim that rests on readers liking it should be graded down. Measured, by others.

Experts read less, not differently. Across 78 professional developers and 3,148 instrumented hours, time spent comprehending fell with experience, 66, 56 and 44 percent of working time for the least, middle and most experienced third *[Xia and others 2018]*; that is what expertise buys. The same study is the measured replacement for the reading-ratio folklore, in [the numbers not to cite, §2](folklore.md#2-how-developers-spend-their-time). Measured, by others.

The direction of the gap runs against the code. Its readers are exactly the knowledgeable, self-paced, goal-directed readers for whom the design effects of [§1](#1-the-why-first-is-carried-and-small) and [§2](#2-the-whole-brief-and-the-few-links-are-carried) are smallest, and the plain-words effects of [§4](#4-plain-words-are-carried-for-experts-too) are the ones measured on them. Reasoned.

## 7. Open

No study watches a professional re-read a long technical document over months. No study tests whether skipping a nested brief does the work of prose written twice. No study tests a link that says what following it gives against one that names its topic. No study evaluates a settledness marker on a claim. And no study measures a document longer than a few thousand words at all.
