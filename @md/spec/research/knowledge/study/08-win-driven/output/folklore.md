# The numbers not to cite

These are figures a writer about knowledge is likely to meet, which the sweep traced to nothing, or to something other than what they are cited for. The code's second law is why they matter: an unmarked guess read a year later becomes law, and every one of these did. Each brief names the number, what it is attributed to, what it traces to, and what is measured in its place, so a brief under the code never carries one unmarked. It stands on [the organisational-knowledge report](../../sweep-2026-08/raw/abdf5bfa14a7ddd82.md), [the citation-verification report](../../sweep-2026-08/raw/a3f4c332843acf946.md) and [the software-folklore trace](../../sweep-2026-08/raw/aec803bcd71ce9745.md), which hold the primary sources and the exact wording behind every item here.

## 1. The cost of late change

The 1:10:100 ratio has a real story, and it is about how a number becomes law. In 1967 IBM opened a customer-education unit in Los Angeles and called it the Systems Science Institute; it taught courses until about 1982. Decades later a chart attributed to it, one at design, six and a half at coding, fifteen at testing, a hundred in maintenance, was repeated into every requirements deck, and the education unit became "IBM research". Laurent Bossavit, who wrote the book on software folklore, searched IBM's own site, Google Scholar and the biographies of the institute's supposed staff, and found a training programme, no study, no data, no N; the chart traces to a textbook footnote. Cite nothing to it. Seen, by Bossavit; the chase verified in the sweep.

Its siblings fall with it. Each item is a claim and where it stands, all seen at the primaries the reports name.

- "100 times in production" *[Boehm and Basili 2001]*: real, and the authors themselves wrote "often", and "more like 5:1 than 100:1" for small non-critical systems.

- The delayed-issue effect at all: the largest test, 171 projects from 2006 to 2014, found no evidence for it *[Menzies, Nichols, Shull and Layman 2016]*.

- "70 to 85 percent of rework is from requirements defects" and "50 to 60 percent of defects originate in requirements": no primary measurement reached; they propagate through requirements-management marketing. A review of 57 requirements-quality papers found 47.5 percent of claimed impacts merely hypothesised *[Frattini and others 2023]*.

- Standish CHAOS project success and failure rates: definitions one-sided, sampling solicited failure stories, design undisclosed *[Eveleens and Verhoef 2010]*.

- "Design docs reduce defects" and "six-page memos produce better decisions": the first has no outcome study; the second traces solely to a 2017 shareholder letter.

## 2. How developers spend their time

"Developers read ten times more than they write" is one author's recollection. The passage in *Clean Code* says the ratio is "well over 10:1" and gives its provenance honestly two paragraphs earlier: watching editor playbacks in the eighties and nineties and finding them mostly scrolling. No instrumentation, no N; the chapter's bibliography has two entries, neither about the ratio; no earlier numerical source exists. The playback observation is about navigation, not reading. Seen, verbatim against the full text.

"Half of a programmer's time is comprehension" rests on a paper nobody has read. The load-bearing citation, Fjeldstad and Hamlen 1983, has no DOI and is in no index, and its two citing papers give incompatible records for it, one as IEEE 1982 pages 13 to 30, the other as Proceedings Guide 1983 article 48: the fingerprint of a reference copied between papers. The 35 percent sometimes cited from *[Ko and others 2006]* is time on the mechanics of navigation, not comprehension. And the "5 percent writing new code" figure is a hypothetical junior persona in a 2002 email, not even its author's own observation. Seen.

What is measured is close and differently denominated. Across 78 professional developers and 3,148 instrumented hours, comprehension took 57.62 percent of working time, navigation 24 and editing 5, and much of the comprehension was in browsers and document editors, not the code editor *[Xia and others 2018]*. Across 20 professionals' whole workdays, coding in all its forms was 21 percent, with email, browsing and meetings taking the rest *[Meyer and others 2017]*. The 70 percent figure comes from 18 developers in one editor where any pause over a second was counted as understanding *[Minelli, Mocci and Lanza 2015]*. Measured; verified from the primary PDFs.

## 3. The limits of the head

Every number cited for how much a head holds is either misread or disowned by its author. Each item says which, all seen verbatim at the primaries.

- "Seven plus or minus two" *[Miller 1956]*: its author called it "only a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence", and recoding into chunks raises capacity. It does not apply to a menu or a page, which asks the reader to recognise, not recall.

- "Four chunks" *[Cowan 2001]*: measured only when chunking was experimentally blocked, and its author says outside those conditions the limit is unknown; his 2010 restatement is three to five in young adults.

- Chess masters and chunk limits *[Chase and Simon 1973]*: three players, one domain, and the chunk limit was the premise, taken from Miller, not the result.

- Dunbar's 150, the supposed limit on how many people one person can know, derived from brain size: the study that re-ran the derivation found the plausible range so wide, 4 to 520 people by one method and 2 to 336 by another, that it concludes no number can be derived at all *[Lindenfors, Wartel and Lind 2021]*.

- "Two levels, three at most", as a rule for hierarchies: nothing verified supports it, and the widths that won in the depth-and-breadth studies exceed every span above; the studies are in [how people read, §3](reading.md#3-nobody-knows-how-deep-or-how-wide).

- "Three clicks": tested on 44 users and 620 tasks, no more likelihood of quitting after three than after twelve *[Porter 2003]*.

## 4. What documentation is said to buy

The documentation multipliers are the newest folklore and the fastest retracted. Each item gives the number and its ground, all seen at the primaries.

- DORA's "2.4 times" and its 656 and 1,525 percent lifts: withdrawn by the next two reports; the retraction is in [what documentation does, §2](documentation.md#2-the-multipliers-were-retracted-by-their-publisher).

- "Structured content saves 30 to 40 percent" and "up to 79 percent in translation": vendor calculators and consultancy retellings; no primary study in any index reached.

- "Structured knowledge bases outperform unstructured by 29 to 46 points": the cited vendor page contains no such figure.

- GitLab's handbook at 605,000 words: absent from every GitLab page fetched, live and archived; the 2,000 printed pages is real.

- Graphic organisers at 1.24 against written ones at 0.80: in neither of the two advance-organiser meta-analyses of [how people read, §1](reading.md#1-the-why-first-is-carried-and-small), by abstract or record; blog-laundered.

- "80 percent success is good" in tree testing (people finding an item in a bare hierarchy): no vendor publishes a threshold, and the method's whole evidence base is one 2009 anecdote.

The card-sorting curve is a real story of the same shape as the first. In 2004 two researchers had 168 employees sort 46 cards and drew a graph of how well small samples matched the full one, printing no numbers. A well-known usability consultant read values off the graph, 0.90 agreement at fifteen participants, and published them as findings; twenty-two years later every guide to the method quotes them as measured, and nobody has revised them. The method itself has been tested for validity once, and failed; that study is in [how people read, §3](reading.md#3-nobody-knows-how-deep-or-how-wide). Seen, at the 2004 paper and the 2004 article.

## 5. The AI-era numbers

The newest figures are already folklore, and some are illustrative examples cited as measurements. All seen at the source named.

- "98.7 percent, 150,000 tokens to 2,000", for presenting tools to a model as a folder tree: Anthropic's post gives it as an illustrative example with no methodology; the mechanism is real at one to two orders of magnitude smaller.

- "Knowledge graphs make LLMs three times more accurate": 43 questions, vendor-authored, a weak baseline, and a hand-built ontology; evidence for human-declared structure, not extracted graphs.

- "Tool descriptions get selected 260 percent more often": 20 to 72 percent selection among competitors, restated as relative lift; it does not measure task success.

- "Agent Skills won": adoption in the tens of thousands; the only controlled benchmark shows them lowering pass rate at higher cost.

- The "grep tax", a 9,649-experiment study said to show that a compact data format, TOON, costs a model more than plain JSON: not reached; TOON's own benchmark shows it 0.8 points more accurate than JSON with a margin of error of 2.8 points, a null.

- Any "N times" figure for internal-data quality from DORA 2025: the report publishes ranked bands, not numbers.
