# What documentation does, on the evidence

This is what has been measured about documentation as a thing organisations make: whether it changes outcomes, whether it stays true, and why knowledge fails to travel even when written. It stands on [the organisational-knowledge report](../../sweep-2026-08/raw/abdf5bfa14a7ddd82.md), [the documentation-effectiveness sweep](../../sweep-2026-08/raw/a7b21ac3d71f8c8f6.md), [the minimalism and quality sweep](../../sweep-2026-08/raw/a5e1231b95c4c9ac5.md), [the decision-record sweep](../../sweep-2026-08/raw/aae121db667d39f2d.md) and [the DORA audit](../../sweep-2026-08/raw/abeeefde341d33a2d.md), which hold the paper links, the exact wording and the full numbers behind every finding here. Reading it, you know what a writer under the code can expect a brief to do for a team, and what bounds the code's promise that nobody has to be walked through it.

## 1. Nothing has shown that documentation causes an outcome

No experiment anywhere has changed the documentation a group had and measured what the organisation got from it. The sweep looked in software engineering, technical communication and information design and found a handful of true experiments, all small, and no meta-analysis. The documentation-quality literature, larger and newer, catalogues what is wrong with documents, incompleteness, ambiguity, staleness, from surveys of 323 IBM professionals and 878 mined artefacts, and never measures what a good document buys *[Uddin and Robillard 2015; Aghajani and others 2019]*. Seen: three agents searched independently and found none.

The one replicated programme is forty years old. The Minimal Manual, a short task-first manual for a word processor, cut learning time by 40 percent for ten temp-agency typists against nine, and in a second experiment of 32 got 58 percent more subtasks done *[Carroll, Smith-Kerker, Ford and Mazur-Rimetz 1987]*. A meta-analysis of 13 effects and 288 participants put the pooled effect at 1.12 standard deviations, large, and since the studies that only cut repetition got 0.89 of it, its authors suspected that cutting repetition, not the theory, was the active ingredient *[Ginns, Hollender and Reimann 2006]*; that meta-analysis was never peer-reviewed, and its studies were chosen with help from the theory's own authors, which it acknowledges. The most-repeated misreading, that minimalism means brevity, is the first item on the authors' own list of misconceptions, and a minimalist manual takes 30 percent longer to write *[van der Meij 2017]*. Measured, small and old; the effect sizes verified from the 1987 paper.

Two modern experiments exist and both are about code. Students given a platform of vetted cryptography examples were 73 percent more effective and wrote 66 percent fewer possible vulnerabilities, 58 participants, one task *[Mindermann and Wagner 2018]*. Sixty-five people randomised to a narrative essay or a structured document about a software architecture understood it equally well; prior exposure to the code was what mattered *[Ernst and Robillard 2023]*. The second is the best-controlled result on document format the sweep found, and it says format did not matter. Measured, by others.

The XML lineage of technical writing, structured authoring, has no evidence base either: no peer-reviewed study of its reuse savings in any index the sweep could reach, and none behind the claim that XML content is better for AI. Seen, by two agents who searched and found none; the lineage's state is in [the structured-authoring report](../../sweep-2026-08/raw/a5e68a671a08e519a.md) and [the tech-comm status report](../../sweep-2026-08/raw/a8712480bac2a248a.md), which are not brought in here.

## 2. The multipliers were retracted by their publisher

The figure most often quoted for documentation's payoff, DORA's "2.4 times", is real, and its own successors withdrew it. DORA is an annual survey of software delivery; its 2021 report, 1,200 respondents answering seven agree-or-disagree items, said teams with high-quality documentation were 2.4 times more likely to see better delivery performance, with no interval, effect size or p-value. The 2022 report listed under "Surprises" that documentation practices negatively impacted delivery performance. The 2023 report found "No effect" and footnoted that it was the second year. The 2025 report dropped documentation quality as a named capability, published its successor's effects as ranked bands with no numbers, and wrote that it would speak of comparisons, not effects, so as not to give false assurances about the causal structure. The percentage-lift table with its 656 and 1,525 percent figures appears on a web page and a blog post, not in any report. Seen, in the reports' own text.

## 3. Records of decisions are written once and left

The one measurement of architecture decision records, short files recording a technical decision and its reasons, is not flattering. Of 6,362 such files across 921 GitHub repositories, 51 percent were committed once and never modified; over half the adopting repositories hold one to five records; 4.6 percent show sustained team use, twenty records, two authors, six months *[Buchgeher and others 2023]*. The first study to look for an effect, on the same corpus with code-quality tools, found at most modest correlations with quality or productivity, and that 63 percent of records are opened already marked accepted, skipping the deliberation they exist to hold *[De Santana and others 2026]*. The consultancy ThoughtWorks recommended the practice to everyone in 2017 on zero data and has not revisited it since. Measured, by others; seen for the recommendation.

What this does to the code's law that a fact has one home: a home is necessary and not sufficient, since a record nothing reads decays in its home. Reasoned.

Design documents and proposal processes have no outcome evidence at all. The canonical text on design documents, an engineer's account of how Google writes them, is a 2020 experience report with no data, and the one number Google has published is that around 90 percent of its old wiki's documents had no views or updates in the previous few months. Seen.

Written proposals for decisions accumulate what nobody closes. Rust's queue holds 215 open proposals with a median age of 905 days, and Kubernetes has 343 neither finished nor rejected and 14 ever explicitly killed; the only change with a visible before-and-after, in Python and Rust alike, was routing around the heavy process with a lighter one that decides less. Seen; the counts were computed by the sweep from the repositories on 2026-08-26, in [the proposal-process sweep](../../sweep-2026-08/raw/ab6fb706729be0f77.md).

Capturing why is well theorised as a failure and never costed. The reasons are cognitive overhead, tacit knowledge that cannot be introspected, structure imposed before it is known, and structure that differs by task *[Shipman and Marshall 1999]*, an essay from a decade of building such systems rather than a measurement. The one observational study handed six professional designers a rationale document written by dedicated scribes over nine months and found it answered 41 percent of the questions they asked of it *[Karsenty 1996]*; the one controlled experiment, 17 subjects, found rationale helped on one of two systems and was inconclusive on the other *[Bratthall, Johansson and Regnell 2000]*. Seen and measured, small; the lineage is in [the design-rationale sweep](../../sweep-2026-08/raw/a86776519f614dd3a.md).

## 4. Insight fails to travel because of the receiver

The barriers to knowledge moving inside an organisation are about knowledge, not motivation. Across 122 transfers of best practice in eight firms, the barriers that weighed most were the recipient's absorptive capacity (how much a receiver can take in, which depends on what they already know), the causal ambiguity of the knowledge (nobody being sure why it works), and an arduous relationship between source and recipient; motivation ranked low *[Szulanski 1996]*. The headline analysis used only 87 of the 271 questionnaires returned, and the author says only the top three ranks are stable. Absorptive capacity has held up in later meta-analysis, with heavy moderation by industry and era *[Cohen and Levinthal 1990; Stettler and others 2025]*. Measured, single well-designed study, never independently replicated; the numbers verified against the PDF after a summariser fabricated them.

The boundary the sweep was asked about, between a client and the team delivering for it, is where the receiver's capacity is the ceiling. A client that has outsourced its technical staff has removed its own capacity to absorb, and no document written by the vendor restores it. Boundaries come in three kinds, different words, different meanings and different interests, and only the first two are crossed by translation; the third needs both sides to give something up, and a client and a vendor differ in interest *[Carlile 2004]*. Reasoned by the report's author, on the study above.

A document cannot repair a misunderstanding in the moment. Conversation is efficient because the two parties minimise their joint effort, repairing an imperfect utterance as they go; a document is maximal in being re-readable and revisable and zero in being shared in time, so grounding failures compound silently, and the least joint effort is usually to go and ask someone *[Clark and Brennan 1991]*. Reasoned by others, with laboratory evidence.

How a team is organised predicts more than what it writes, which bounds what any document can do. On Windows Vista, 3,404 binaries, measures of the organisation that built each component predicted which would fail at 86 percent precision and 84 percent recall, beating every measure of the code itself *[Nagappan, Murphy and Basili 2008]*. Among 411 Microsoft developers asked what slowed their first check-in, lack of documentation was the strongest of twelve listed factors, and when asked freely, mentorship came first and documentation second *[Rastogi and others 2015]*. Measured, single-system and opinion data respectively.

## 5. What gets maintained is what something consumes

Practitioners keep the artefacts that generate something and let the ones that only describe go stale. Across 53 practitioners in six automotive companies, motivation to maintain prescriptive models, ones that produce code or other artefacts, was higher than for descriptive ones, and the guideline that followed was to produce descriptive artefacts as late as possible and only when needed *[Wohlrab and others 2019]*. Ninety-three percent of 5,500 randomly sampled open-source contributors named incomplete or outdated documentation as a problem they had met, in GitHub's 2017 survey. Measured for the first, seen for the second.

An archetypal story carries the cause. A team writes the architecture page in its first week, because the guide says to. Nothing reads it but people, and people, taking the least joint effort, ask the person who wrote it instead. Six months on, a new colleague reads the page and it is wrong, and nobody can say since when, because nothing downstream broke when it drifted. The cause is not carelessness; it is that a description has no consumer that fails when it lies. Measured in its parts, above; the story is reasoned.

The code's promise that keeping knowledge in order stops being a job of its own therefore has a condition the code does not state: something must consume the briefs, so that a wrong brief fails somewhere. A session bootstrapped on the knowledge is such a consumer, and so is a test that reads a brief, and a person is not. Reasoned.

## 6. Open

No causal study of documentation exists, and stating that is stronger than citing a multiplier. Szulanski's design has never been re-run in another population. Whether a brief under the code survives a client-vendor boundary of interest is untested. And what counts as a consumer strong enough to keep a brief true, a session, a test, a review, has not been measured for any of the three.
