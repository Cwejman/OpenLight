# How a reader takes in a text

This is the science under the code's first law, read from the sweep: what the comprehension literature, the professional-reading studies and the information-architecture research found when they measured how a reader takes in a written text, and where each stops. It leaves you knowing which of the principles you write under have a study behind them, which have a study against them, and which nobody has tested.

## 1. The effects that held

Six findings replicated across enough studies to stand on, and together they are most of the ground under why-first and the whole brief. All of them were measured on students reading short passages once; the gap that opens is in [section 2.5](#25-the-gap-students-short-passages-fifteen-minutes).

### 1.1 What sits at the top is what gets understood

The relations among a text's ideas dramatically influenced what readers understood when those relations sat in the top third of the text's structure, and the same relations low in the structure affected comprehension minimally [Meyer 1975]. Where an idea sits in the hierarchy is load-bearing for whether it is understood and recalled. Later work in the same line found that explicit signalling words, the problem is, in contrast, as a result, are what switch weaker readers out of reading a text as a list and into reading it as a structure [Meyer and Ray 2011]. Measured, decades old, and the one comprehension result that speaks directly to the gradient.

### 1.2 A story is understood and kept better than statements

A meta-analysis of more than seventy-five samples and thirty-three thousand participants found stories better understood and better recalled than expository text, an effect the authors call robust and not moderated by study characteristics [Mar et al. 2021, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*]. It is the largest and most stable effect in this whole literature. Its bound is the seductive-details effect: interesting material that does not serve the point reliably harms learning [Sundararajan and Adesope 2020]. The operative variable is relevance, not concreteness. Measured. The code's story principle cites its sources from memory and says so; the sweep reports this meta-analysis with these numbers, and whether the agent read its text is not stated.

### 1.3 Signal words, segments, and asking the reader to explain

Three cheap interventions each carry a small replicated effect that no principle in the code mentions. Signalling words: r = .17 across twenty-seven studies and 2,464 readers [Richter, Scheiter and Eitel 2016], and d of roughly .32 to .39 across twenty-nine studies and 2,726 readers, larger for readers with little prior knowledge [Alpizar et al. 2020]. Segmenting a presentation into learner-paced pieces: a small-to-medium effect across fifty-six investigations, with the mechanism being the reader's control of pace rather than any self-containment of the pieces [Rey et al. 2019]. Prompting the reader to explain the material to themselves: g = 0.55 across sixty-four reports [Bisra et al. 2018]. Measured.

### 1.4 Help costs the expert less than the folklore says

The expertise-reversal effect is real and large as an interaction, d = 0.971 across sixty studies and 5,924 participants with no publication bias, but it is one-sided. Readers with little prior knowledge gain from assistance, d = 0.505. Readers with much prior knowledge show d = −0.428 overall, and that harm is not significant inside any single education level: secondary p = .72, higher education p = .75, vocational p = .70 [Tetzlaff et al. 2025, *Learning and Instruction*]. Much of the headline harm is an artifact of comparing populations of different education levels. Adding help for the novice is the larger effect; stripping it for the expert is the weaker half of an already student-bound finding. And there is no category for practising professionals in the meta-analysis, because there were no such studies to code. Measured.

## 2. What fell or was never tested

The same literature contradicts three things the code's neighbourhood commonly asserts, and is silent on two more. The citation audit in the sweep corrected several numbers on the way.

### 2.1 The advance organizer works a little, and not for Ausubel's reason

Advance organizers help learning and retention by about a fifth of a standard deviation: d = .21 across 135 studies, which the authors themselves frame as the average treated reader performing better than fifty-eight percent of controls [Luiten, Ames and Ackerson 1980, *American Educational Research Journal*, venue corrected by the audit]. The mechanism did not survive. Across 112 studies, the high-effect studies were not the ones Ausubel's model predicted: lower effects for written-only and abstract organizers, higher for non-subsuming ones, and aural beating written [Stone 1983]. Anyone citing Ausubel to justify an abstract summary at the top that subsumes the detail below is citing the prediction the data failed. Framing a text as an answer to a stated question does the same narrow thing: g = 0.54 for the material the question covered, g = 0.04 for everything else in the same text. Why-first, on this evidence, is an attention allocator, not a comprehension multiplier. Measured.

### 2.2 No number for size or depth

Nothing verified supports a rule about how wide or deep a unit may be, and the numbers usually cited for one invert their sources. Miller's seven reported a span, then suspected the number was only a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence, and gave chunking as the escape [Miller 1956]. Cowan's four is a capacity visible only when the experiment blocks recoding into larger chunks, conditions a re-readable hierarchy never meets, and he later restated it as three to five in young adults [Cowan 2001; Cowan 2010]. Chase and Simon's chess masters assumed Miller's span as a premise and had three subjects [Chase and Simon 1973].

The depth-and-breadth studies each found an optimum for a fixed number of items, and the optimum moved with the count. Sixty-four targets: a U-shaped curve minimised at two levels of eight, from Dwight P. Miller, not George [D. P. Miller 1981]. Sixty-four targets again: the fastest structure was four top-level items each holding sixteen, not reliably different from the next two [Kiger 1984, known only through a later review]. Five hundred and twelve items and nineteen experienced web users: the three-level eight-by-eight-by-eight was significantly slower and more disorienting than either two-level structure, and sixteen-by-thirty-two and thirty-two-by-sixteen did not differ [Larson and Czerwinski 1998]. The winning level there was thirty-two items wide. No result says where the ceiling sits at five thousand items. Measured, with the audit's corrections.

### 2.3 The redundancy effect is not about one home

The redundancy effect that cognitive-load theory documents is about identical content presented in written and spoken form at the same moment, within one working-memory episode, to twenty-five technical apprentices [Kalyuga, Chandler and Sweller 2004]. It says nothing about a fact stated in one document and restated in another read weeks apart. Pushing the other way, the high-coherence texts that helped low-knowledge readers were the more elaborated, partly restating ones [McNamara et al. 1996], and the benefit of a less coherent text for knowledgeable readers turned out to be restricted to knowledgeable readers who were poor comprehenders; skilled readers with high knowledge did better with the coherent text [O'Reilly and McNamara 2007]. One home may be right for maintenance and correctness. It is not a comprehension finding, and citing the redundancy effect for it is a misreading. Measured.

### 2.4 Status marks, and a text's structure after a week

Nothing in the advance-organizer, signalling, cognitive-load or comprehension-model literatures addresses epistemic status markers on claims; the nearest body, multiple-document sourcing, concerns the reliability of sources, not author-assigned settledness. Two further cautions. Every delayed effect of text-structure instruction across forty-four studies was non-significant [Bogaerds-Hazenberg et al. 2021], and study quality nearly halves the effect [Pyle et al. 2017]. And a meta-analysis of twenty-nine reviews and 78,177 participants found design mattering more in system-paced settings such as lectures than in self-paced ones such as websites [Noetel et al. 2021]. A reader who controls their pace recovers much of what a bad design costs. Cognitive-load theory itself survived by amputation: germane load was dropped as unfalsifiable, and five design factors are now known to raise extraneous load while improving learning [Skulmowski and Rey 2021]. Measured; the status-mark absence is seen.

### 2.5 The gap: students, short passages, fifteen minutes

The populations are junior-high and undergraduate students; the materials a few hundred to a couple of thousand words, read once in a fifteen-to-thirty-minute session; the outcomes immediate recall and multiple choice. Even the expertise-reversal meta-analysis holds three vocational studies out of sixty, and its expert is a student above the median on a pretest. Professionals reading specifications are the high-knowledge, self-paced, goal-directed corner where signalling, organizer and coherence effects shrink or reverse. Almost nothing studies a five-thousand-word specification, and nothing studies a corpus read repeatedly over months. The direction of the gap runs against any project writing for professionals. Seen: the sweep's own census of the literature it read.

## 3. Professionals reading the documents of their work

The studies that used practising lawyers, physicians, pilots and engineers are few, and they say something sharper than the student literature: expertise is a main effect, not a shield, and the failure is reaching, not reading.

### 3.1 Syntax taxes the expert as much as the novice

A corpus of about ten million words showed contracts disproportionately loaded with rare jargon, centre-embedded clauses, passives and odd capitalisation; two experiments with 184 readers found these features cut recall and comprehension even for experienced readers, centre-embedding worst [Martínez, Mollica and Gibson 2022]. Then 105 practising lawyers against 108 laypeople on identical materials: main effects of both register and legal training, and no interaction, p = .36 for recall. A further 102 lawyers rated simplified contracts equally enforceable and higher in quality, style and likelihood of a client signing [Martínez, Mollica and Gibson 2023, *PNAS*, full text read by the agent]. Domain expertise does not neutralise bad prose. Measured, and the best-designed professional-reader experiment in the sweep.

### 3.2 The failure is reaching, not reading

Thirteen healthcare professionals preparing two consults from a paper guideline could read it and could not find the patient-specific recommendation in it [Kilsdonk et al. 2016]. NASA's analysis of eleven airline quick-reference handbooks found flow lines hard to follow across pages and when nested, and jumps chained for no evident reason, and tagged the load-bearing usability questions as further research needed [Burian 2014]. The cockpit checklist guidelines that everyone cites came from 140 flight hours of observation and fifteen interviews with no experiment: subdivide a long list into function-bounded chunks, put the most critical items nearest the beginning, and where that conflicts with logical order, criticality wins [Degani and Wiener 1990]. Among sixty-one general practitioners making 12,880 decisions, vague recommendations were followed thirty-six percent of the time and clear ones sixty-seven, an association confounded with controversy and with how much routine had to change [Grol et al. 1998]. Measured or observed; none is a manipulation.

### 3.3 Preference is not comprehension

The one randomised trial of guideline format in practising clinicians gave 181 physicians across four countries a layered format or a standard narrative one. Seventy-two percent preferred the layered format against sixteen. Correct understanding was seventy-two percent against fifty-eight, p = .06, and appropriate action ninety-eight against ninety-two, p = .10 [Brandt et al. 2017, *BMJ Open*]. Strong preference, unproven comprehension gain. Sixty-five readers randomised to a narrative essay or a structured template for the same architecture documentation showed no significant association between format and understanding; prior exposure to the source code dominated [Ernst and Robillard 2023, *Empirical Software Engineering*]. Any format claim resting on practitioner preference should be graded down accordingly. Measured.

### 3.4 Reading code is non-linear, and the expert reads less of it

Fourteen novices followed the linear story order about eighty percent of the time on prose and seventy on code; six professional engineers with five to twenty-eight years' experience showed about sixty percent linearity on code, skipping intermediate lines [Busjahn et al. 2015]. The best-powered study of how developers spend time instrumented seventy-eight professionals across every application for 3,148 hours: comprehension 57.62 percent, navigation 23.96, editing 5.02; a quarter of the comprehension time was in a web browser and a tenth in a document editor; and comprehension time fell monotonically with experience, 66 percent to 56 to 44, a large effect [Xia et al. 2018, *IEEE TSE*]. Twenty professionals measured over whole workdays spent 21 percent of the day on code at all [Meyer et al. 2017]. Among 323 IBM professionals, content problems in documentation outranked presentation problems on every measure, and novices did not experience the problems differently [Uddin and Robillard 2015]. Experts read faster and are blocked by the same content defects as everyone else. Measured.

### 3.5 The checklist story

A real story, three studies long, and the sweep's clearest evidence that a document does not carry its own effect. In 2009 a surgical safety checklist introduced in eight hospitals in eight cities cut deaths from 1.5 to 0.8 percent and complications from 11.0 to 7.0, in 3,733 patients before and 3,955 after, with no concurrent control [Haynes et al. 2009, *NEJM*]. Then a hundred and one hospitals adopted the checklist under a mandate. Measured across 109,341 procedures before and 106,370 after, mortality moved from 0.71 to 0.65 percent, odds ratio 0.91, p = .13, and complications not at all [Urbach et al. 2014, *NEJM*]. Then the one randomised trial, a stepped-wedge design in two hospitals, 2,212 control against 2,263 checklist procedures: complications fell from 19.9 to 11.5 percent, length of stay by most of a day, mortality significantly in one hospital of two [Haugen et al. 2015]. Where the checklist arrived as an engaged programme it worked; where it arrived as a policy across a system it did not. The pattern is measured in each study; the reading of the chain, that implementation carries the effect, is the sweep's and is reasoned, first argued in a two-page comment with no data [Bosk et al. 2009].

## 4. Hypertext and finding things

The hypertext literature is genuinely unflattering, and its fix is hierarchy rather than fewer words. The information-architecture profession's own methods, when traced, rest on almost nothing.

### 4.1 Fewer links, and hierarchy over network

A review of the field concluded that the decision-making and visual processing that links demand impaired reading, that learning was better with fewer links, and that readers low in working memory and prior knowledge were the ones disadvantaged [DeStefano and LeFevre 2007]. With fifty-four biology teacher-trainees, network link structures produced far more disorientation than hierarchical ones, Cohen's f = 0.58, and far less coherent reading sequences, f = 1.35, hurting low-prior-knowledge readers most, in a 2020 study the sweep attributes to Amadieu and colleagues. People prefer navigating a hierarchy to searching, even with good search: hierarchy is a retrieval affordance people want, not a filing artifact [Bergman, Beyth-Marom and Nachmias 2008, *ACM TOIS*]. Conklin's cognitive overhead remains conceptual [Conklin 1987]. The specific rule that a link should name its outcome rather than its topic has no direct test. Measured, save the last.

### 4.2 Information scent is the one thread with predictive models

Information foraging theory is information architecture's strongest empirical thread: genuine predictive models of which link a reader follows, cited over a thousand times [Pirolli and Card 1999; Chi et al. 2001]. The tool built on it never shipped, and the profession's standard textbook mentions scent once, in a footnote. Faceted navigation won on recall and confidence, not speed: thirty-two students found complete recall rising from 21 to 77 percent on one collection, at ten times the seconds per step, against a keyword-search baseline rather than a hierarchy [Yee et al. 2003]; transfer to real catalogues was inconclusive. Measured, narrow.

### 4.3 Card sorting and tree testing rest on almost nothing

The chain is short. A non-peer-reviewed 2004 conference paper with one dataset, 168 employees sorting forty-six cards, contains no table of correlations; its other table shows a third of clusters still breaking at forty participants [Tullis and Wood 2004]. A 2004 blog post read the correlations off that paper's graph, and every vendor since repeats them as data [Nielsen 2004]. The single direct validity test found that the match between the elicited mental model and a site's structure has no effect on browsing performance [Schmettow and Sommer 2016, *Behaviour & Information Technology*]. The one positive study validated card sorting against a legacy architecture chosen for known problems, and has eight citations [Ntouvaleti and Katsanos 2022]. Cross-study reliability of open sorts runs from r = 0.42 to 0.93 [Katsanos et al. 2019], and the clustering step is partly an artifact of algorithm choice. Tree testing's entire empirical base is one vendor anecdote, thirty-one to sixty-seven percent success on a government site [O'Brien 2009]. The three-click rule was tested and falsified across 44 users and 8,000 clicks [Porter 2003]. Language models sort cards at agreement of about 0.73 normalised mutual information with human sorts, degrading with scale [Kuric et al. 2025]. Measured and seen; the methods are shipped and widely adopted.
