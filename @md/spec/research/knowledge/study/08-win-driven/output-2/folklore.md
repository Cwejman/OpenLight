# Numbers not to carry

These are the figures this field runs on, and what the sweep found when its agents traced each to its source. It leaves you able to drop each one knowing why, or to say it in the form its source actually supports; the safe forms are in the citation report linked at the end.

## 1. The cost of late change

The one-to-ten-to-a-hundred defect-cost ratio, attributed to an IBM Systems Sciences Institute, traces to an IBM customer-education unit in Los Angeles, a corporate training programme that ran from about 1967 to 1982 and published no study; the granular chart traces to a textbook footnote [Bossavit 2013]. Cite nothing to it. Its neighbours fell with it.

- Boehm's hundred-times curve carries its author's own qualification: we have added the word often, and for small non-critical systems the factor is more like five to one, with good architecture flattening it further [Boehm and Basili 2001].

- The largest test of the delayed-issue effect, 171 projects from 2006 to 2014, found no evidence for it and calls it a long-held truth that should not be considered a global truism [Menzies et al. 2016].

- Seventy to eighty-five percent of rework from requirements defects, and fifty to sixty percent of defects originating in requirements, reach no primary measurement; the field's own audit of fifty-seven publications found the evidence dominantly hypothesised [Frattini et al. 2023].

- The Standish CHAOS success and failure rates count overruns and ignore underruns, solicited failure stories, and do not disclose their design [Eveleens and Verhoef 2010].

- Team Topologies' twenty-five percent less context switching and twenty percent productivity gain circulate on marketing pages with no study behind them.

## 2. The bounded head

Every number cited for the size of a unit inverts or overreaches its source, per the audit that read each one.

- Seven plus or minus two: its author suspected the number was only a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence and distinguished two different spans [Miller 1956].

- Cowan's four chunks is visible only when the experiment blocks recoding into larger chunks, conditions a re-readable hierarchy never meets; restated as three to five in young adults [Cowan 2001; Cowan 2010].

- Chase and Simon assumed Miller's span as a premise and had three subjects; they did not establish a chunk limit [Chase and Simon 1973].

- Dunbar's hundred and fifty: the point estimates run from sixteen to a hundred and nine and the ninety-five percent intervals from two to five hundred and twenty, and the paper concludes that a cognitive limit on human group size cannot be derived this way [Lindenfors, Wartel and Lind 2021]; reporting the interval as sixteen to a hundred and nine understates the demolition thirty-fold.

- Two levels, three at most: nothing supports it; the measured optima were eight-by-eight at sixty-four items, four-by-sixteen, and sixteen-by-thirty-two at five hundred and twelve, every one a fixed-item optimum, and the winning breadths all exceed the working-memory numbers the rule is derived from.

- Luhmann's slips as an existence proof for shallow structure: the archive documents up to four sub-levels and a division with 120 subdivisions.

- Graphic organizers at 1.24 against expository at 0.80: in no primary source; assume fabricated or blog-laundered.

- Romer's knowledge that compounds: his own words are that unbounded growth is more like an assumption than a result of the model, and that a large population is not sufficient to generate growth [Romer 1990].

## 3. How developers spend their time

The ratios everyone repeats about reading and writing code are not measurements.

- Ten times more time reading than writing is Robert Martin's recollection of watching editor session replays in the eighties and nineties, with no instrumentation and a two-item bibliography that does not concern the ratio; and the replays he describes show scrolling and navigation, not comprehension [Martin 2008].

- Fifty percent of maintenance time on comprehension rests on a 1983 report that no index holds, that two citing papers give incompatible bibliographic records for, and that one of them describes as based on anecdotal evidences; the primary claim cannot be confirmed to exist.

- Five percent of time writing new code is a hypothetical junior persona in a 2006 blog post reproducing a 2002 email, followed by the words no, I am not making this up, and then argument, not data [Hallam 2006].

- The thirty-five percent of time understanding a program that two peer-reviewed papers cite is, in the primary text, thirty-five percent on the mechanics of navigation within and between source files [Ko et al. 2006].

- What is measured is in [the reading file](reading.md#34-reading-code-is-non-linear-and-the-expert-reads-less-of-it): fifty-eight percent comprehension of effective development time across seventy-eight professionals, and twenty-one percent of the whole workday on code at all.

## 4. What documentation buys

The payoffs claimed for documentation trace to surveys that retracted them, to figures no source holds, or to one small experiment forty years old.

- DORA's 2.4 times is real and primary from 2021; DORA's own 2022 report found a negative association and its 2023 report no effect, the second year we see this behaviour, and its 2025 report dropped the capability; the six-hundred-and-fifty-six-percent lift table exists only on a web page and a blog post.

- GitLab's six hundred and five thousand words appears in no GitLab source the sweep could reach; over two thousand printed pages is the sourced figure.

- Wikipedia's fifty-kilobyte split trigger is a starting point with a burden of proof, in readable prose, and the size rule now runs on word counts.

- Six-pagers produce better decisions traces entirely to a 2017 shareholder letter with no measurement.

- Design docs reduce defects or save rework has no study in any design; the one controlled experiment on format found none.

- Structured content reuse saves thirty to forty percent, seventy-nine percent of translation cost, positive return in six to twelve months: vendor calculators and trade books citing each other; no primary study in any scholarly index.

- Structured knowledge bases outperform unstructured by twenty-nine to forty-six points, attributed to a vendor page: the page contains no such figure.

- The Minimal Manual's forty percent less learning time is real, from nineteen people in 1987; the meta-analysis pooling it was never peer-reviewed and its authors suspect the gain is redundancy reduction.

## 5. Structure for models

The figures on both sides of the structure-for-models argument are mostly vendor illustrations, unreached studies, or effects inside the noise.

- A hundred and fifty thousand tokens to two thousand, a 98.7 percent saving: the vendor's post presents it as an illustrative example with no methodology; the mechanism is independently supported at one to two orders of magnitude less.

- Knowledge graphs make language models three times more accurate: forty-three questions, vendor-authored, a zero-shot SQL baseline, and a hand-built ontology.

- Lazy graph retrieval at 0.1 percent of the cost: indexing cost only, in a blog post about code that never shipped.

- Vector search removed from a coding agent in May 2025, and agentic keyword search at 94.5 percent of retrieval faithfulness: no primary source reached for either.

- The grep tax study of 9,649 experiments: not reached; the compact format's own benchmark shows a 0.8-point accuracy edge inside a plus-or-minus 2.8 interval.

- Tool descriptions selected 260 percent more often: a real controlled result, twenty to seventy-two percent selection among competitors, restated as relative lift, and about selection, not task success.

- Agent skills won: fifty-three thousand specifications adopted; the only controlled benchmark shows them lowering pass rates at more cost.

- Sixty thousand repositories use the agent instruction file: from a December 2025 press release, repeated unchanged eight months later; the file's effect on task success is null in the one rigorous test.

- Nine percent of temperature-zero outcomes flip between identical runs, so most single-digit effects on either side of this debate are inside the noise.

## 6. Methods of information architecture

The profession's methods are widely adopted and rest on a chain that the corrective sweep traced link by link.

- Fifteen participants give a correlation of 0.90 in a card sort: read off a graph in a 2004 conference paper that prints no coefficients, by a 2004 blog post, and repeated by every vendor since [Nielsen 2004].

- Card sorting produces a better architecture than an expert: untested in the paper that asserts it and anywhere else; the one direct test found no effect of mental-model match on browsing performance.

- Eighty percent success is a good tree-test score: no published threshold anywhere; the whole empirical base of tree testing is one vendor anecdote.

- The three-click rule: tested and falsified across forty-four users and eight thousand clicks.

- Too many cross-listed items and the hierarchy loses its value: no threshold, no study.

- Seven plus or minus two menu items: menus are recognition, not recall.

## 7. The sweep's own caution

Two of the forty-five agents independently caught automated summarisers fabricating verbatim quotations and sample sizes, one inventing twenty-two respondents in ten companies for a study of 271 observations across 122 transfers in eight firms. Several agents exhausted their search budgets and fell back to direct fetches, which over-samples sources with machine-readable endpoints and under-samples blogs and conference talks. So a figure in these files is worth what its agent's verification note says it is worth.
