# How readers actually comprehend

The code makes claims about how understanding is built — why before how, folds that stand alone, links as offers, one home per fact, confidence in the writing. The sweep pulled the cognitive-science floor and the professional-reader evidence under those claims. **The sweep ran against the code's six-rule predecessor; the mapping onto the current code is ours, and marked.** The headline: the ordering and bounding instincts hold, several justifications the project has leaned on do not, the professional evidence relocates the failure mode from comprehension to *retrieval*, and the code's actual domain — long interlinked documents read repeatedly by knowledgeable readers — is almost entirely unstudied.

## What holds

- **Where an idea sits governs what is comprehended.** Meyer's levels effect: relations at the top third of a text's structure dramatically influence comprehension; the same relations placed low barely register. The code's why-first opening is supported as *placement*, with a correction below on what it buys.

- **Bounded units help** (segmenting: 56 investigations, small-to-medium effects) — though the measured mechanism is learner-controlled pacing, not conceptual self-containment; the self-containment case comes obliquely from hypertext research: units whose comprehension requires following links out are worse.

- **Hierarchy rescues links; networks disorient.** Link-driven decision-making impairs reading; hierarchical structures consistent with the domain largely fix it, network structures don't, and low-prior-knowledge readers are hurt most (d ≈ 2.5 in the sharpest study). Fewer links, predictably placed, beat many.

- **Signal words and connectives are the cheapest supported intervention** (d ≈ 0.32–0.39 across meta-analyses, strongest for low-knowledge readers) — and the code currently says nothing about them.

- **Narrative beats exposition by the largest, most robust margin in this literature** (>33,000 participants, unmoderated). The code's felt-case-first instinct has this behind it.

- **Prompting the reader to generate** (self-explanation, g ≈ 0.55) works; documents can carry such prompts; the code doesn't contemplate the text asking anything of the reader.

## What is corrected

- **Why-first is an attention allocator, not a comprehension multiplier.** Prequestions produce g = 0.54 on exactly the material they name and g = 0.04 on everything else in the same text. Stating the purpose steers what lands; it does not lift the rest.

- **Abstract openings lose to concrete ones.** Ausubel's advance-organizer *effect* survived meta-analysis weakly (d ≈ 0.2); his *mechanism* — subsumption under a more abstract frame — is what the data specifically failed to confirm, and graphic/concrete organizers beat abstract prose ones. An opening should be a felt case or a picture, not a higher abstraction. (The code's rebuild moved this way; the evidence says keep going.)

- **"Delete a section that only sets up other sections" would delete the two instructional elements with replicated support** — pre-training and organizers are exactly setup-only sections. Front-load the load-bearing relations, yes; ban setup, no.

- **One-home is a maintenance rule, not a comprehension finding.** The redundancy effect concerns identical content in two channels *simultaneously*, within one working-memory episode — it says nothing about a fact restated in a document read weeks apart. Distributed restatement is the mechanism behind spacing, one of the most robust effects known; and restatement at a coarser abstraction (what the code permits) is the part with actual backing. Keep single-source for correctness; don't cite psychology for it.

- **Under-explaining does not help your best readers.** The reverse-cohesion effect, corrected by its own literature: minimal coherence helps *knowledgeable readers who are poor comprehenders*; skilled, knowledgeable readers do better with high-cohesion text. No licence to write tersely-to-the-point-of-gaps for experts.

## What has no evidence either way

Status/confidence marking (nothing in these literatures addresses epistemic labels — unevaluated, not contradicted); any split criterion or size rule for a fold (7±2 and Cowan's 4 are misapplied as length prescriptions — [`folklore.md`](folklore.md)); the link-phrasing rule (outcome-before-topic is a plausible extrapolation from signalling, untested); whether skippability substitutes for audience-forked prose (untested, both directions).

## The professional evidence

- **Retrieval, not comprehension, is the failure mode.** Physicians could read the guideline but couldn't *find* the applicable recommendation; pilots face flow-lines across pages and unexplained jumps. Aviation and medicine independently converge: for long documents read repeatedly, navigation architecture is the dominant variable.

- **Expertise is a main effect, not a shield.** Practising lawyers (n = 105, preregistered) show no expertise × register interaction — complexity taxes experts as much as laypeople; they just start higher. Experts read less linearly (eye-tracking) and spend monotonically less time comprehending (66% → 44% of instrumented work time across experience levels, the best-powered finding here) — that is what expertise buys — but they are blocked by the same content defects. And **expertise reversal has zero field studies in professionals**; "strip guidance because your readers are experts" is the weak half of a student-bound finding.

- **Specificity correlates with use** (vague recommendations followed 36% vs clear 67% — confounded but large, in real decisions), and **preference is not comprehension**: the one randomized guideline-format trial found 72%-vs-16% preference for the layered format with a non-significant comprehension gain. Grade any format claim resting on reader satisfaction accordingly.

- **What professionals' time actually goes to:** instrumented, comprehension is ~58% of working time (editing 5%), and much of that reading is documentation and the web, not code. The circulating ratios are folklore ([`folklore.md`](folklore.md)).

- **The IA methods are not a foundation.** Card sorting and tree testing rest on one non-peer-reviewed dataset, numbers read off a graph, and one vendor anecdote — and the single direct validity test found *no effect* of mental-model-matched structure on browsing performance. What IA does have: information scent (its strongest thread), faceted browsing's recall gains (against keyword search, at 10× the interaction cost), and the finding that people actively prefer navigation to search — hierarchy is a wanted affordance, not a compromise. Menu studies at fixed item counts favour wide-and-shallow (winning breadths 8–32) but license no universal depth rule.

## The gap that decides how to hold all of this

The populations are students; the materials are 500–2,000-word passages read once; the outcomes are immediate recall. Delayed effects are null essentially everywhere they were measured. And the meta-meta-analytic moderator runs *against* the project: design matters most for complex material under system pacing, least for self-paced expert readers — which is exactly who reads a spec. **Nothing in these literatures studies a 5,000-word specification, let alone an interlinked corpus read over months.** The code's domain is unstudied; the cognitive floor bounds and corrects it but cannot validate it. Its tests — the senior reader with no unanswerable questions, the cold-open session — have to be its own instrument, and the sweep's strongest methodological finding agrees: the writer's closure is invisible from inside, and only a fresh head finds it.

## Not established

Longitudinal reading of professional documents over months (no study anywhere); eye-tracking of physicians reading guidelines (zero PubMed records); any evidence on epistemic-status labels; the exact Luiten effect size at source.
