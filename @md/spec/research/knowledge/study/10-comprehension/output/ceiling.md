# Where better writing stops helping

The organisational literature is older and better designed than anything in the documentation lane, and it sets a ceiling on what any document can do. The ceiling is not set by how well the document is written. It is set by who is receiving it, what relationship they stand in, and what a document structurally cannot do that a conversation can. It also holds the strongest measured pro-writing result in the material, because a ceiling is not a dismissal.

## 1. The barriers are knowledge-related, not motivational

The measurement is Szulanski's, from 271 returned questionnaires spanning 122 transfers of best practice in eight companies. The barriers that dominated were the recipient's lack of absorptive capacity — their ability to take the knowledge in — followed by the causal ambiguity of the knowledge itself, and then how arduous the relationship between source and recipient was. Motivation ranked low, and the author's own reading is that fixing incentives alone is "inadequate or misled."

Three caveats travel with it and are visible in the paper. The headline analysis used only 87 of the 271 observations, raised to 142 in a robustness run that preserved the rank order. The method gives no significance indication for individual weights, by the author's own footnote. And there has been no true replication in thirty years. So the top-three ranking is robust and everything below rank three is not. *Measured once, well, and never replicated.*

Note what "arduous relationship" means in his gloss: the relationship is the conduit, and it governs the recipient's ability to acquire the knowledge when needed. A contract, a procurement gate or a ticket queue is an arduousness generator.

## 2. Absorption is a property of the receiver, and weakest where this applies

The underlying construct is older: a firm's ability to use external knowledge depends on prior related knowledge. Absorption is a function of what the receiver already knows, not of how well the sender writes.

The construct replicated and is heavily moderated, and the moderation matters here more than the effect. A 2025 meta-analysis finds effects roughly twice as large in low-technology industries as in high-technology ones — which means the ceiling this brief describes is at its weakest exactly where a software project sits. *Measured, replicated, heavily moderated.*

The consequence is still structural rather than editorial. An organisation that has outsourced away the people who understood a thing has, by construction, reduced its own capacity to absorb writing about it, and no amount of writing recovers it.

## 3. Three kinds of boundary, and only two are writable

Carlile's distinction is the sharpest tool available. A syntactic boundary is a difference of vocabulary, and a glossary fixes it. A semantic boundary is a difference of meaning, and translation fixes it. A pragmatic boundary is a difference of interests, and it requires transformation: both sides give something up.

That predicts where documentation improvements will systematically underperform expectations — at contractual boundaries, where the parties want different outcomes. A client and a delivery vendor differ not merely in terms or in meanings but in interests, and no shared vocabulary closes that. *Framework with case-study support; the best modern empirical work behind it is one practitioner study.*

## 4. Why a document cannot repair a misunderstanding

There is a clean account of this, and it is worth holding as a scene rather than as a principle.

Two people talking do not produce perfect utterances. They produce good-enough ones and repair them: a puzzled look, a "wait, which one?", a correction three seconds later. That loop is cheap, and because it is cheap, neither party spends effort on precision they do not need. The exchange minimises the effort of the pair, not the effort of the speaker.

Now take the loop away. A document can be reviewed and revised freely, and it offers nothing at all of the two things that make repair cheap: the reader is not there at the same time, and cannot signal while the writer is still speaking. So a misunderstanding is invisible at the moment it happens, cannot be repaired then, and compounds silently. And since the reader is also minimising the effort of the pair, the cheapest repair available to them is not to reread more carefully. It is to go and ask someone.

*Theory with laboratory support, not a field test of organisational handover.* It is nonetheless the clearest account of why a document has a ceiling that better writing does not raise.

## 5. What people do when the document fails

The one large onboarding study — 411 professional developers plus version-control data across eight product groups — asked in two ways and got two answers. Given a list of twelve factors, lack of proper documentation was the strongest. Asked to name factors freely, developers put mentorship first and documentation second. Both are opinion data rather than measured effects, and the paper obscures its time units, so there is no absolute effect size.

One much larger datum points the same way: in a survey of 49,000 developers, when an AI tool fails them, 75.3% go to a person rather than to documentation. *Measured as a survey; self-report.*

## 6. Better writing does help, and here is the best evidence that it does

The strongest measured document-design result in the whole material is forty years old. Carroll's minimal manual — stripped of preamble, organised around the tasks a learner actually wants to do — cut learning time by 40% and let learners accomplish 2.7 times as many subtasks as the conventional manual, in the original experiments, and replicated once at a large effect.

Two things in it cut against the way it is usually cited. The meta-analysts' own inference is that the plain cutting of verbiage accounts for nearly the whole effect, rather than the task-orientation theory built on top of it. And Carroll himself put "minimalism means brevity" first in his list of misconceptions about it. *Measured, N = 19 and N = 32 in the original experiments; the meta-analysis of the line was never peer-reviewed and two outliers carry much of the effect.*

## 7. What cannot be claimed

There is no causal study of documentation against an organisational outcome: no randomised trial, no quasi-experiment, no interrupted time series with documentation as the manipulated variable. Saying that plainly is stronger than citing correlations that have since been withdrawn.

The scope is easy to overstate: individual-task experiments do exist, and section 6 above is the best of them. What is missing is the organisational link, not every link.

So what a structure can plausibly do is reduce friction where two parties differ in vocabulary or in meaning. There is no evidence it closes a boundary of interest, none that it substitutes for mentorship, and good evidence that it decays unless something downstream consumes it.
