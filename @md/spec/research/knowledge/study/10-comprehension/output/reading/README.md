# What a reader is measured to get

There is a real science under document design, and it is smaller, older and narrower than the practice that cites it. This folder holds what survived checking: two bounds that govern every figure, three effects that replicate, the most-cited principle inverting, the absence where the length and depth numbers should be, and what changes when the reader is a professional rather than a student.

Effect sizes are reported throughout as correlations (*r*) or as standardised differences (*d* or *g*), which say how far apart two groups are in units of their own spread. As a rough scale, *r* ≈ .1 or *g* ≈ 0.2 is small, *g* ≈ 0.5 moderate, *g* ≈ 0.8 large. Nothing in this literature is large.

## 1. Two bounds to hold before any figure

Design effects shrink when the reader controls the pace, which is what reading a document is, as against sitting through a lecture. And in the one meta-analysis that tested delay, every delayed effect was non-significant. So the text-design figures in sections 2 to 4 are immediate effects, measured on a captive reader, usually a student, on a passage of a few hundred to a couple of thousand words read once. Section 5 is the separate literature on professionals at work, and it is where that limit is tested.

That is not a reason to discount the findings. It is a reason to treat them as the floor under a set of writing rules rather than as a validation of one. *Measured; both bounds come from meta-analyses of the same literature.*

## 2. Three effects replicate, and two costs sit against them

Cue words that announce structure — "the problem is", "in contrast", "as a result" — help comprehension across 27 studies, and a second meta-analysis of 29 studies finds the effect larger for readers who know less about the subject. Breaking material into units the reader paces themselves is supported across 56 investigations, at a measured cost in increased learning time. And stories beat expository prose by the largest margin anywhere in this literature, across 78 samples and more than 33,000 participants, better understood and better recalled — with the caveat that the sweep never opened that paper and took its numbers from a list of leads. *Signalling and segmenting measured; narrative's direction reported by an agent that read the paper's abstract, its size taken from an unopened lead.* Narrative is the largest of the three and signalling the cheapest to apply; the two are closer in size than their raw figures suggest, because they are reported on different scales, and converted to one scale narrative is something like half again as large rather than several times.

Two costs replicate against them: detail that interests without serving reliably harms learning, and links impose a real tax on the readers least able to pay it. The second comes with the finding that makes it manageable, and that finding is the nearest thing here to a measured argument for a hierarchy over a graph. [What the effects actually say](effects.md) holds the figures, the two costs, and how to write for a reader who already knows.

## 3. The most-cited principle does not work the way it is cited

The dominant design principle in this field is Ausubel's advance organizer: open with something more abstract than the body, so that it subsumes what follows. That is specifically the part its own meta-analyses failed to confirm. Organizers do help, at a small effect — the source frames it as the average treated subject scoring better than 58% of untreated ones, which is a weak effect expressed flatteringly — but the analysis of 112 studies found the high-effect studies violating the prediction, with *lower* effects for abstract and for written-only organizers and *higher* effects for organizers that did not subsume.

This is the one place the reading evidence speaks directly to the code's first claim, and how far it reaches has to be argued rather than assumed. The nesting is the shape the theory describes — the understanding at the top is the one everything beneath stands under. The opening is not, because the code asks for the opposite of an abstraction there: say what the brief gives, in the reader's terms, in small. So the finding warns against one way of writing the top of a brief, and the code already instructs against that way; the fuller argument is in [what the evidence did to each claim](../verdict.md) §1. What the evidence positively supports is that an opening should be concrete — a scenario, a case, a picture. *Measured; the pattern is the meta-analysts' own finding about their own literature, and the reach into the code's claim is this piece's reasoning.*

The separate claim that the governing relation should nonetheless sit at the top rests on an older result: in Meyer's 1975 work, the way ideas are related to each other near the top of a text's structure strongly influenced what readers comprehended and recalled, while the same relations low in the structure barely did. *Measured once, in 1975, and not part of the organizer literature.*

## 4. The numbers for length and depth are not there

No study in this literature identifies an optimal section or document length. The figures routinely cited for it are misapplications. Miller's seven was two different spans in a 1956 paper whose author suspected the number was "a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence," and he named recoding into larger chunks as the escape. Cowan's four is visible only when the experiment blocks that recoding, which a browsable, re-readable structure does not do.

The depth-and-breadth experiments cannot answer it either, and the reason is structural: each fixed the total number of items and varied the shape of the tree over it, so the optimum moves with the item count by construction. The winning breadths were wider than the memory numbers the "keep it narrow" rule is derived from — eight and sixteen in two different studies at 64 items, which disagreed with each other, and thirty-two at 512. The one thing all three agree on is negative and useful: at a fixed item count, adding a third level cost time and cost orientation. *Measured; three studies, each at its own fixed item count of 64 or 512, and none able to speak to a corpus of thousands.*

Something close to that shallow ceiling appears on the machine side, where one level of routing helps and a second gives no benefit and sometimes hurts. The model result has its home in [what models do with what we write](../structure/models.md) §8; the convergence is suggestive rather than evidence, since neither study was designed against the other.

Where the real constraint sits is better located by the disorientation evidence in [what the effects actually say](effects.md) §2: it is not how many items sit at a level, it is whether the reader can tell where they are.

## 5. Professionals, and the lever with the worst evidence

Where professionals have actually been observed — lawyers with contracts, physicians with guidelines, pilots with checklists, developers with code — expertise turns out to be a main effect rather than a moderator. It makes every reader better and buffers nobody against bad prose, and the expertise-reversal effect everyone cites has no professional evidence at all. The dominant failure in a long professional document is finding the applicable part rather than understanding it, and that lane also produced the only direct design prescription anywhere in the sweep. [How professionals actually read](professionals.md).

Those two findings do not sit comfortably together, and the discomfort is the point. Finding is where the failures happen, and the discipline that exists to solve finding has the thinnest evidence base in this whole material — its two canonical methods rest on a non-peer-reviewed conference paper and a vendor blog post, and the single most direct validity test returned a null. So the most important lever has the worst-supported methods. [What is known about finding](finding.md).
