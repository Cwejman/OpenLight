# What the effects actually say

These are the figures under the three effects that replicate, the two costs that replicate against them, and how to write for a reader who already knows the subject. All of it is carried from one agent's read of the primary meta-analyses, with effect sizes taken from source where the publisher allowed. The notation is glossed in [the folder's entry](README.md).

## 1. The three that hold

Signalling is the cheapest supported intervention in the literature. Richter, Scheiter and Eitel (2016), 27 studies and 45 comparisons over 2,464 participants, report r = .17 for comprehension and transfer, moderated by prior knowledge. Alpizar and colleagues (2020), 29 studies over 2,726 participants, report retention d = 0.39, comprehension d = 0.32 and transfer d = 0.33, larger for readers with low prior knowledge. The mechanism identified is that explicit signalling words switch weak readers out of reading a text as a list and into reading it as a structure.

Segmenting is supported across 56 investigations and 88 pairwise comparisons, at a small-to-medium effect for retention and transfer, with reduced cognitive load and increased learning time. Note what the authors identify as the mechanism: learner-controlled pacing, not the conceptual self-containment of each segment. That matters for any rule that a part should stand alone, because the measured benefit is in letting the reader stop, not in what stopping leaves them holding.

Narrative is the largest and most robust finding in the area. Mar, Li, Nguyen and Ta (2021) report g = 0.55 overall and g = 0.72 for memory across 78 samples and 33,078 participants, with the effect "robust… not moderated by various study characteristics." *Paper-only meta-analysis; these effect sizes reached the sweep through its coverage chart — a survey of leads the project held before the sweep, none of them opened — rather than through the agent's own read of the paper, which reported only the direction.*

One further effect is worth knowing because most sets of writing rules never contemplate it: worked examples cut learning time roughly sixfold in the original study, with the gain bounded to problems of the form shown. It reappears in the one design prescription this material contains, in [how professionals actually read](professionals.md) §6.

## 2. The two costs, and the one that has a fix

Detail that interests but does not serve reliably harms learning, across 68 effects. The operative variable is relevance, not concreteness — the literature calls the harmful kind a seductive detail, and the test is service rather than vividness — a concrete story that carries the understanding is not one, and a vivid aside is. That is the boundary any use of narrative has to respect.

Links impose a real tax. The decision-making and visual processing demanded by hypertext impairs reading performance, learning is better with fewer links, and the readers most disadvantaged are those with low working memory and low prior knowledge. *Measured; the source is a 2007 review of the hypertext literature.*

Shape changes the size of that tax, and this is the finding to carry. A study of 54 teacher-trainees found network structures producing far more disorientation than hierarchical ones, F(1,50) = 17.23, p < .001, and much less coherent reading sequences, hurting low-prior-knowledge readers most. So links cost, and a hierarchy is what makes them affordable — the nearest thing this literature has to a measured argument for a holarchy over a graph, and the clearest support in it for anything the code claims. *Measured once, 54 participants, one domain.*

## 3. Writing for a reader who already knows

The result usually cited for "write less for experts" is McNamara and colleagues (1996): low-knowledge readers benefit from coherent text while high-knowledge readers benefit from minimally coherent text, because poor writing forces them to infer the connections.

O'Reilly and McNamara (2007) added the third variable and changed the conclusion. The low-cohesion benefit was restricted to less-skilled readers who happened to have high knowledge; skilled comprehenders with high knowledge did better with the high-cohesion text. Deliberately under-explaining does not help your best readers. It helps knowledgeable readers who are poor at comprehension. *Measured, peer-reviewed.*

That bears on one thing the code does not do and the practice literature sometimes recommends: writing two versions of the same material for two kinds of reader. The evidence does not support forking, and it does not test the alternative either — see [the missing studies](../unknown.md) §4.

## 4. Naming the question up front buys attention, not comprehension

A preregistered meta-analysis found g = 0.54 across 97 effects for material that the prequestions covered, and g = 0.04 across 91 effects for everything else in the same text. Framing a document as the answer to a stated question improves learning of exactly what you named and does nothing for the rest.

That bears on the rule that a brief should open by saying what it gives, and it bears on it in a limited way: the opening buys attention for what it names, and the rest of the text is unaffected. *Measured, preregistered; the extension from prequestions to an opening paragraph is an inference, not a result.*

## 5. What the field itself is worth

Three cautions belong to the literature as a whole rather than to any figure in it. Cognitive load theory survived by amputation, dropping one of its three categories of load in response to the charge that the theory could not be falsified. Disfluency as a desirable difficulty — the idea that making text harder to read helps — is refuted by direct replication. And the discipline this all sits in returned a 36% replication rate in its largest replication effort.
