# How people take in written knowledge

There is a real science under document design, and the sweep read it at source. What it establishes is narrower than the practice that cites it, the effects are small, and almost all of it was measured on students reading short passages once. This folder holds what survived checking, what did not, and the gap between the population studied and the population a specification is written for.

## 1. The three things that hold, and what bounds them

Two bounds govern every figure in this folder, so take them first. **The design effects shrink in self-paced reading** — which is what reading a document is, as against sitting through a lecture. And **the effects that were tested on delay went null**, in the one meta-analysis that tested delay. Everything below is an immediate effect measured on a captive reader.

With that held: signalling, segmenting and narrative are the effects that replicate. Cue words that announce structure ("the problem is", "in contrast", "as a result") carry r ≈ .17 for comprehension and transfer across 27 studies, moderated by prior knowledge; a separate meta-analysis of 29 studies finds the effect *larger* for readers with low prior knowledge. Breaking material into units the reader paces themselves is meta-analytically supported across 56 investigations, at a small-to-medium effect and at a measured cost in **increased learning time**. And stories beat expository prose by the largest margin anywhere in this literature — better understood *and* better recalled, across 75+ samples and 33,000+ participants, unmoderated by study characteristics.

Against those sit two well-evidenced costs. Detail that interests but does not serve reliably harms learning, because readers remember the detail instead. And links impose a real tax: the decision-making and visual processing of hypertext impairs reading, learning is better with *fewer* links, and the readers hurt most are exactly those with low working memory and low prior knowledge.

[`text-design.md`](text-design.md) holds the effect sizes, the principles that did not survive, and the six things this literature is never asked about.

## 2. The one that inverts

The most-cited design principle in the field — open with something more abstract than the body, so it subsumes what follows — is specifically the part its own meta-analyses failed to confirm. Advance organizers do help, at d = .21, which the source itself frames as the average treated subject beating 58% of controls. But Stone's analysis of 112 studies found the high-effect studies violated Ausubel's predictions: *lower* effect sizes for abstract and written-only organizers, *higher* for non-subsuming ones, and aural beating written.

The reading that survives is the opposite of the usual one. Put the governing relation at the top, because where an idea sits in the hierarchy is load-bearing for what gets comprehended. But make the opening concrete, graphic, or a scenario — not an abstraction.

## 3. The numbers that are not there

No study in this literature identifies an optimal section or document length. The numbers routinely cited for it — seven plus or minus two, Cowan's four, two levels and no more than three — are all misapplications, and each is retired in [`folklore.md`](../folklore.md). What the depth-and-breadth experiments actually found, and why every one of their optima is an artefact of a fixed item count, is in [`bounded-head.md`](bounded-head.md).

## 4. The readers nobody studied

The generalisation gap runs against anyone writing for professionals, and beyond the two bounds in §1 it runs one way more. The coherence effect reverses for knowledgeable readers — and then the reversal itself is confined to knowledgeable readers who are *poor* at comprehension, while skilled high-knowledge readers do better with the coherent text.

Where professionals have actually been observed — pilots with checklists, physicians with guidelines, lawyers with contracts, engineers with code — the findings are more useful and more deflating. That lane also produced the sweep's only direct design verdict, and it tells against format and layering depth as levers. [`professionals.md`](professionals.md) holds them, and the verdict is its §11.

## 5. Being found before being read

Information architecture is the practice of arranging knowledge so a reader can reach the part they need, and it has its own evidence base, which is worse than the comprehension literature's. Its two canonical methods rest on a non-peer-reviewed 2004 conference paper and a blog post that invented the numbers everyone quotes, and the single most direct validity test returned a null. [`finding.md`](finding.md) holds it.
