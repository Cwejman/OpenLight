# What the bounded-head studies actually support

Every argument for keeping a piece of writing small eventually reaches for a number — seven, four, two levels, fifty kilobytes. The sweep chased each of those numbers to its primary source. This brief holds why none of them can be quoted flat, and then the formulations the sources will bear. What they do *not* support is retired in [`folklore.md`](../folklore.md).

## 1. Why every depth optimum is an artefact

Read this before the formulations below, because it governs all of them.

The depth-and-breadth experiments each fixed the total number of items and then varied the shape of the tree over it. D. P. Miller — not George Miller, a confusion the citation chain repeats — fixed 64 targets; Kiger fixed 64; Larson & Czerwinski fixed 512. **The U-curve's minimum therefore moves with the item count by construction**, and none of the three can say anything about where the optimum sits at 5,000 items or 50,000.

Read together they also refute the working-memory framing they are usually deployed to support. The winning breadths were 8 at 64 items, 16 at 64, and 32 at 512 — every one of them larger than the memory numbers the "keep it narrow" rule is derived from. Kiger's fastest condition, 4×16, was *narrow then wide*; his subjects nonetheless preferred 8×8. Hold that one loosely: **Kiger's own text could not be obtained** — no abstract in the citation databases, the publisher returning 403, the commonly cited identifier wrong — so the account is Larson & Czerwinski's secondary description, which adds that the 4×16 winner was "followed closely, and **not reliably different** from" the next two conditions. In Larson & Czerwinski's own study the three best conditions were likewise not reliably different from each other.

The one thing all three agree on is negative and useful: adding a third level, at a fixed item count, cost time and cost orientation.

The memory numbers carry their own conditions, and they are strict. Cowan's limit is visible only when recoding into larger chunks is experimentally *blocked*, and a browsable, re-readable hierarchy meets none of those conditions. Miller's span was two different spans, and he named recoding as the escape. Chase and Simon assumed a span rather than measuring one.

## 2. The safe formulations

With §1 held, these are the claims the primary sources will bear, written so they can be quoted directly.

Cowan (2001) argues for "a single, central capacity limit averaging about four chunks," observable only when experimental conditions block recoding into larger chunks; he later restates it as 3–5 items in young adults. It was a *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* target article — published with open peer commentary, contested by design.

Miller (1956) reported a span of about seven items but suspected the number "is only a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence," distinguished the span of absolute judgment from the span of immediate memory as "quite different kinds of limitations," and named recoding into chunks as what raises effective capacity.

Chase and Simon (1973), studying three players, found that chess masters' recall advantage disappeared on randomly arranged boards, and framed their chunk counts inside Miller's already-assumed span.

D. P. Miller (1981) found goal-acquisition time over 64 targets to be U-shaped across depth, minimised at two levels of eight — at that item count and no other.

Larson and Czerwinski (1998), with 19 experienced web users and 512 items, found the three-level 8×8×8 structure significantly slower and more disorienting than either two-level structure, with no reliable difference between 16×32 and 32×16.

Lindenfors, Wartel and Lind (2021) report 95% confidence intervals of 4–520 and 2–336 for human group size and conclude that "a cognitive limit on human group size cannot be derived in this manner."

## 3. Where the real ceiling is

The disorientation evidence locates the cost better than the capacity numbers do: hierarchical link structures consistent with the domain produce far less disorientation than network structures, and the readers hurt most by a network are those with low prior knowledge. So the constraint that binds is not how many items sit at a level; it is whether the reader can tell where they are. The figures are in [`text-design.md`](text-design.md) §2.

That is consistent with what professionals report when observed. Across aviation and medicine independently, the failure mode in long documents is retrieval, not comprehension: practitioners can read the guideline and cannot find the applicable recommendation. See [`professionals.md`](professionals.md).

## 4. The existence proofs do not prove what they are used for

Two artefacts are routinely cited as showing that knowledge naturally forms a shallow pyramid, or that a large flat handbook scales. Neither does.

**Wikipedia's class distribution** — 4.0 million stubs, 2.87 million start, 52,696 good articles, 9,195 featured — "measures *assessment coverage*, not a designed pyramid." Most stubs are stubs because nobody expanded them.

**GitLab's handbook** is real at over 2,000 printed pages and genuinely handbook-first, and **its own style guide argues against depth**: "Keep your handbook pages short and succinct… the biggest challenge cited by new employees is the vast amount of information to take in." The artefact's own maintainers name size as the problem.

## 5. What is not measured at all

No study in this literature identifies an optimal document or section length. Wikipedia's "50 kB" is a starting point with a burden of proof attached, corrected in [`folklore.md`](../folklore.md). Nobody has tested whether allowing a reader to skip a level substitutes for writing two versions of the prose for different readers — the adaptivity literature recommends varying assistance by reader knowledge, and the skip-instead-of-fork alternative is simply untested. The one verdict the sweep carries on the forked half is a practitioner's preference rather than evidence, and it is in [`folklore.md`](../folklore.md) §9.
