# What document design is measured to do

This is the cognitive-science floor under any set of writing principles: which text-design effects replicate, how large they are, and which widely-held principles the evidence specifically contradicts. It comes from one agent's read of the primary meta-analyses, with effect sizes taken from source where the publisher allowed.

Read it holding one caveat that applies to every figure below. The populations are school students and undergraduates; the materials are passages of a few hundred to a couple of thousand words, read once in a fifteen- to thirty-minute session; the outcomes are immediate recall, multiple choice, and "transfer" measured minutes later. Almost nothing here studies a five-thousand-word specification, and nothing studies a corpus read repeatedly over months.

## 1. The effects that replicate

**Signalling.** Richter, Scheiter & Eitel (2016), 27 studies, 45 comparisons, N = 2,464: r = .17 [.11, .22] for comprehension and transfer, moderated by prior knowledge. Alpizar et al. (2020), 29 studies, 44 effects, N = 2,726: retention d+ = 0.387, comprehension d+ = 0.317, transfer d+ = 0.327 — larger for low-prior-knowledge readers. Meyer's structure-strategy work identifies explicit signalling words as the cue that switches weak readers out of a list strategy into structural processing. This is the cheapest supported intervention in the literature.

**Segmenting.** Rey, Beege, Nebel, Wirzberger, Schmitt & Schneider (2019), 56 investigations, 88 pairwise comparisons: significant effect, small-to-medium for retention and transfer, reduced cognitive load, increased learning time. Note the mechanism the authors identify — learner-controlled *pacing*, not conceptual self-containment.

**Narrative.** Mar, Li, Nguyen & Ta (2021), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*: **Hedges *g* = 0.55 overall and *g* = 0.72 for memory, across 78 samples and 33,078 participants**; stories are better understood and better recalled than essays, and the effect is "robust… not moderated by various study characteristics." This is the largest and most robust finding in the whole area. *Paper-only meta-analysis; the effect sizes come from the sweep's coverage chart rather than from the agent's own read of the paper, which reported only the direction.*

**Self-explanation prompts.** Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi & Winne (2018), 64 reports, 69 effects: inducing self-explanation yields g = 0.55. A document can carry such prompts; most sets of writing principles never contemplate the text asking anything of the reader.

**Worked examples.** Sweller & Cooper (1985) found worked examples cut learning time roughly sixfold and errors to about a fifth — and the gain did not survive variation in the problem, so the effect is bounded to problems of the form shown. *Paper-only, named in the coverage chart rather than fetched.*

**The levels effect.** Meyer (1975): the type and structure of relations among concepts "dramatically influenced comprehension when they occur at the top third of the structure," while the same relations low in the structure "affected comprehension minimally." Where an idea sits is load-bearing for what is comprehended and recalled.

## 2. The costs that replicate

**Seductive details.** Sundararajan & Adesope (2020), 68 effects: irrelevant-but-interesting material reliably harms learning. The operative variable is *relevance*, not concreteness — a concrete story that serves the understanding is not a seductive detail; a vivid aside is.

**Link load.** DeStefano & LeFevre (2007), *Computers in Human Behavior* 23:1616–1641: "the increased demands of decision-making and visual processing in hypertext impaired reading performance"; learning is better with fewer links; readers low in working memory and prior knowledge are the ones disadvantaged. Conklin's "cognitive overhead" (1987) is the conceptual ancestor and remains conceptual.

**But structure rescues it.** A 2020 study of 54 biology teacher-trainees found network structures produced far more disorientation than hierarchical ones — F(1,50) = 17.23, p < .001, Cohen's f = 0.58 — and much less coherent reading sequences (f = 1.35), hurting low-prior-knowledge readers most (d = 2.47 against 1.58). Hierarchy is not a compromise against the network; it is the thing that makes links affordable, and it is the nearest thing this literature has to a measured argument for a holarchy over a graph.

## 3. Where the design effects shrink

Noetel et al. (2021), *Review of Educational Research* — a meta-meta-analysis of 29 reviews, 1,189 studies, 78,177 participants — reports the moderator that matters most here: "Good design was more important for more complex materials, and in **system-paced** environments (e.g., lectures) than **self-paced** ones (e.g., websites)." A reader who controls their own pace recovers much of what bad design costs.

Bogaerds-Hazenberg et al. (2021), *Reading Research Quarterly*, 44 studies: immediate effects of text-structure instruction are g = 0.25 comprehension, 0.38 recall, 0.58 summarisation — and **all delayed effects are non-significant**. Pyle et al. (2017), 21 studies, 4,254 students: high-quality studies d = 0.70 [0.42, 0.98] against low-quality d = 1.13, so study quality nearly halves the effect.

## 4. The coherence question, stated correctly

McNamara, Kintsch, Songer & Kintsch (1996) found that low-knowledge readers benefit from coherent text while high-knowledge readers benefit from *minimally* coherent text, because poor writing forces compensatory inference — and that the benefit appears at the level of the situation model rather than the textbase. This is the result usually cited for "write less for experts."

O'Reilly & McNamara (2007) added the third variable and it changes the conclusion. The low-cohesion benefit was **restricted to less-skilled, high-knowledge readers**; skilled comprehenders with high knowledge did better with the *high*-cohesion text, and the interaction was confined to text-based questions. Deliberately under-explaining does not help your best readers. It helps knowledgeable readers who are poor at comprehension.

## 5. The outcome this project cares about has a name, and no measurement

A test like *decide a case the text does not cover* maps onto a named construct in this literature: Kintsch's **situation model**, as against the textbase — the difference between having reconstructed what the text is about and having understood its surface.

That mapping is useful twice. It is the right theoretical target: McNamara et al. (1996) found that "the rewards to be gained from active processing are primarily at the level of the situation model rather than at the superficial level of text-base understanding." And it is where the measurement stops. The situation model has only ever been operationalised as inference questions and sorting tasks on short passages, typically with school students or undergraduates — never as professional judgment on a document read repeatedly over months. So the construct exists, the payoff of effortful reading is located in it, and nobody has measured it for the population or the materials that matter here.

## 6. Prequestions buy attention, not comprehension

A preregistered meta-analysis found g = 0.54 (k = 97) for material the prequestions covered and **g = 0.04 (k = 91) for everything else in the same text**. Framing a document as the answer to a stated question improves learning of exactly what you named, and does nothing for the rest. "Why before how" is an attention allocator, not a comprehension multiplier.

## 7. What the principles miss

Six things are absent from most written sets of principles and present in the evidence: signal words and connectives, which are the cheapest lever there is; genre, where the narrative advantage is the largest effect in the field; prompting the reader to generate; adaptivity to reader knowledge as an explicit design variable; durability, since every delayed effect measured was null; and reader goals, since relevance instructions reliably reshape what is encoded.

Three further things have no evidence at all and should be held as unevaluated rather than supported: marking a claim's epistemic status in the text; the idea that a reader who stops early has been paid ("prefix usability"); and the rule that a link should announce what following it *gives* rather than its topic, which has no direct experimental test and is a plausible extrapolation from the signalling literature rather than an established result. Nothing in the advance-organizer, signalling, cognitive-load or comprehension-model literatures addresses any of them.

One of the three has a nearest literature worth chasing rather than none. Multiple-document comprehension and sourcing (Britt, Rouet, Bråten) finds that readers who attend to a claim's *source* build better situation models — but it concerns attribution and source reliability rather than author-assigned settledness labels, and the agent that named it did not verify it. *Unverified lead, not evidence.*

## 8. The redundancy effect does not say what it is used to say

One rule is routinely justified by the redundancy effect: say a fact in exactly one place, because copies compete. The evidence does not support that, and the agent that read it returned a flat verdict — **contradicted as justified by cognitive load theory, and no evidence as a comprehension rule.**

What the redundancy effect measures is narrow. Kalyuga, Chandler & Sweller (2004, *Human Factors*, three experiments, 25 technical apprentices) showed that presenting *identical* content in written and spoken form **simultaneously**, inside one working-memory episode, degrades learning. It is a claim about concurrent channels under time pressure. It is not a claim about a fact appearing authoritatively in one document and being restated in another read weeks apart.

Two things push the other way. McNamara's high-coherence texts — the ones that won for low-knowledge readers — *were* the more explanatory, more elaborated, partly restating ones. And distributed restatement is the mechanism behind spacing, one of the most robust effects in learning science.

The one carve-out with backing is restatement **at a coarser level of abstraction**, which is roughly what the advance-organizer literature endorses, since an organizer is a higher-level restatement that measurably helps.

So: a single-source discipline may be excellent for maintenance and correctness — a fact with one home changes in one place and every pointer stays true — and that is a claim about *drift*, not about comprehension. Citing the redundancy effect for it is a misreading.

## 9. One principle the evidence contradicts outright

Deleting a section that exists only to set up other sections runs directly into Mayer's **pre-training principle** — which is exactly a segment that names and characterises components before the process is explained, and which appears among the meta-analytically supported multimedia principles. Advance organizers are the same shape. Applying the deletion rule literally would remove two instructional elements the evidence supports.

## 10. The state of the field itself

Cognitive load theory survived by amputation: Sweller dropped germane load as a separate category in response to the unfalsifiability critique, and Skulmowski & Rey (2021) document five design factors that *raise* extraneous load while still improving learning — a live challenge to the theory's central prescription. Disfluency-as-desirable-difficulty is refuted: a direct replication returned null and a 2025 multiverse analysis found no replicable moderation by working memory. And the whole field sits inside a discipline whose 2015 large-scale replication rate was 36%.

One lead is named as the most likely place a recent negative finding is hiding: a 2025 *Educational Research Review* meta-analysis searching Mayer's own multimedia research for boundary conditions, which the agent could not reach. *Unfetched; chase it before relying on §1.*
