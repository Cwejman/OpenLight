# What moves knowledge between people

One agent was sent to the organisational literature, and it came back with better-designed studies than anything in the documentation lane and a conclusion that bounds them. This is where the ceiling on what a document can do is set — not by how well it is written, but by who is receiving it and what relationship they stand in.

## 1. The barriers are knowledge-related, not motivational

Szulanski's result, across 122 transfers of best practice in 8 firms, is the opposite of the folk story. The barriers that dominated were the recipient's lack of **absorptive capacity** (canonical weight 0.54), **causal ambiguity** (0.34), and an **arduous relationship** between source and recipient (0.33). Motivation ranked low, and his data says fixing incentives alone is "inadequate or misled."

Insight fails to travel not mainly because people will not share, but because the receiver cannot absorb, the sender cannot explain, and the channel between them is painful. [`transfer.md`](transfer.md) holds this and the frameworks around it.

## 2. Structure predicts more than any artifact

On Windows Vista — 3,404 binaries, over 50 million lines — organisational metrics predicted failure-proneness at **86.2% precision and 84.0% recall**, beating code churn, complexity, dependencies, coverage and pre-release bugs. Conway's Law has real teeth, and the boundary you draw in the org chart is the one you later pay to cross in the code.

That finding sits alongside the onboarding evidence, where documentation ranks second behind mentorship in what developers name freely, and the maintenance finding that governs every document in this corpus: **prescriptive artifacts get maintained, descriptive ones do not.** [`structure.md`](structure.md) holds them.

## 3. The concession a documentation project has to make

There is **no causal study of documentation against an organisational outcome**, and saying that plainly is stronger than citing correlations that have since been withdrawn. The scope matters and is easy to overstate: a handful of true experiments *do* manipulate documentation and measure an individual task outcome ([`../writing/evidence.md`](../writing/evidence.md) §§2 and 7). What is missing is the organisational link, not every link; the precise form of the claim is in [`structure.md`](structure.md) §5.

What a documentation structure can plausibly do is reduce friction where two parties differ in vocabulary or in meaning. There is no evidence it can close a boundary of *interest* — where the parties want different outcomes — no evidence it substitutes for mentorship, and good evidence that it decays unless something downstream consumes it. The ceiling is set by the receiver's absorptive capacity and by whether the artifact is load-bearing.
