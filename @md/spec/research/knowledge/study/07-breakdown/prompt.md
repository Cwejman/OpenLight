## The pattern under test

One shape recurs through that document. A section opens with a slim paragraph — two or three sentences — and then hands the reader a list of bold-led bullets. It is the only place bullets appear anywhere in the piece: never mixed into the middle of a section, never followed by more prose that builds on them. It happens at every level, under `##` headings and `###` headings alike, in most sections that carry a body at all.

Read that shape for what it is. The opening paragraph is a surface. Each bullet under it is a whole gain — a finding that changes what the reader holds. So the section is already a brief with nested briefs inside it; the nested briefs simply never got their headings.

The cost is the reading gradient. Every gain arrives at one depth, in one serving, in the order the bullets happen to stand. The reader is force-fed exactly where the code asks the wins to be graded, and a reader who stops after the surface leaves holding almost nothing, because the surface announces the section rather than giving it.

This is not a new rule. The code already says a bullet past a few sentences is a brief and a brief gets a heading, and already asks each brief to open with its why. The exercise is to apply that harder than the 06 writer did, and to find out what the result costs and what it buys.

## Instruction

Rework the document so that every one of those cases is broken down one step further. Two moves, together:

- **The wins come up.** What each bullet actually gives — the finding, the correction, the thing that changes what the reader holds — belongs in the brief's surface, written as one organic piece of prose that carries the gains together and shows how they bear on each other. A reader who reads only that surface should leave holding the section's gains, not a promise of them.

- **The remainder goes down.** What is left after the win is lifted out — the chase, the numbers, the names, the sample sizes, the caveats, the sourcing — is what the nested briefs hold, one step below, each under its own heading, for the reader who wants it.

Two things to hold while you do it:

You are moving what is there and rewriting the prose that carries it — not researching. Do not open the raw reports the document links to; do not fetch anything. If a nested brief comes out thin because the source document does not hold enough to fill it, leave it thin and report it.
