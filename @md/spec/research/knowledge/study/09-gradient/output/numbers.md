# The numbers you may no longer cite

Every figure the material behind the code leaned on was chased to its source by one agent working only from primary texts, and a second set fell in other reports of the sweep. Read this and you will know, for each, what the source actually says and what sentence may be used in its place. Each entry gives the number once; the other parts of this debrief point here rather than repeat it. The grades are the agents'; nothing was checked again.

## 1. The head holds more than the numbers say

Five numbers about what a head can hold have been used to set how much a brief may carry and how deep a holarchy may go. None of them says that. Each was measured under conditions a reader of a document never meets, and the studies of depth against breadth found their best shape at widths larger than any of the numbers. Reasoned, from the five entries below.

Seven plus or minus two is the number its own author disowned. [Miller 1956] reported a span of about seven items, distinguished the span of absolute judgment from the span of immediate memory, noted that recoding into chunks raises capacity, and closed by suspecting the number "is only a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence." Measured, from the paper. It supports no rule about items on a page.

Cowan's four is a limit seen only when chunking is blocked. [Cowan 2001] argues for "a single, central capacity limit averaging about four chunks," observable under four conditions that stop the reader recoding items into larger ones; his 2010 restatement is three to five items in young adults, and the paper was a target article published for open dispute. Measured, from the abstract. A navigable, re-readable hierarchy meets none of the four conditions, so citing four as a ceiling on categories inverts the paper.

Chase and Simon assumed the span; they did not find it. [Chase and Simon 1973], studying three players, found that a chess master's recall advantage disappeared on randomly arranged boards, and framed their chunk counts inside Miller's already-assumed seven plus or minus two. Measured, from the paper, N equals three.

The depth-and-breadth studies each found an optimum at a fixed item count, and none supports "two levels, three at most." [D. P. Miller 1981], not George Miller, found goal-acquisition time over sixty-four targets U-shaped across depth, minimised at two levels of eight. [Kiger 1984], read through Larson and Czerwinski's account since the paper itself was unreachable, found four top-level items each holding sixteen fastest, and not reliably different from sixteen by four or eight by eight. [Larson and Czerwinski 1998], with nineteen experienced web users and 512 items, found the three-level eight by eight by eight structure significantly slower and more disorienting than either two-level structure, with no reliable difference between sixteen by thirty-two and thirty-two by sixteen. Measured. Every winning breadth, eight, sixteen, thirty-two, exceeds the working-memory numbers the rule was derived from, and none of the studies says where the ceiling sits at five thousand items.

Dunbar's 150, the supposed limit on a person's stable relationships, has confidence intervals thirty times wider than the correction usually quoted. [Lindenfors, Wartel and Lind 2021] report 95% intervals of 4 to 520 and 2 to 336 for human group size and conclude that "a cognitive limit on human group size cannot be derived in this manner." Measured. The material behind the code carried the intervals as 16 to 109, which mistakes the two point-estimate ranges for the intervals.

## 2. Late is costly, by no known factor

The cost numbers say that understanding late is expensive and that most of a developer's time goes to reading what others wrote. The direction of both is established; every multiplier attached to them traces to no study, and the one large test of the first found no effect at all. Reasoned, from the entries below.

One to ten to a hundred traces to a training unit, not a study. The "IBM Systems Sciences Institute" was a customer and employee education unit, active roughly 1967 to 1982; Bossavit, the author who catalogued software engineering's unsourced numbers, chased it and found no study, no data and no N behind it, and the granular chart of 1x, 6.5x, 15x, 100x traces to a textbook footnote. Measured, by the chase. [Boehm and Basili 2001], in their own words, added the word "often" to the hundredfold claim and wrote that for small non-critical systems the factor "is more like 5:1 than 100:1," with good architecture flattening it further. And [Menzies, Nichols, Shull and Layman 2016], over 171 projects, "found no evidence for the delayed issue effect." Paper-only, both. The direction is established; every multiplier is soft, and the largest test found none.

Its siblings fall with it. "Seventy to eighty-five percent of rework is due to requirements defects" and "fifty to sixty percent of defects originate in requirements" reach no primary measurement; a review of 57 requirements-quality publications [Frattini et al. 2023] found the evidence for impact "dominantly hypothesized" and "anecdotal or folkloric." The project-failure rates of the Standish Group's CHAOS reports count overruns and ignore underruns, with undisclosed design. "Team Topologies delivers a 25% reduction in context switching," a claim for a book on team design, circulates on marketing pages with no study behind it. The "30 to 40% reuse saving" and "up to 79% reduction in translation costs" claimed for DITA, the XML standard for technical writing, trace to vendor calculators and consultancies citing each other. Folklore, all.

Ten hours reading for one writing is one author's memory of watching editor playbacks. Martin's *Clean Code* [Martin 2008] says "the ratio of time spent reading vs. writing is well over 10:1," and the preceding paragraphs give its provenance: Emacs sessions replayed in the 1980s and 1990s, in which "the vast majority of the playback was scrolling and navigating." No instrumentation, no N, and the chapter's bibliography has two entries, neither about the ratio. Measured against the book's full text. What was measured instead is in [the readers brief](readers.md#4-how-a-professional-reads).

"Programmers spend half their time on comprehension" rests on a paper nobody has read. [Fjeldstad and Hamlen 1983] has no DOI, is absent from every index, and its two citing papers give incompatible bibliographic records for it, the signature of a reference copied rather than consulted; Xia et al. say outright that the sources for the figure report it "based on anecdotal evidences." Peter Hallam's "five percent writing new code" is the number for a hypothetical junior persona in a 2006 blog post reproducing a 2002 email, with no measurement. And the "35 percent understanding" attributed to [Ko et al. 2006] is, in the paper, 35 percent on "the mechanics of navigation within and between source files." Folklore, or mis-cited.

DORA's 2.4x is real and retracted. DORA is the annual survey of software delivery performance published by Google; its 2021 report says teams with quality documentation are 2.4 times more likely to see better delivery performance, from seven agree-or-disagree items, snowball sampling and correlation-based modelling with no intervals. The 2022 report's own "Surprises" page says documentation "negatively impacted software delivery performance"; the 2023 report finds "no effect" and calls it the second year running; the 2024 report says "we didn't do longitudinal studies or a proper experiment"; the 2025 report drops documentation quality as a capability and declines to speak of effects at all. The widely quoted percentage lifts, 656% and 1525%, appear only on a web page and a blog, not in any report. Measured, from the PDFs. The multiplier may be cited only with the retraction attached.

## 3. The newest folklore is vendor arithmetic

The numbers about structure and models are the newest folklore: vendor illustrations, tiny benchmarks and readings off a graph, each restated downstream as a measurement. Each has a real mechanism or a real experiment somewhere behind it, at a fraction of the size. Reasoned, from the entries below.

"150,000 tokens to 2,000, a 98.7% saving" is an illustrative hypothetical. The Anthropic post gives a Google Drive to Salesforce example with no methodology, conditions or validation. The mechanism it illustrates, making tools navigable rather than present, is supported elsewhere at one to two orders of magnitude smaller effect, and [the models brief](models.md#2-what-helps-a-model) gives those studies. Stated for the figure, paper-only for the mechanism.

"Knowledge graphs make LLMs three times more accurate" is 43 questions against a deliberately weak baseline. [Sequeda, Allemang and Jacob 2023]: 16.7% for zero-shot text-to-SQL, 54.2% over a hand-authored ontology with mappings, vendor-authored, no schema linking on the baseline. Paper-only. It is evidence for a human-built semantic layer, not for a graph a model extracted.

"LazyGraphRAG at 0.1% of the cost" is a blog number about code that never shipped, and it concerns indexing only. It was announced by Microsoft Research in November 2024 as a cheaper successor to its GraphRAG project, promised as "the next top priority" in December 2024, never released, and the parent project went into maintenance mode in August 2026. Announced, then folklore.

"Structured content outperforms unstructured by 29 to 46 points for AI" was attributed to a page by the documentation vendor Heretto that contains no such figure; its only statistic is that 80% of business information begins unstructured, sourced to a topic index page. Folklore.

"Standard-compliant tool descriptions are selected 260% more often" is a real experiment restated as a relative lift: 20% to 72% selection probability among competing tools, which measures selection, not task success. Paper-only, framed as folklore.

Nielsen's card-sorting correlations were read off a graph. Card sorting is the method of asking users to group labelled cards so that a site's categories follow their grouping; the figures 0.75 agreement at five participants, 0.90 at fifteen and 0.95 at thirty appear nowhere in the text of [Tullis and Wood 2004], whose Figure 1 has error bars and no printed values and whose only recommendation is that more than twenty to thirty participants "may not be cost effective." Measured, from the recovered PDF. Every downstream repetition treats the graph reading as published data.

Three claims about proposal processes have nothing behind them. "KEP fatigue," the weariness said to surround Kubernetes' enhancement proposals, has no primary measurement. "Requests for discussion demonstrably work at Oxide," the hardware company that publishes its design process, rests on employee assertion with no metric published. "The IETF is three to four times slower than in 1998" traces to a single sample of twenty RFCs. Folklore, all three. The staleness counts that do exist were computed by the agent and are in [the documentation brief](documentation.md#5-records-of-decisions-are-written-once-and-left).

## 4. The counts that point the other way

These counts were carried with the right source and the wrong detail, and several point the other way once the detail is right. Reasoned, from the entries below.

Luhmann's Zettelkasten, the slip-box behind the case for small linked notes, is a counter-example to shallow structure, not evidence for it. The archive reports about 90,000 slips over about forty-five years, not forty; with 108 divisions in the first collection, up to four sub-levels in the second, one division of 120 subdivisions, and "without one being able to say that it is a strictly hierarchical or systematic relationship." Measured, from the archive's own description.

GitLab's handbook is "over 2,000 pages" printed, by its own page; the figure of about 605,000 words appears in no GitLab source the agent could reach. Its own style guide argues against depth: "Keep your handbook pages short and succinct… the biggest challenge cited by new employees is the vast amount of information to take in." Measured for the pages; unverified for the words.

Wikipedia's 50 kilobytes is a starting point, not a trigger, and the rule has moved to words. Summary style still says "50 kilobytes of readable prose (8,000 words) is the starting point at which articles may be considered too long," with the burden of proof on the splitter; the size rule now runs on word counts, over 8,000 may need dividing, over 15,000 almost certainly, and applies to readable prose, not markup. Measured, snapshot of 2026-08-26. The assessment counts, four million stubs against nine thousand featured articles, measure how much has been assessed, not a designed pyramid.

Pimentel et al.'s notebook percentages are on the wrong denominator when quoted against 1.4 million. Of 1,159,166 unique notebooks from 264,023 repositories, 863,878 valid Python notebooks with unambiguous execution order were attempted; 24.11% ran without error and 4.03% reproduced the stored outputs. Measured. The paper's own diagnosis is missing dependencies, hidden state and absolute paths, not the medium.

Advance organizers are a small effect whose mechanism failed. An advance organizer is Ausubel's term for a short passage read before a text, written more abstractly than the text so the reader can hang the detail on it. [Luiten, Ames and Ackerson 1980], in the *American Educational Research Journal* not the *Review*, meta-analysed 135 studies and found a mean effect of .21 on learning, the average treated subject beating 58 percent of controls, with spoken organizers beating written ones. [Stone 1983] analysed 112 studies and found that "levels of some variables in the high-effect-size studies were not as predicted by Ausubel's model": lower effects for written-only and abstract organizers, higher for ones that do not sit above the text as a more general frame. Measured, meta-analysed. The figures 1.24 for graphic and 0.80 for expository organizers appear in neither paper nor any primary source. Folklore.

Romer's compounding is an assumption, and his knowledge is excludable. [Romer 1990] models technology as "a nonrival, partially excludable good" whose instructions, once created, "can be used over and over again at no additional cost," and writes that "unbounded growth is more like an assumption than a result of the model" and that "having a large population is not sufficient to generate growth." Measured, from the paper. The material behind the code cited it as proof that knowledge compounds with contributors.

DeChurch and Mesmer-Magnus is about teams, and it is 65 studies. [DeChurch and Mesmer-Magnus 2010] meta-analysed 231 correlations from 65 studies across 3,738 groups, finding a corrected .38 between a team's shared understanding and its performance; it says nothing about documents. Measured. The material behind the code carried it as 231 studies.

"Three clicks" was tested and falsified. [Porter 2003], 44 users, 620 tasks, over 8,000 clicks: "no more likelihood of quitting after three clicks than after 12." Measured. "Seven plus or minus two menu items" misapplies a limit on recall to a task of recognition. And "developers waste 17.3 hours a week on maintenance" is a self-estimate from a 2018 Harris Poll of a thousand developers, with no sampling frame or intervals published. Partly traceable.
