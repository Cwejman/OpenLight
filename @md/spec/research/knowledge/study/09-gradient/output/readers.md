# What is known about a reader

The code rests on claims about how a reader takes in prose: why before how, a whole unit that stands alone, a link that says what it gives, one home for a fact, a stopping point that pays. Read this and you will know which of those the science of reading supports, which justifications behind them fell, how a professional actually reads a long document, and how far any of the evidence reaches from a classroom to a specification read over months. The grades are the agents'; nothing was checked again, and the numbers this stands on are corrected in [the numbers brief](numbers.md).

## 1. Where things sit is what is backed

The code's shapes with direct support are the ones about where things sit, the hierarchy and the top of it, not anything about how much a part may hold. Two supported levers, signalling words and a story, the code names lightly or not at all. Reasoned, from the paragraphs below.

A hierarchy beats a web of links, and the cost of links falls hardest on the reader who knows least. [DeStefano and LeFevre 2007] reviewed the hypertext literature and concluded that the decision-making and visual processing links demand impaired reading, that learning was better with fewer links, and that readers low in working memory and prior knowledge were the ones disadvantaged. A 2020 study of 54 biology teacher-trainees found network structures produced far more disorientation than hierarchical ones, f of 0.58, with less coherent reading sequences, f of 1.35, hurting low-prior-knowledge readers most. Paper-only, both. This is the best-evidenced of the code's shapes, and it is the shape of the holarchy: predictable, hierarchical, few links out.

Where an idea sits in the structure decides whether it is understood. [Meyer 1975] found that the relations among concepts "dramatically influenced comprehension when they occur at the top third of the structure," while the same relations low in the structure "affected comprehension minimally"; this is known as the levels effect. Paper-only, long replicated. It is the gradient's ground, and it supports the entry paragraph carrying the governing relation more than it supports any rule about how deep a structure may go.

Signalling words are the cheapest supported intervention, and the code does not mention them. [Richter, Scheiter and Eitel 2016], 27 studies, found r of .17 for comprehension and transfer, moderated by prior knowledge; [Alpizar et al. 2020], 29 studies, found effects around d of 0.32 to 0.39, larger for low-prior-knowledge readers. Meyer's later work names the cue: "the problem is," "in contrast," "as a result" switch weak readers out of a list strategy into structural processing. Paper-only, meta-analysed. A brief that says which relation it is making, before it makes it, is following this.

A story is read faster and remembered longer than the same content as statements, and the effect is the largest and most robust in the whole literature. [Mar, Li, Nguyen and Ta 2021], more than 75 samples and 33,000 participants, found stories better understood and better recalled than essays, "robust… not moderated by various study characteristics." Paper-only, meta-analysed. What moderates it is relevance: details that interest but do not serve the point cost the point, because readers remember them instead. The code's principle on story is the one it most under-uses.

A purpose stated first buys attention, not comprehension. A preregistered meta-analysis of prequestions, questions posed before a text, found g of 0.54 for material the questions covered and g of 0.04 for everything else in the same text. Paper-only, meta-analysed. Why-first improves learning of exactly what the why names and nothing else, which is an argument for a contract that names the win precisely, not for a preamble.

Bounded units are backed, but for a reason the code does not give. [Rey et al. 2019], 56 investigations, found a small-to-medium effect of segmenting a text on retention and transfer; the mechanism is the learner controlling their own pace, not the unit being conceptually self-contained. Paper-only, meta-analysed. On size there are no numbers at all: no study identifies an optimal section or document length, and the working-memory spans are not that number.

Prompting the reader to explain to themselves works, and no principle asks the text to ask anything of the reader. [Bisra et al. 2018], 64 reports, g of 0.55 for induced self-explanation. Paper-only, meta-analysed.

## 2. The justifications that fell

The material behind the code justified four of its rules by reasons the evidence contradicts: an opening more abstract than the body, one home for a fact as a rule of reading, deleting a section that only sets up others, and writing less coherently for expert readers. The code kept two of the rules and dropped two; none of the contradictions touches the gradient itself, only the reasons. Three further rules are simply untested. Reasoned, from the paragraphs below.

An opening that is deliberately more abstract is the part of Ausubel the evidence specifically failed to confirm. The advance-organizer effect, a short more abstract passage read first, is small and real, d of .21; Stone's finding is that the high-effect studies did not match Ausubel's predicted levels, with concrete and graphic organizers beating abstract prose ones. Meta-analysed; the detail is in [the numbers brief](numbers.md#4-the-counts-that-point-the-other-way). A contract that opens with a concrete situation is the version the evidence favours.

One home for a fact is not a comprehension finding, and the redundancy effect the material cited for it does not apply. [Kalyuga, Chandler and Sweller 2004], three experiments, 25 apprentices, showed that identical content in written and spoken form at the same moment, in one working-memory episode, degrades learning. It says nothing about a fact stated once in one document and restated in another read weeks apart. Pulling the other way, McNamara et al.'s high-coherence texts, the more explanatory and partly restating ones, won for low-knowledge readers, and distributed restatement is the mechanism behind spacing, one of the most robust effects in learning. Paper-only. The code grounds one home in drift, three copies quietly becoming three rules, which is the right ground; the only comprehension-backed carve-out is restatement at a coarser level, which is what an organizer is.

Deleting a section that only sets up other sections contradicts two supported elements. Mayer's pre-training principle is exactly such a section, naming and characterising components before the process, and it sits among the meta-analytically supported multimedia principles; advance organizers are the same shape. Paper-only, meta-analysed. What is supported is front-loading the load-bearing relations, not deleting what prepares the reader.

Writing less coherently for experts does not help the best of them. [McNamara, Kintsch, Songer and Kintsch 1996] found high-knowledge readers benefit from minimally coherent text because it forces inference; [O'Reilly and McNamara 2007] added that this held only for less-skilled high-knowledge readers, while skilled comprehenders with high knowledge did better with the coherent text. Paper-only. It is not a licence to under-explain for experts.

Text-structure effects can vanish on delay, and design matters least where documents live. [Bogaerds-Hazenberg et al. 2021], 44 studies, found immediate effects of g 0.25 to 0.58 and all delayed effects non-significant. [Noetel et al. 2021], 29 reviews and 78,177 participants, found "good design was more important for more complex materials, and in system-paced environments (e.g., lectures) than self-paced ones (e.g., websites)." Paper-only, meta-analysed. A reader who controls their own pace recovers much of what bad design costs.

Marking status in place, phrasing a link by what it gives rather than what it is about, and a stopping point that pays are unevaluated. Nothing in the comprehension, cognitive-load or signalling literatures addresses markers of confidence on claims, and no test of link phrasing exists; both are plausible extrapolations from signalling. The levels effect is consistent with a reader who stops early having the most, but is not a test of it. Open, not contradicted.

## 3. Expertise buys speed, not immunity

Expertise buys speed and no immunity. The finding that experts are harmed by help has never been shown in a professional, bad prose taxes lawyers as it taxes laypeople, and what practitioners prefer is not what they understand. Reasoned, from the paragraphs below.

Expertise reversal is real, asymmetric, and absent from every professional population. Expertise reversal is the finding that help which aids a novice hinders an expert. [Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters and Brod 2025], 60 studies, 176 effects, 5,924 participants: low-prior-knowledge learners gain from assistance at d of 0.505, high-prior-knowledge learners lose at d of −0.428, interaction d of 0.971, no publication bias. But the harm to experts is non-significant within every education stratum, and the moderator table has no category for practising professionals because there were no such studies; the nearest proxy, vocational training, is three studies with an interval spanning 2.4 standard deviations. Paper-only, meta-analysed. "Strip guidance because the readers are experts" is the weaker half of a student-bound finding.

Bad prose taxes experts as much as novices. [Martínez, Mollica and Gibson 2023], two preregistered experiments, 105 and 102 practising lawyers against 108 laypeople: register and legal training both raised recall and comprehension, with no interaction, so training lifted the whole curve and did not buffer against complexity; centre-embedded clauses hurt recall most; and the lawyers rated simplified contracts equally enforceable and higher in quality. Paper-only, the best-designed professional-reader experiment in the sweep. Expertise is a main effect, not a shield, which is the ground under the code's rule that the words are the ones everyday speech uses.

Experts read faster and are blocked by the same defects. Comprehension time falls with experience in the largest instrumented study, [below](#4-how-a-professional-reads), while three professional studies find experience does not moderate which documentation defects block a reader. Measured. What expertise buys is speed, not immunity.

Preference is not comprehension. [Brandt et al. 2017] randomised 181 practising physicians to a multilayered or a standard narrative guideline format: 72% preferred the layered format against 16%, while correct understanding moved from 58% to 72% at p of .06 and appropriate action from 92% to 98% at p of .10. Paper-only, the only randomised format trial in professionals, and a null on the outcome that matters. Any format claim resting on practitioner preference should be graded down accordingly.

## 4. How a professional reads

The instrumented studies say a professional spends most of the working day understanding rather than writing, reads out of order and more so with experience, and fails with a long document at finding the right part, not at understanding it once found. And where a document was deployed at scale, the document did not carry the effect; how it was introduced did. Reasoned, from the paragraphs below.

Comprehension is most of the work, and much of it is not reading code. [Xia et al. 2018], 78 professional developers over 3,148 instrumented hours: comprehension 57.6% of effective working time on average, falling with experience from 66% to 56% to 44%; navigation 24%; editing 5%; and of comprehension time, 27% in browsers, 20% in IDEs, 10% in document editors. [Meyer et al. 2017], 20 professionals over about eleven workdays each, measured the whole day: reading, editing and navigating code together 21%, the rest email, browsing, documents and meetings. Measured, both, and not in conflict, since the denominators differ. [Minelli et al. 2015], the source of the "70% comprehension" figure, had 18 developers, most of the data from three PhD students, and defined comprehension as every gap over one second, which the authors themselves say "could be mini interruptions unrelated to development." Measured, fragile.

Reading is non-linear, and less linear with expertise. [Busjahn et al. 2015]: fourteen novices followed linear order about 80% on prose and 70% on code; six professional engineers about 60% on code, with more eye movements skipping lines. Measured, six experts. The eye-tracking base rate across the field is 28 student studies to 6 professional, typical N of 10 to 30.

Where professionals fail with a long document is finding, not understanding. [Kilsdonk et al. 2016], thirteen healthcare professionals preparing consults from a paper guideline: they could read it and could not find the patient-specific recommendation. [Burian 2014], NASA's analysis of eleven cockpit quick-reference handbooks, found flow lines hard to follow across pages, conditional sets easier when marked as a set, and chains of jumps for no evident reason, and tagged the usability questions that matter as "Further Research Needed." [Grol et al. 1998], 61 GPs and 12,880 decisions: vague recommendations followed 36% of the time, clear ones 67%, confounded with controversy and required change. Measured or observational, all three. Aviation and medicine independently converge on navigation architecture as the dominant variable for a document read repeatedly.

The document does not carry the effect; the implementation does. The surgical safety checklist cut mortality from 1.5% to 0.8% in eight engaged hospitals [Haynes 2009], before-after with no control; had no effect across 101 hospitals under mandate [Urbach 2014], odds ratio 0.91; and halved complications in the only randomised trial, two hospitals [Haugen 2015]. Measured. Where checklists are introduced as an engaged programme they work; where mandated across a system they do not.

## 5. How far the evidence reaches

The gap between the evidence and a professional reading a specification is severe, and it runs against the code. Populations are junior-high to undergraduate; the most professional meta-analysis codes its expert as a student in the upper half of a pretest split. Materials are a few hundred to a couple of thousand words, read once, in one session; almost nothing studies a 5,000-word specification, let alone an interlinked corpus read over months. Outcomes are immediate recall and multiple choice minutes after reading. Professionals reading specifications are the high-knowledge, self-paced, goal-directed readers, precisely the corner where signalling shrinks, organizers shrink, coherence effects reverse or vanish, and design matters least. Reasoned, by the agent, from the studies above.

The field's own replication record limits every effect here. The Open Science Collaboration replicated 36% of psychology findings; cognitive load theory survived by giving up one of its three categories of load when it was shown unfalsifiable; [Skulmowski and Rey 2021] list five design factors that raise the load the theory says to minimise and still improve learning; and the claim that harder-to-read text helps by making the reader work is refuted by direct replication. Paper-only.

Information architecture's own methods rest on a null result. Card sorting's sample-size numbers were read off a graph, [as the numbers brief says](numbers.md#3-the-newest-folklore-is-vendor-arithmetic); the one direct validity test, [Schmettow and Sommer 2016], found "the match between mental model and website structure has no effect on browsing performance"; cross-study reliability of open sorts runs from r of 0.42 to 0.93; the categories you get depend partly on the clustering algorithm; and tree testing, the method of asking users to find things in a bare category tree, has one vendor anecdote as its entire empirical base. Paper-only, peer-reviewed for the null. What the field does have is information scent, the model of readers following cues that smell of their goal, which is well cited and never shipped as a tool; and faceted browsing, which won on recall and confidence against keyword search, not against a hierarchy.

## 7. What could not be established

The agents could not establish the exact effect sizes of [Luiten, Ames and Ackerson 1980], [Sundararajan and Adesope 2020] or [Rey et al. 2019] at source; any evidence on markers of confidence, on a stopping point that pays, or on documents longer than a few thousand words; any eye-tracking of physicians reading guidelines; any study of lawyers' reading process; or any longitudinal study of professionals re-reading a long document.
