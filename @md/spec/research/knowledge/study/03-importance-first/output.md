# What Holds

*Research register — structured, uncommitted. One reading of the 45 raw reports from the [2026-08-26 web sweep](../../../sweep-2026-08/README.md), written from the raw alone. Figures carry the verification notes their agents attached; where an agent did not check a number at its source, that is said in the sentence. Two cautions from the sweep travel with everything here: agents ran out of search budget and leaned on open-access sources, and two agents caught automated summarisers inventing quotes and sample sizes — so a figure is worth what its note says, no more.*

Four understandings sit at the centre of this corpus. Everything else in it takes its meaning from them. They are stated first, plainly, and the rest of the piece hangs from them — so that wherever you stop, what you have read holds together on its own.

1. **The famous numbers of this field were never measured.** The claims everyone builds on — cost curves, reading ratios, sample-size rules, documentation multipliers — trace to training-course slides, one man's memory, and figures read off a graph. Almost nothing in the whole space is a real experiment.

2. **Where real measurement exists, the win is in finding, not in reading.** For skilled human readers and for models alike, what moves outcomes is whether the right piece can be found and named — not how well the middle of the text explains itself.

3. **The receiver sets the ceiling, and use sets the lifetime.** Knowledge fails to travel mainly because the receiver cannot absorb it, not because the writing is bad. And a written thing stays alive only while something downstream consumes it.

4. **Text won the body; structure won the edges.** Every measured winner of 2025–26 keeps the content as plain text and puts types only on the edges — names, addresses, permissions, versions, sources. Every loser typed the body.

Each is unpacked below. After them come the people and lineages of the wider world — who mean more once the four are held — and then the working detail for building, folded toward the end.

---

## 1. The famous numbers were never measured

This is the finding that changes how every other finding reads. The sweep ran a dedicated citation-verification pass, and several other agents ran their own traces. The pattern repeated in every lane.

- **"A defect costs 1x in design, 10x in development, 100x in production."** The usual source is an "IBM Systems Sciences Institute study." Laurent Bossavit's trace, which the sweep's agent followed and confirmed, found that this institute was an IBM training school in Los Angeles, active roughly 1967–1982. It ran courses. It published no study. There is no data and no N. The chase is public in [the Morendil gist](https://gist.github.com/Morendil/ebfa32d10528af04e2ccb8995e3cb4a7) — worth reading whole, because it shows what a real trace looks like. Even the curve's own author walked it back: Boehm and Basili wrote in 2001, in their own words as read from the primary PDF, that the factor is "more like 5:1 than 100:1" for small non-critical systems, and that good architecture flattens it further. A separate study of 171 projects (Menzies and colleagues, 2016; verified at source by one agent, unreachable for another) found no delayed-issue effect at all.

- **"Developers spend ten times more time reading code than writing it."** Verified against the full text of *Clean Code*: Robert Martin's sentence — "the ratio of time spent reading vs. writing is well over 10:1" — rests on his memory of replaying Emacs editing sessions in the 80s and 90s. No instrumentation, no count. And what he watched was scrolling, not reading. The best real measurement is Xia and colleagues (2018, read from the primary PDF): 78 professional developers, 3,148 instrumented working hours. Comprehension took 57.62% of working time and editing 5.02% — but over a quarter of that "comprehension" happened in web browsers, reading documentation and forums, not code. Experience helped in the plain way: the most experienced spent 44% of time comprehending, the least experienced 66%.

- **"Fifteen users are enough for a card sort (r = 0.90)."** These numbers come from a 2004 Nielsen article. The sweep recovered the primary paper it cites (Tullis & Wood 2004) and found it contains no table of correlations at all — the famous figures were read off a graph and have circulated as published data for twenty years. Worse for the method itself: the one direct validity test, a peer-reviewed 2016 study, found that matching a site's structure to the card-sorted mental model had **no effect on browsing performance**. The most widely used method in information architecture rests on a graph reading, and its one real test came back empty.

- **"Teams with good documentation are 2.4x more likely to perform well."** That is DORA 2021, and it is a real number in the report — from a self-reported, snowball-sampled survey with no confidence intervals. The sweep read all five reports. DORA 2022 found documentation *negatively* associated with delivery performance and called it a surprise. 2023 found "no effect" and said so twice. 2025 dropped the documentation construct entirely and stopped speaking of "effects" at all. Anyone citing 2.4x today is citing a finding its own authors have since reversed, nulled, and retired.

- **The requirements-cost family** ("70–85% of rework comes from requirements defects," and its siblings) was formally audited: a review of 57 publications ([Frattini and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.10355), read at source) found 47.5% of claimed impacts merely hypothesised, 92% of impacted activities picked ad hoc, and the authors calling the evidence "anecdotal or folkloric." The Standish CHAOS failure rates were dismantled by Eveleens and Verhoef in 2010: overruns counted, underruns ignored, failure stories solicited.

- **The small-numbers psychology is misused too.** Miller himself called 7±2 "only a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence" — the sweep confirmed the quote in the 1956 paper. Cowan's "about four chunks" holds only when experiments deliberately block people from grouping items; a document you can re-read meets none of those conditions. And Dunbar's 150: the 2021 reanalysis reports 95% confidence intervals of roughly 2 to 520, concluding that no number can be derived this way at all.

- **The DITA reuse savings** ("30–40%") could not be traced to any primary study in any index the sweep could reach; the figures circulate through vendor calculators that cite each other. The sweep is explicit that its indexes do not cover trade whitepapers, so this is a strong absence, not a proof.

What is left when the folklore is removed is startlingly little. Across three separate agents that hunted independently, the entire body of experiments where a document was the thing changed and a human outcome was measured comes down to a handful: the Minimal Manual line (1987 — nineteen and thirty-two office temp workers learning a word processor; a pooled follow-up study found a large effect, d ≈ 1.12, but that pooling was a conference paper that was never peer reviewed, totalled 288 people, and was co-curated by the theory's own authors), one crypto-API study with 58 students, a couple of 1990s-2000s pattern-documentation and API experiments, and nothing since. Multiple agents converged on the same sentence, independently: **no causal study of documentation exists anywhere** — no experiment, in any field the sweep could reach, where documentation quality was the manipulated variable and an engineering outcome was measured.

One more thing belongs here because it happened inside the sweep itself. Two agents caught automated summarisers fabricating evidence: one invented a plausible "N=22, 10 companies" for a study whose real figures are 271 questionnaires, 122 transfers, 8 companies, and another invented verbatim quotations. The agents then re-verified their findings by reading primary PDFs directly. The lesson is not about those two tools. It is that the pipe that carries claims now actively generates confident falsehoods, and that a claim without a stated source and confidence is not merely weak — it is indistinguishable from an invention.

What this changes: writing the confidence into every sentence is not a stylistic nicety. On the evidence of this corpus, it is the one piece of hygiene the entire field lacks, and the reason the field's beliefs drifted so far from its measurements.

## 2. Finding beats reading

Once the folklore is cleared, a real pattern shows through the studies that remain — and it shows twice, in two very different kinds of reader.

**In professionals.** The sweep hunted specifically for evidence about experts reading long documents for work, and the failure mode it found again and again is retrieval, not comprehension.

- Thirteen healthcare practitioners working from a real clinical guideline could read it fine; they could not *find* the recommendation that applied to their patient (Kilsdonk 2016, small but a real professional population on a real long document).

- Across 61 general practitioners and 12,880 real decisions, clear recommendations were followed 67% of the time and vague ones 36% (Grol 1998, verified at source; the study only shows the two things move together — clarity, controversy and required change are tangled in the same items).

- NASA's most detailed analysis of airline emergency handbooks (Burian 2014, read from the primary PDF) found the problems were flow lines that break across pages and jump chains with no evident reason — navigation — and marked the deep usability questions "further research needed." NASA's own conclusion is that the evidence does not exist.

- The design effects taught in instructional psychology shrink exactly where working documents live. A pooled review of reviews (Noetel 2021: 1,189 studies, 78,177 participants) found design mattered most in system-paced settings like lectures, and least where the reader controls their own pace — which is what reading a document is.

- The "expertise reversal" effect — the idea that guidance which helps novices harms experts — has, per the 2025 pooled analysis of 60 studies, **no professional evidence at all**: there was no category for practising professionals because no such studies exist, and the nearest proxy (three vocational studies) gives an estimate whose confidence interval spans 2.4 standard deviations. Meanwhile the best-designed professional-reader experiment in the whole sweep points the other way: 105 practising lawyers, pre-registered, found that convoluted contract prose hurts lawyers' recall and comprehension about as much as laypeople's — expertise raises the whole curve but does not shield against bad prose — and 102 lawyers rated simplified contracts equally enforceable and *better*. The full text is free: [the PNAS lawyers study](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2302672120), and it is the strongest single argument in this corpus that writing simply for experts costs nothing.

- One caution against trusting what readers *say*: 181 physicians overwhelmingly preferred a layered guideline format (72% vs 16%), while their measured understanding did not significantly improve (p = 0.06). Preference is not comprehension.

**In models.** The 2026 agent literature reached the same conclusion by experiment, and faster.

- The central result: repository context files (AGENTS.md and kin) "do not generally improve task success rates, while increasing inference cost by over 20% on average" — the ETH Zürich group's evaluation across two settings and multiple agents ([arXiv:2602.11988](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11988), quote verified at source). An independent 288-run ablation — a test that removes the file to see what changes — bounded any effect at under 10–15 points and found agents fail on implementation skill, not missing repository knowledge. The most adopted convention in the field (60,000+ repositories, self-reported) has the clearest measured null attached to it.

- But the one positive result isolates the mechanism: tuned repository guidance lifted resolve rates from 25.5% to 33.0%, and the entire gain came from agents *reaching the correct files* (+14.5 points of coverage) while patch quality stayed flat ([arXiv:2606.20512](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.20512), verified at source). Guidance works as a map, not as a teacher.

- Prose explanation carries almost nothing for a model at the point of action. Held to the same tasks with localisation fixed, natural-language summaries answered 4 of 45 behavioural questions where the source answered 27 — and a frontier model's summaries scored exactly as badly as a tiny model's, so the loss is in the representation, not the summariser ([arXiv:2607.09691](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09691), pre-registered, verified at source). The same paper supplies a fact that undermines much of the surrounding literature: at temperature zero, about 9% of per-task outcomes flip between byte-identical runs. Any small single-run effect on these benchmarks may be noise.

- What *does* move retrieval is carrying the document's own hierarchy into each piece: prefixing every chunk with its chain of headings lifted retrieval quality 23.8% on a production Markdown knowledge base of 1,600 queries ([arXiv:2608.00824](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00824), single author, not peer reviewed). Its sharpest detail: removing the heading chains also collapsed the human annotators' agreement (kappa 0.45 → 0.04) — the headings are the context *people* need to even agree what is relevant.

- llms.txt splits the same way. As a channel to crawlers it is dead: a nine-month log study across ~900 domains recorded zero requests from any frontier AI crawler, and Google said flatly it has no effect. As a *navigation map* an agent follows on demand, one vendor benchmark (2,400 trials, open-sourced design, still vendor-run) found it cut dead-URL errors by about 90% — while answer accuracy stayed flat across every format. Same lesson: the value is in finding, not in the prose.

- And the strangest, most under-cited result in the corpus points the same direction from below: across 18 models, *shuffled* piles of text outperformed logically coherent ones as context, and a focused ~300-token prompt beat the same content inside ~113k tokens for every model tested ([Chroma's Context Rot report](https://www.trychroma.com/research/context-rot), replicated in spirit by an ICML 2025 study). Careful composition of what enters the window matters more than the coherence of what is in it. Related and sharper: compacting a long context by summarisation silently deletes rules — policy-violation rates went from 0% with full context to ~30% average after compaction, and the fix that restored 0% was marking the rules as non-compressible ([arXiv:2606.22528](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22528), single-author, deterministic benchmark). One controlled study also found that pointer-following pays exactly once: one level of routing beats raw navigation, a second level adds nothing and sometimes hurts ([arXiv:2607.17598](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.17598), the only such ablation found).

Two entirely different readers — a busy expert and a language model — and the measurements agree. The lever with evidence behind it is *where things are, what they are called, and what arrives together*: naming, placement, headings that travel with content, links that can be followed on demand, and ruthless selection of what enters the reader's working span. The measured effect of explaining the middle better, for skilled self-paced readers, is small, often absent, and in no study durable. One honest limit on this: the strongest positive result for prose anywhere in the corpus is that stories beat essays for understanding and memory (pooled over 33,000+ participants, robust) — and nobody has studied professionals re-reading a long document over months at all. The finding is about where measured leverage is, not proof that explanation is worthless.

## 3. The receiver sets the ceiling, and use sets the lifetime

The largest and best organisational study in the corpus is Szulanski (1996): 122 transfers of working practice inside 8 firms, 271 questionnaires. Its finding is the opposite of the folk story. Knowledge fails to move not mainly because people will not share, but because of three things, in order: the receiver's limited **capacity to absorb** (weight 0.54), the **ambiguity** of the knowledge itself (0.34), and an **arduous relationship** between the two sides (0.33). Motivation ranked low. One strong study, never independently replicated, with its top-three ranking robust to its own re-runs — the sweep read the primary PDF, including the caveats. [The paper is free](https://josephmahoney.web.illinois.edu/BADM%20545_Spring%202008/Paper/Szulanski%20(1996).pdf), and its robustness paragraph is the part to read.

Around it, independent lines converge:

- Absorption depends on what the receiver already knows (Cohen & Levinthal 1990 — replicated as a construct, heavily moderated in later pooled studies). A client who has outsourced away its technical staff has, by construction, destroyed its own ability to absorb what the vendor writes. That is a structural fact no document fixes.

- Where the two sides have different *interests* — client and vendor, not just different vocabularies — Carlile's boundary work says translation is not enough; both sides must give something up. A glossary addresses the shallowest layer only. This is a framework with good field support and no controlled test.

- When 411 Microsoft developers were asked freely what helped them onboard, mentorship came first and documentation second (Rastogi 2015, read at source; the ranking is opinion data). The best-instrumented onboarding factor in the corpus is a person, not a page.

- The single hardest number in the organisational lane: on Windows Vista (3,404 binaries, over 50M lines), the structure of the *organisation* predicted which components would fail at 86.2% precision and 84.0% recall — beating code churn, complexity, dependencies, coverage, and pre-release bugs ([the Microsoft study](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/tr-2008-11.pdf), one system, never replicated at this scale). Conway's law has real teeth: the boundary you draw between people becomes the boundary you pay to cross in the artifact.

- Why documents hit a ceiling at all has a clean theory with lab support: Clark and Brennan's grounding work. Conversation is cheap because it repairs itself in the moment; a document has maximum reviewability and zero ability to repair a misreading as it happens, so misunderstandings compound silently — and the least-effort solution for the reader is to go ask someone.

- The surgical checklist gives the cleanest natural experiment on "a well-designed procedural document changes outcomes." Introduced as an engaged programme in 8 hospitals: deaths halved (1.5% → 0.8%). Mandated by policy across 101 hospitals in Ontario: no significant effect. Run as a proper randomised trial in 2 hospitals with real implementation: complications fell 19.9% → 11.5%. All three verified from primary abstracts. The artifact does not carry the effect; the implementation does.

The second half of this understanding is about lifetime, and it is the most actionable finding in the corpus for anyone who writes. Across 53 practitioners in six automotive companies, artifacts that *generate* something downstream — code, tests, other artifacts — get maintained; artifacts that merely describe do not ([Wohlrab and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.12131), solid qualitative evidence, no controlled test). Their blunt guideline: produce descriptive documents as late as possible and only when actually needed. The rest of the corpus is one long confirmation:

- Google's own book reports that when the internal GooWiki was retired, around 90% of its documents had no views or updates in the preceding months. One footnote, but Google's only published documentation-decay number.

- Architecture Decision Records, measured across all of GitHub (921 adopting repositories, 6,362 files): 51% of ADRs were committed once and never touched again; only 4.6% of adopting repositories show sustained team use; and per the 2026 follow-up, 63% of ADRs are *born* "accepted" — skipping the deliberation they exist to record — with "at most, modest observable effects" on quality or productivity. Fifteen years of advocacy, one study that looked for an effect, and it found essentially none.

- Heavyweight proposal processes never close. Rust: 215 open RFCs, median age 905 days. Kubernetes: 343 proposals alive, only 14 ever explicitly killed. The one move with a visible before-and-after anywhere in this data is not fixing the process but *shrinking what it must decide*: Python delegated whole domains to small councils; Rust's lightweight compiler-proposal track has cleared 425 of 585 filings with 17 open. The heavy channel silts; the light channel flows.

What this changes: a code for writing can lower friction at the boundaries where vocabulary and finding are the problem — that is real, and section 2 says where. It cannot cross a boundary of interest, substitute for a mentor, or keep itself alive. The one mechanism this corpus offers for staying alive is to be load-bearing: to have something — a build, a test, a derivation, an agent — that consumes the writing and breaks visibly when it rots. Everything kept fresh in this corpus is kept fresh by a consumer, never by discipline.

## 4. Text won the body; structure won the edges

The sweep ran a deliberate stress test against the idea that typed structure should replace prose, and the verdict is precise enough to build on. Its one-line form, from a paper on multi-agent message passing and worth keeping whole: *"Structure buys a faithful, error-localizing channel — not an error-correcting code."*

The case against typing the content:

- Types produced by a model are wrong at rates that make them worse than prose. The cleanest study reconstructed database documentation from data values: a model used directly scored 0.223 accuracy while answering 94% of the time; the same model behind a hard verification gate scored 0.475 on the 42% it would commit to — and the authors' own line is that the deterministic layer is "a competence detector, not a competence amplifier" ([arXiv:2608.07946](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07946)). On a 369-field financial schema, every frontier model produced 0% valid output ([arXiv:2602.12247](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12247)). Form is free — schema *compliance* is near-perfect — while content is not. A wrong type answers confidently; incomplete prose is visibly incomplete. Text rots loudly; a type rots silently and keeps answering.

- Even huge, human-built vocabularies concentrate to almost nothing. Schema.org's own 2026 usage data: of 958 types, 12 reach ten million domains and about 77% sit below a thousand ([the schema.org announcement](https://blog.schema.org/2026/06/04/announcing-the-schema-org-usage-statistics-dataset/)). Wikidata's collaboratively edited class hierarchy holds roughly 2.4 million classes that are simultaneously first- and second-order — the transitive subclass structure is the thing that rots under many editors (measured on live data, [arXiv:2411.15550](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.15550v1)).

- The winners that do use structure keep the vocabulary tiny and *declared*: a memory system with 13 fixed categories beating graph memories; a query agent with a 15-operation typed language beating both free-form generation and heavy pipelines. Induced vocabularies lose; small declared ones win — the pattern held in every head-to-head the stress-test agent could find.

The case for typing the edges is just as sharp. The domains where structure is uncontested share one property: *a claim must be checked later, by someone who was not there, with a consequence for being wrong.* Permission checks that must hold even when the model is fooled. Provenance — where a claim came from. Counting and set questions ("how many," "all items where"), which prose retrieval cannot answer without reading everything. Merging what several writers wrote with no coordinator. Costs that must not grow with the size of the catalogue. None of these get easier as models get smarter, because they are not reading problems. The stress-test's asymmetry line is the durable one: **scaling dissolves the problems of reading; it does not touch the problems of writing, committing, and being accountable.**

Three 2025–26 events make the same point at ecosystem scale, all verified against primary sources:

- **MCP got less conversational and more typed in the same revision.** The July 2026 spec deleted sessions and the handshake — the model-facing conversational layer — and simultaneously hardened the typed layer underneath: mandatory discovery, required result types, required cache metadata on every read ([the changelog](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2026-07-28/changelog)). The protocol stopped trying to be the conversation and became a typed, addressable capability layer.

- **Agent Skills won on adoption and lost on evidence.** Markdown skill folders are everywhere (~53,000 on one marketplace alone), and the only controlled benchmark found target skills *lowering* pass rates by 1.3–4.2% at 72–394% more tokens, with skill sets going stale silently across 105 of 105 release transitions. Adoption without measurement, again.

- **Plain files became a moat because agents read them.** Logseq spent four years moving off files into a database and shipped into a market that had just re-valued the thing it gave up; the community verdict was "too little, too late." Obsidian's answer — typed database views *over* plain Markdown files, files remaining the substrate — is the market's chosen compromise, and it is exactly "structure at the edges, text as the body."

What this changes for anyone designing a knowledge medium: the evidence supports typing identity, address, ownership, version, confidence, and links — the things a later reader who was not there must check — and leaving the explanation itself as prose. And it adds one hard gate: if a type would have to be *inferred* rather than declared or observed, do not type it.

## 5. The people and works, now that the centre is held

The four understandings above are mostly negative space — what was never measured, what does not work. The corpus also maps who is alive out there and what they learned at full price. These encounters mean more now, because each one lands on one of the four.

**Shipman and Marshall** wrote the oldest objection, and nothing in 2025–26 has refuted it. *Formality Considered Harmful* (1999) is an experience essay, not a measurement — the sweep read the full ten pages — and its four mechanisms are still the live ones: structuring costs extra decisions at capture time; experts cannot reliably state what they know; imposed structure arrives before understanding does; and different people need different structure from the same material. Its sharpest observation: design students *could not produce* formal argument structure even though videotapes showed their natural discussion already followed it — "post hoc analysis is very different from generation." The whole design-rationale tradition downstream confirms the cost side: the one observational study of a professionally scribed rationale document found it answered only 41% of the why-questions real designers brought to it, and the entire quantitative case that recording "why" pays is one 17-subject experiment that succeeded on one of its two systems. The essay is archived free: [Formality Considered Harmful](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1008716330212) — the door to open if only one gets opened, because it is the cost model your whole undertaking must beat.

**Ink & Switch** is the lab nearest to this project's ambitions, ten years old in 2026, and unusually honest about its failures. Three of its results matter here.

- **Patchwork**, its current centre of gravity, is a running system where tools, their data types, and composition rules are all data over shared documents — the closest shipped neighbour to "the interface is data over the same contracts." Its plugin contract encodes hard-won constraints, written for agents to read: [patchwork-skills](https://github.com/inkandswitch/patchwork-skills).

- **Cambria**, their schema-evolution project, is marked Completed (2020) with dead code and no successor — and the sweep's deep read found its real lessons are not the headline. The team abandoned translating data at write time ("too much translation work was happening eagerly on write") in favour of tagging every write with the schema it was written under and translating at read — which is what Avro has done in production for eighteen years. And the mathematics went against them: the composition law you would need to chain migrations safely (PutPut) "fail[s] to satisfy… for reasons that seem pragmatically unavoidable," in the words of the foundational lenses paper. The sweep's blunt recommendation, after reading both: do not build lenses; tag writes with their contract version instead. [The Cambria essay](https://www.inkandswitch.com/cambria/) is candid enough to be worth reading as a post-mortem the lab never separately wrote.

- Their **malleable software** essay (2025) makes the app-enclosure argument — apps as walls that stop data composing — better than anyone else currently writing: [the essay](https://www.inkandswitch.com/essay/malleable-software/).

**The opposition worth taking seriously is Dynamicland.** Bret Victor's group has run the counter-experiment for a decade and states the inverse principle outright: *"to maximize agency, minimize what the computer knows."* Their objection to rich machine-readable substrates is that every layer of contract added to make composition automatic makes human comprehension harder — the smarter the system, the less the user needs to understand, which they call outsourcing understanding. The sweep found the group publicly dormant (newest material 2024), but the argument does not age: [the Dynamicland FAQ](https://dynamicland.org/2024/FAQ/). Geoffrey Litt — Patchwork's own lead, now at Notion — titled a 2026 post "Understanding is the new bottleneck," which is the same objection from inside the structure camp.

**Nobody argues the whole position.** The sweep's dedicated who-else-says-this pass found every neighbour holding two or three of the relevant claims and ignoring or rejecting the rest. The closest single statement of "facts carry their context and lineage as first-class slots" is Kendall Clark's Pentad Labs — an essay series plus a pre-release product, not a shipped system: [PLRN/020](https://pentad.ai/PLRN/020/). Palantir is the only typed-object layer at national scale, and it is itself a monolith. Letta holds "memory is core, not a bolt-on" and locates it exactly where this project says it must not live — in the harness. The full historical arc — Unix enclosed, then the web, then AI — the sweep could not find anyone making. That absence is either an opening or a warning; the raw cannot say which.

**The graveyard is faculty, not debris.** The pattern across it is one lesson repeated:

- **TerminusDB** is the closest prior art in existence to a versioned, typed, branchable knowledge store — commits, branches, schema constraints, all shipped since 2019. In 2025 it was handed off to a third-party maintainer after a year-long release gap; its 2026 commit history is essentially one person; and its one first-class schema-migration idea ("weakening" — changes that cannot invalidate existing data merge automatically, everything else needs an explicit replayable script) leaves its hardest operation, migrating the inheritance hierarchy, explicitly unimplemented. This is what happened to this exact idea, and its half-built migration lattice is the most instructive artifact it left: [the migration guide](https://terminusdb.org/docs/schema-migration-reference-guide/).

- **Darklang's** founder wrote the most candid commercial post-mortem in the corpus (June 2025, quoted verbatim by the sweep): "our online structured editor didn't make sense when the LLM is generating the code." The structured editor — half their codebase — is the thing the model era invalidated. **Unison** (code as a database, not text) and **feenk/Glamorous Toolkit** ("Objects, not text") both moved to consulting to fund the product within a year of each other. Three independent structure-first ventures, one funding answer.

- **Jonathan Edwards**, after twenty years of Subtext, wrote in his 2025 retrospective: "It is fair to say that Subtext was a series of overambitious failed experiments… What Subtext needed was a Theory of Change" — the research-side echo of the same lesson: structure-first mediums have repeatedly failed not on vision but on a missing account of how people get from here to there.

- And one correction to a story often told in this space: **Luhmann's Zettelkasten**, per the archive's own description (read in German at source), is ~90,000 slips over about 45 years with divisions running to four sub-levels and one division holding 120 subdivisions — explicitly *not* a strictly hierarchical system, and not a flat web either. The real box is a counter-example to most of the tidy morals drawn from it.

## 6. The builders' lanes

What follows is working detail — the folds nearest the edge. Each lane is what shipped systems paid to learn, compressed, with the door left open.

**Schema change.** Every production system that survived it bought safety by *forbidding*, not translating: Protobuf forbids reusing field numbers and killed `required`; Datomic says "you can never alter `:db/valueType`"; ATProto's rule is "Types can not change. Fields can not be renamed" — breaking changes get a new name. The one honest compatibility setting for a federation nobody can force to upgrade is full compatibility in both directions, which ATProto states in prose and Kafka calls FULL_TRANSITIVE — and Kafka's default (BACKWARD, non-transitive) quietly allows chains of pairwise-compatible changes that are globally incompatible. The sweep's single strongest recommendation, ahead of everything else in its lane: **stamp every write with the version of the contract it validated against**, because write-time-only validation is safe only when the writer's schema travels with the datum. Rename gets one blessed mechanism: aliases. And nobody merges structure — Dolt takes whole sides, Iceberg only fast-forwards, Confluent's escape hatch is "make a new topic." The Avro specification's schema-resolution section is the eighteen-year-proven core of all of this: [the Avro spec](https://avro.apache.org/docs/1.12.0/specification/).

**Permissions.** Transitive permission is a write-time bomb disguised as a read-time convenience. Google's Zanzibar paper says recursive pointer-chasing cannot hold latency on deep or wide groups, and its fix — materialising the closure — turns one tuple write into "potentially tens of thousands" of index events ([the paper](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/atc19-pang.pdf)). The clone ecosystem spent 2026 abandoning depth limits as the wrong cost metric — fan-out, not depth, is the cost ([OpenFGA's announcement](https://openfga.dev/blog/weighted-graph-upcoming-changes)). And the AI layer converted latent over-permission into actual leakage, which Microsoft now documents in its own product pages — its stopgap "isn't a security boundary," in its own words ([the Microsoft doc](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/sharepoint/restricted-sharepoint-search)). The AI did not create the leak; it enumerated it. Non-transitive hierarchy has a decade-tested precedent: SKOS made `broader` deliberately non-transitive in 2009 and survived.

**Interface as data.** Every product with typed user data — Notion, Airtable, Tana, Anytype — has an extensible instance schema and a *closed enum* of view kinds, verified down to the protobuf; none lets a renderer declare what it accepts and bid to render it. The one shipped system that answers "which components can draw this content?" as a runtime query is WordPress Gutenberg's block registry, at WordPress scale. Five companies (Uber, Plaid, DoorDash, Lyft, Shopify) independently hit the same server-driven-UI wall — shipping a *new* component type to an old client — and independently invented the same partial fix, capability negotiation; none solved it. And Retool's 2026 reversal is the sharpest commercial datum: it abandoned its own visual abstraction because it "prevented LLMs from working fluently" — a verdict against closed, non-inspectable derivation, not against deriving UI from typed contracts, which is precisely the layer every survivor kept as its moat.

**Agent memory and the harness.** The platform absorbed the memory layer while the sweep ran: versioned, session-attributed, permission-scoped memory stores are now a lab product, so file-plus-version is the free floor anyone building here must beat. What no lab will build is memory *across* vendors — the gap three independent vendors rushed within six weeks of each other. Unsolved and dangerous: poisoning a memory corpus at 1.2% dropped accuracy from 0.850 to 0.300, a screening pipeline caught 0 of 360 poisoned memories, and the shipped defence was statistically indistinguishable from none ([arXiv:2608.21230](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.21230)). Nobody has safe branching of a running agent's context — a 2026 paper formalises why forking without deriving what the fork must preserve is unsafe ([arXiv:2608.22928](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22928)) — and exact reproducibility of a completion from a recorded context exists nowhere in shipped products; compaction and background consolidation are trading it away deliberately.

## 7. What is missing

The corpus is clearest about the experiments nobody has run. These are the open edges, stated so they are not mistaken for settled ground:

- **The crux experiment does not exist.** No study compares LLM-assisted, human-*declared* structure against a well-written prose document, on the same corpus, with the same frontier reader. Every structure win in the stress-test lane beat extracted or unstructured baselines — never good prose. The whole text-versus-structure argument turns on a comparison nobody has made.

- **No causal study of documentation exists** — no experiment anywhere with documentation as the manipulated variable and a real outcome measured. Three agents looked independently and all said so.

- **Nobody has studied professionals re-reading a long document over months.** The entire comprehension literature is single 15–30 minute sessions on short passages, mostly with students. The reader this project cares about — returning to a living document across a project's life — is scientifically unobserved.

- **Expertise reversal has never been tested in the field.** "Strip the guidance, your readers are experts" is, on current evidence, a rule from student labs with the professional half missing — and the professional data that exists (the lawyers) points against it.

The four centres, held together, come to one sentence: the field's confident numbers are borrowed, the measured leverage of writing is in finding and naming rather than explaining, no document outlives its consumer or outruns its receiver, and the durable role of structure is at the edges — which is where the checking, the permission, and the memory of who-said-what live. Everything else in this corpus is detail hanging from that.
